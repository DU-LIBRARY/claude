#### The third condition: being male

The third condition for the head of the country is that he be male, not a woman. And the evidence for that is the saying of the Prophet ﷺ: "A people who make a woman their ruler will never prosper."¹ He ﷺ said it when it reached him that the people of Persia had made the daughter of Chosroes queen over them.

And it is narrated in another hadith which al-Tirmidhī included from Abū Hurayra; he said: the Messenger of God ﷺ said: "If your commanders are the best of you, and your rich are the generous of you, and your affairs are [decided by] consultation among you, then the surface of the earth is better for you than its belly. And if your commanders are the worst of you, and your rich are the stingy of you, and your affairs are [handed] to your women, then the belly of the earth is better for you than its surface."²

In addition to that, the Sharia regards authority as an imamate, as was detailed earlier. And the imamate is divided into two kinds: the greater, which is this, and the lesser, which is the imamate in the prayers. And the word [of the scholars] has agreed that the woman is not fit for the lesser imamate (i.e. to become an imam for men in the prayer); so how could it be said that she is fit for the greater imamate, with what it has of gravity and standing?!

#### The fifth condition: knowledge

The scholars of politics have agreed on stipulating knowledge for the imam, and no one has had an effect of disagreement in stipulating that; rather the disagreement is in the amount with which sufficiency is achieved in the case of the imam. Some — among them al-Māwardī and al-Juwaynī — held that it is stipulated that the imam be a mujtahid who has reached the level of the mujtahids; while the later ones among them denied stipulating his reaching the rank of ijtihad, and rather contented themselves in that with the amount that enables the sultan to administer his government according to the rulings of the Sharia only.

### 3 – Appointing the imam and investing him

Then Islam commands its followers to undertake — by consultation among them — handing over the office of the imamate over them to one of them in whom these qualities are combined and these conditions are present. And this principle comprises two matters: the obligation of appointing the imam over the Muslims, and that it be from consultation among them.

As for the detail of the first, let it be known that appointing the imam is obligatory on the Muslims by the consensus of those of the Muslims on whom the sun has risen and set, and the agreement of the schools of all the scholars. And the meaning of that is that it is imposed on the Muslims as a communal obligation (*ʿalā al-kifāya*) that they make one of them commander and appoint over them one of them in whom those conditions are combined and those qualities are present.

But let it be noted that this ruling is directed to the Muslims only in what if they have no imam and no commander, and they have come to live without a ruler in anarchy, with no one to gather them on the truth, and no restrainer restrains them, and no deterrent deters them from following the footsteps of Satan. Then it is obligatory on them

[p. 212] to appoint one of them as an imam who performs his functions through establishing the ḥudūd, defending the sanctity of Islam and so on. As for if they have an imam — even if he took on the imamate by seizure and taking control, and against their will — and even if he is not comprehensive of the qualities required for the imam, as is the case in most Islamic states today: what then should the Muslims do in this form? It is a question related to the subject of deposing the imam, whose details we leave to its place in Chapter Six, God willing.

As for the second part of this principle, it is that the appointment of the imam must be by way of consultation among the people who loosen and bind (*ahl al-ḥall wa-l-ʿaqd*) among the Muslims. And it is not permissible for a person to seize the affair and so take it over without consultation, or upon the consultation of some of those not counted among the people who loosen and bind. And the evidence for that is the saying of God, glorified and exalted: **"And whose affair is [determined by] consultation among themselves"** [al-Shūrā, 42:38].

And the greatest evidence for that is that the Prophet ﷺ left this world and did not appoint anyone as successor in it; rather he entrusted his affair to the generality of the Muslims to choose from among them for this matter whoever of them is the fittest and most just. And it suffices as evidence that the reference in that is to the generality of the Muslims, not others; otherwise he would have spared them the trouble of appointment by appointing one of them as successor.

And more explicit than that is what al-Bukhārī narrated from ʿĀʾisha, may God be pleased with her, that the Messenger of God ﷺ said to her while he was in the illness in which he died: "I was about — or I wanted — to send to Abū Bakr and his son and make a covenant [of succession], lest speakers say — or wishers wish —; then I said: God refuses [otherwise] and the believers repel [otherwise]."¹

So this hadith indicated with all clarity and explicitness that the Prophet ﷺ left the matter of appointing his successor to the Muslims; and since he was confident that they would not be pleased except with Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, may God be pleased with him, he therefore did not see any need for appointing him personally.

And there is another hadith which al-Bukhārī included too with its details, from which the standing of consultation in the appointment of the caliph and the designation of the imam becomes clear; and its summary: that ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf heard, in the last days of the pilgrimage which ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb, may God be pleased with him, performed, [a man] saying: "If ʿUmar had died I would have pledged allegiance to

So these proofs all suffice to stand as an argument for what we claimed: that the appointment of the imam and the investing of the caliph must be from consultation of the Muslims. So after this is established, this question poses itself in the mind of the researcher: how does this consultation among the Muslims take place? Is it by voting on the basis of the ballot of every adult, or by election from among the members of a particular body or two particular circles, or in another way besides that? We say: it is among the secondary matters for which Islam did not specify a special formula to act by; rather it entrusted its matter to the majority of the Muslims to choose for it a form that agrees with the principles of the Sharia on one side and harmonizes with their external reality on another. ### 4 – Appointment by designation of a successor (istikhlāf) and covenant

And its meaning: that the rightful imam who combines all the conditions of the imamate appoint someone as successor in his lifetime to be the imam of the Muslims after his death. And the term "covenant" (*al-ʿahd*) in royal circles is synonymous with the word. And the Sharia also considers it, with its conditions, as a method for the conclusion of the imamate. And they argued for that by Abū Bakr's appointing ʿUmar, may God be pleased with them both, as successor, and by what ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, said shortly before his death: "If my term reaches me while Abū ʿUbayda is alive, I will appoint him successor." So it indicated that designation of a successor is permissible in the Sharia. But three points must be noted here with precision and care:

**The first:** it is not permissible for the imam to appoint one of his children or his guardians as successor merely for his kinship and blood relations with him, regarding the caliphate as an inheritance as property and goods are inherited. And the evidence for that is that Abraham, upon him be peace, when he asked for the office of the imamate for his descendants, God, glorified and exalted, answered him, saying: **"My covenant does not include the wrongdoers"** [al-Baqara, 2:124].

And that Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him, when he appointed ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, as successor, said his statement which history has recorded from him, so it became a bright lamp lighting the way for whoever seeks to follow it: "For by God I did not fall short in effort of opinion, and I did not appoint a relative."

And it is a matter on which the Umma has agreed. ʿAllāma Ibn Ḥazm (may God have mercy on him) said: "And there is no disagreement among anyone of the people of Islam that inheritance is not permissible in it."

**The second:** the imam who wants to designate a successor or covenant to someone must in the Sharia choose the fittest of those he is able [to find] among those in whom the qualities of the imamate are combined and the conditions of the caliphate are present; and that his election of him be from consultation of the people who loosen and bind and of knowledge, as Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, may God be pleased with him, did in his appointing al-Fārūq, may God be pleased with him, as successor.¹
