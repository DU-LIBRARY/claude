## Chapter Three: Theoretical Studies in Democracy

### Introduction

[p. 83] Since democracy is one of the political systems, it should have been included in the second chapter following the other political systems. However, we have preferred to devote a special chapter to it: on the one hand because of its extreme importance and the lofty standing it has attained among the people of this age, in addition to the first place it has occupied among all its sister [systems], so that it has become the ideal and most developed political system according to the prevailing and claimed belief in political circles in the present age; and on the other hand, one also knows the extent of their esteem for it from the fact that they do not count a person as anything nor reckon with him in the field of politics unless he embraces it, believes in it and accepts its principles; while uttering anything against it, or mouthing objections to it and refuting its principles, leads its proponent, in the eyes of modern politicians, only to heresy and political misguidance! This is what makes us feel the pressing need to study it and understand it in some detail. Our discussion will cover the following points:

(1) What is democracy?
(2) A review of its principles and basic ideals.
(3) A summary of the history of democracy and its development in the world.
(4) The institutions and organizations arising from it.
(5) A look at the forms of democratic government prevailing in the world.

### Section One: What is democracy?

Democracy is in fact a rendering of the English word "Democracy", which is taken from Greek and compounded of two words: (Demo), meaning the common people, and "Cracy", meaning rule; when the word was transferred into Arabic they said: *al-dīmuqrāṭiyya*. At any rate, the basic conception [p. 84] of democracy is: that the right to rule over the people belongs to it alone. From this the meaning of democracy becomes clear to us: that it is a form of government in which the people, or its opinion, is taken as the basis for setting state policies and taking its decisions.

Political scholars have not yet reached agreement on defining it in a way that is comprehensive and consistent; rather their statements on it conflict and their opinions contradict one another, such that you will not find some of them resembling, approaching or coming close to others. But the common measure we can extract from their collective [views] is what we mentioned just now: that it is a system of government built on giving the people the opportunity to interfere in government policies and decisions to some extent.

Democracy in its general concept mentioned above is not the child of the present age or the product of the thought of modern man. Rather, its first forms were carved out with all simplicity and naivety in Greece, coinciding with the operation of the city-state system there, where each city was self-governing and independent. Those cities were not very large; rather, the largest of them was "Athens" (Athens), which the world still knows by its name; yet the number of its inhabitants at the time did not exceed ten thousand souls, let alone the small cities such as Sparta, for example. So the gathering of this number of souls in one place and the taking of decisions in the light of their opinions and views was not at all impossible or difficult. Hence the prevalent form of democracy among them was direct democracy, such that if the king wanted to lay down important policies or take important decisions, he ordered all the citizens to gather in the square, and put the draft decision to them, asking them to give their opinion on it. Naturally people would divide into supporters and opponents, and that might lead to discussion among them, and in the end he obtained approval for it by a show of hands. Thus they had a simple democracy without being governed by a constitution that defined the king's powers and distinguished the matters in which the people's approval was necessary from what he could conclude himself without needing to present it to the people. Rather, all that was entrusted to the king and his personal opinion: whatever he saw a need to present to a meeting of the people and obtain [p. 85] their approval on, he did; and whatever he did not wish to, he did not, and took a decision on it alone. Over time this simple form of direct popular consultation acquired a kind of coordination and arrangement, alongside the laying down of many laws for it; but those laws had no great importance among them nor significant benefit. Rather, direct democracy, which was dominated by the character of simplicity, remained in most cases in that form of its.

But it is also self-evidently clear that such a democracy can be applied only if the state is small with limited population density, so that the king can gather his subjects in one square and then consult them on an issue. If, however, the extent of the state widens and its horizons stretch and its inhabitants multiply, applying it becomes difficult and almost impossible — as actually happened when the Roman Empire arose with its dense population. This led to narrowing the conception of democracy, such that the king came to content himself with establishing a council that performed the task of advising him on state affairs, and he would consult them on them sometimes.

The consequence of that was that the idea of democracy centred on the participation of the people in state policies and plans gradually contracted from actual implementation in external reality, and absolute monarchy and other forms of dictatorship took its place. From this one can rightly say that democracy's star set with the absence of the city-state system in Greece, until the beginning of the eighteenth century CE, when currents set off in the world and tendencies towards democracy prevailed and continued in it, through which its idea was revived and its system was revitalized with a kind of regulation and coordination, which ultimately resulted in the birth of the democracy the world knows today as liberal democracy (Liberal Democracy). This kind of democracy is what is prevalent in the world today. To understand it with insight and mastery and to arrive at its essence and reality, we need to deal briefly with four important topics, which I list before you in logical order, which will make our aim easier for us and achieve our goal in the shortest time and with the least hardship. The four headings are:

[p. 86] (1) The intellectual foundations of democracy (or the philosophy motivating the creation of democracy).
(2) A look at the history of its development in the world up to the eighteenth century CE.
(3) An introduction to the institutions arising from it.
(4) An introduction to the various systems of democratic government that have been implemented in various states of the world.

So here is the discussion of these topics according to the order mentioned above.

### 1 – The intellectual foundations of democracy

We stated earlier that Europe, in its second renaissance, was pervaded by the spirit of rationalism and individual independence, following its liberation from the strict restrictions that the Church had imposed on it — and which judged from the outset, then executed or burned alive at times, everyone who opposed the Church or contradicted its theories. However, after the catastrophe of the fall of al-Andalus and the transfer of the sciences of the Muslims to them, tendencies towards thinking and reflection arose in them; but since the Church still enjoyed complete political control over them, the advocates of intellectual liberation were soon struck by kinds of persecution and harm. Yet the ember that the liberals had kindled for the purpose of freeing themselves from the yoke of intellectual servitude continued to blaze and increase, and the plant its first planter had sown continued to grow and rise, despite the raging storms it faced blowing from the direction of the Church. But its steadfastness and firmness in the face of the waves of persecution earned it acceptance and popular currency on the one hand, just as the effects of aversion and disapproval against the Church [grew] on the other. In the wake of that, the hegemony and control of the Church gradually began to wane and decline, which made possible the existence of a considerable number of men of thought in various fields of life, who in turn called for rebellion against the theories of the Church that were unsuited to human nature and not in harmony with the reality of humanity.

Leaving aside the other fields, we shall confine ourselves here to letting our gaze roam over what [p. 87] Europe witnessed of intellectual development in the field of politics, and specifically liberal democracy. We say: the thinkers who drew the picture of liberal democracy and strengthened it, and whom the world today regards as its founders, are three men, whose names are as follows: (1) Voltaire (2) Montesquieu (Montesquiue) (3) Rousseau.

These three — all from France — spread ideas and published theories that brought about a revolution in the democratic world. As for Voltaire, he was born at the end of the seventeenth century CE and died in the eighteenth. He enriched the human library with his writings in many fields such as philosophy, the natural sciences, the arts and so on, to the extent that their collection has been published in ninety volumes. Among his most important distinguishing features is that he launched a blind assault on all religions, accusing them of distortion and calling for the embrace of a single school he called what he claimed to be the natural religion (Natural Religion) of man. It is said: he is the one who sowed the seeds of doubt and suspicion about the existence of God, glorified and exalted. Leaving aside the soundness of this attribution, it is established from him that its meaning is that man should embrace only the natural school, i.e. that it should bring him to acknowledge the existence of God and believe in it; as for anything beyond that — the laws, rulings and morals that religions impose on him — let him throw them against the wall without paying them any attention or regard, let alone following and complying with them.

Second, among his most important theories that found acceptance and currency with an enormous effect in the world is that he regarded religion as a personal matter for the individual, and denied that anyone has the authority to compel a human being or force him to any religion. Rather, he claimed that every individual human being is free to embrace whatever religion he wishes and to follow whatever school he is pleased with; he alone has the right to adopt a heavenly school or submit to paganism and so prostrate to idols and trees; likewise he alone has the choice of following the Jewish or Christian religion, and neither the Church nor the state (state) has any authority to force him to Christianity and impose it on him against his will.

This theory later developed and took shape in what prevailed in the West: a wave of [p. 88] indifference to religion and disregard of religious considerations, which is expressed by saying that religion is nothing but a personal matter by which a person aims at the comfort of his conscience and the tranquillity of his heart, and that judging a religion as true or false turns only on the person himself. So he has the right to embrace whatever gives him tranquillity and spreads comfort in his soul, among religions and schools of whatever kind. Whoever finds his comfort in prayer, that is the truth as far as he is concerned; and whoever finds it in paganism, it is the truth for him; and whoever is given something of comfort by meditation, he should undertake it regardless of its being true or false. It is not for anyone else — individuals, a state or a church — to interfere in his affair in this; rather they must treat him tactfully and respect his religion. Arguing over religions and discussion to convince one another of some of those religions is nothing but futility of which no benefit is gained. (The encouragement to respect the other's school and religion among these people is not out of respect for religion in itself and reverence for it; it is only because it is a means that someone has chosen for the tranquillity of his soul and the spreading of comfort and calm in his conscience. So its status among them resembles that of the house a person builds to provide comfort for himself and his family; hence others must respect it and not break into its boundaries without his permission and consent. Likewise the religion a person follows is nothing but his personal matter, and others have no right to deny his religion or undertake to negate and refute it.)

When this principle was established among them and they regarded religion as a personal matter of the citizen, it was natural that they should cut its link with the state and government, and declare that the state is not subject to any religion. Proceeding from this — and from the womb of this alleged idea — the theory of secularism was born. Secularism (*al-ʿalmāniyya*) linguistically means: the worldly — that is, everything adopted for the sake of worldly interest and benefit — or that which includes worldly material interest and brings it to a person. So it is a system laid down for securing worldly interests and benefits, and then it concentrates all its efforts and devotes all its activities to attaining and acquiring them, nothing else. Hence its consequence and logical result could only be heresy (*ilḥād*) and the categorical rejection of religion. From this, linking the state to secularism means nothing other than cutting its link with religion entirely, and administering its system according to what achieves its worldly interests and provides its material benefits. This philosophy was built on the basis of the theories presented by Voltaire as a reaction and an attempt to free [themselves] from what the Church had perpetrated under the curtain of theocracy by way of harm and persecution, [p. 89] until it became rooted in the minds of thinkers that religion is what forms an obstacle on the path of progress and development, and that it is not possible to advance in the horizons of material progress unless the yoke of religion is thrown off necks entirely and rejected categorically.

The second person who contributed abundantly to paving the way for present-day democracy was Montesquieu (Montesquiue). His book *The Spirit of the Laws* has become widely renowned in political circles; it is printed in about four hundred pages, and producing it so exhausted and wore him out that he used to say: "I want to rest for the rest of my life because of the severe fatigue and exhaustion that afflicted me while writing this book." This book of his in fact revolves around law and philosophy, but in it he presented a theory of democracy built on the basis of the theory of separation of powers (Separation of power). Its summary is that the sole element behind the abuses and evils that despotic governments brought upon humanity is: that all the powers of the state were concentrated in one person or one organization, which led to aggression and injustice and the turning of state affairs to anarchy and turmoil. Hence the administration of government cannot be good unless we separate the powers and delegate them to different bodies. From this he put forward the idea of dividing the powers of the state into three parts: (1) the power to legislate and make laws; (2) the power to execute the laws and manage state affairs in accordance with them; (3) the power of the judiciary (settling disputes and resolving conflicts, interpreting the law and showing what falls within its framework and what does not). These powers should not be combined in one hand; rather each of them should be separated from the others, while granting each its full power, so that none of them interferes in the affairs and powers of the other.

The body that enjoys the power of making laws is called the legislature (Legislature), and democracy grants this power to the parliament and the legislative assembly (Assembly).

As for the authority that carries out the execution of laws in the affairs of government, it is called the executive (Executive), which is headed by the head of state in the presidential system and the prime minister [p. 90] in the parliamentary system.

As for the power of interpreting laws and settling disputes, it is called "the judicial power" (Judiciary), which is represented by the courts in the state.

Montesquieu added: these three powers in past despotic governments were concentrated in one hand — either a person or a special body — which was what laid down the laws first, executed them second, and resolved the disputes occurring concerning them third. The result was that if the executive of the state was tyrannical and aggressive, the appeal for help could only be to it itself! This is what the Urdu poet meant when he said:

> *Wohī qātil wohī shāhid wohī munṣif ṭhahre*
> *Aqribā mere karen khūn kā daʿwā kis par?*

He killed me, and he was the witness to my killing, and then he became the judge who assumed jurisdiction in my case — so to whom, then, should my relatives bring the suit of my assassination?!¹

This does not mean that we deny the existence of courts in the monarchical system; rather we grant that there were courts, but they were entirely subject to the king: first because the final judgement lay with the king, and second because the authority for appointing and removing judges lay with the king himself. So the judges would hasten to the king's pleasure and wish in judging, out of fear of his reproach and to secure their continuation in their positions. Thus all powers were combined in one hand, either in reality or through the mediation of subordinate intermediary powers or bodies. So there was nothing equivalent to appealing for help against the ruler or the law he issued except appointing him himself as arbiter in it. Thus the monarchical system increased in nothing but despotism, absolutely.

> ¹ Or as al-Mutanabbī said: "In you lies the dispute, and you are the opponent and the judge." (Translator)

As for the theory of separation of powers, it focuses on separating these three powers among the three bodies while granting each full power and complete independence in exercising its duties. The legislature lays down the laws, the executive implements them, and the judiciary should be [p. 91] entirely free and independent, so that it becomes possible for citizens to plead against the two powers — legislative and executive — if they deviate from the right path. Hence the powers in the state must be divided into these three powers; and this is what is called the theory of separation of powers (Separation of power).

This idea of his was able to impose itself on all democratic systems, just as it became a principal chapter adopted by all states in laying down constitutions for them, such that they have come to give attention in them to explaining the distinction between systems and separating these three powers and defining the powers of each of them, then taking them fully into account — to the point that if the legislature lays down a law and implements it, it retains no authority to explain and interpret it; rather the authority in it is transferred to the judicial power. How astonishing! The person who composed its wording and whose fingers wrote the text of the law, which the parliament agreed and approved, is deprived of the authority to clarify its meaning; rather this authority passes to the judicial system, to see the text of the law and the words used in it, then clarify its meaning and define its import! Yes, parliament can abolish that law, or replace it with another, or amend and change it; but it has no right to explain and interpret it as long as it is in force in the form of a law!

In this regard it is worth paying attention to the difference and distinction between two similar, close terms: separation of powers and distribution of powers. We have already discussed separation of powers; as for the distribution of powers (Division of power), it means distributing them between the central government and the local regional governments. Montesquieu's theory was about the separation of powers and their distinction, not about dividing and distributing them.

**3 –** The third personality who played a prominent role in drawing the picture of democracy was Rousseau (Roussesau), whom we have mentioned before. He is the one who renewed the social contract theory by writing a book on it with the same title (*The Social Contract*).

It is well known that the social contract theory is ancient, dating back to Greek philosophy, and I explained in the earlier discussion of it that it is very ancient; but its explanation and interpretation later resulted in two contradictory results that clash explicitly with each other, and hence in two systems [p. 92] conflicting clearly with each other. The first of them: that the social contract theory leads to absolute despotic government; and the second of them — and Rousseau is its father — is: that the social contract theory requires every government that its laws express the general will of the people, and that the people be free in it, and that the government do nothing but play the role of deputyship and representation of the people; for the people has only relinquished to the government its right to rule in order to protect its freedom and its other interests. From this, belief in the social contract theory requires that the people be given the opportunity to establish governments that act on their behalf and represent them in laying down laws, and then look after their interests and achieve their benefits. Thus Rousseau's philosophy focused on two things: the freedom of the individual, and that the government be in the service of the general will and bound by its decisions (in the sense that the people has the right to install the government and depose it).

The summary of the discussion: these three theories (separation of state from religion, separation of powers, and government expressing the general will in the light of the freedom of the individual) are what contributed in turn as basic building blocks on which the edifice of liberal democracy was built.

### The historical factors that led to the rise of democracy

After the brief survey of the theories at work behind the emergence of democracy, we would like to follow it with an explanation of the historical events that consolidated its core and strengthened its pillars. In this regard, a quick review of two historical events is fitting, which actually played their tangible role in consolidating democracy and rooting it in the world, just as they brought about great changes in Western society and left far-reaching effects on it: namely, the independence of America and the French Revolution. They are contemporaneous, with only 12 years between them; the independence of America preceded the French Revolution. From this we present a brief introduction to it, then follow it with the brief history of the French Revolution.

### American independence

Columbus is the one who discovered the continent of America. Its story is that Europe was still searching [p. 93] for the routes by which it could reach India in order to expand the scope of its trade, but it was still failing to find them, since the Suez Canal had not yet been built. As for the sea routes, they involved unbearable difficulties and countless hardships; so it used to send expeditions from time to time to search for it, but all of them ended in disappointment and failure, except two expeditions that attained a share of success. The first of them was the expedition led by Vasco da Gama, which managed to reach India by way of the South Cape (South cape), the farthest point of Africa in the south. The second was the expedition led by Columbus, who also set out to find the road to India, but he sailed in the direction exactly opposite to Vasco's. (Modern studies have proved that the guides of both of them were Arab sailors: Columbus's guide was a sailor named Ibn Mājid, and a book of his has also been printed.) At any rate, Vasco da Gama reached India, while Columbus continued travelling westwards until he reached the islands known today as the West Indies (West Indies), believing them to be part of India — hence these islands were attributed to India, though in reality they were not; they were the islands of the Caribbean Sea (Caribbean islands). When he continued his journey and advanced well, he found another vast continent, which was the continent of America. Thus America was discovered, which the West and the English regarded as a wide field, a spacious domain and a fertile land for trade and settlement. So they sent colonial missions to it, as others also sent them — the Dutch, the French and the Portuguese as well; but the share of the English in America was more abundant and their portion of it greater. Hence England's control was established over most of it, and it exerted effort to establish its colonies there. If you let your gaze roam today over the map of America you will find in it 52 states [sic], over most of which, and the largest of them, Britain had established its control and domination, so that its affairs were run and concluded from London for a long period. Since it had resolved, first, to rule its inhabitants, and second, to levy taxes from them, when it once wanted to impose taxes on them they did not leave it to do so, but rose up to resist it, by virtue of some democratic ideas having reached them and found acceptance among them. The result of that was that they refused to pay taxes to the government of England — which was the nucleus of their rebellion against its system. When it developed a little, they resolved to throw off [p. 94] the yoke of subordination to it and to establish states with absolute authority enjoying independent self-government. So wars broke out between them, resulting in the victory of the Americans under the leadership of George Washington, who declared the independence of America, and also declared the establishment of a federal democratic government comprising all the states of America. That declaration of his is called "the Declaration of Independence" (Declaration of Independence), in which they gave the inalienable democratic rights of equality, liberty and so on to every human being, and opened the declaration with their words: "All people have been created from the wombs of their mothers free; no one has the right to enslave another human being." (In fact, this sentence approximates what ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, said to ʿAmr ibn al-ʿĀṣ, may God be pleased with him: "Since when have you enslaved people, when their mothers bore them free?"¹)

According to what is believed, it is the first liberal democracy established on the face of the earth. However, since Europe was at a vast distance from America, nothing worth mentioning or reckoning with of its democratic effects reached it; rather, the thing that changed the course of European popular thought and its currents was the French Revolution, which occurred twelve years after the event of American independence. Here, then, is a brief, summarized account of it below, leaving aside its detailed history, for which this narrow discussion has no room.

### A look at the French Revolution

This account goes back to the late eighteenth century CE, when France and other European states were governed by a monarchical system, and the king of France at that time was "Louis XVI". As was customary with the kings of that time, they had formed councils for themselves for consultation that were hostage to their will and bound by their wish entirely: whenever they wished they convened it, and whenever they did not wish they did not; and on whatever issue they wished to consult on, they did, otherwise not; and they were never bound or committed to consult them. Louis XVI had a consultative council of this kind, which he named the council of the estates of the nation (Senate genral) [sic: Estates General], and he had formed it [p. 95] from the representatives of the three estates: (1) the estate of men of religion from the members of the Church; (2) the estate of the feudal nobles; (3) the estate of the general people. The number of members of the three estates was equal. So if the third estate wanted to issue a decision in the interest of the people from the council, the first two groups would object to it, which caused it to be regarded as automatically void. Moreover, the members of the third group were not elected and given membership in the council on the basis of elections; rather the king appointed certain persons from them and granted them this membership. This added insult to injury, and deprived the people of a prominent, active role in taking decisions and putting them into effect.

Add to this that the king was not bound to consult it at all times; rather — as we mentioned a little earlier — its meeting turned on the king's wish and inclination. In 1789, 116 years had passed since its last meeting, such that it had not convened any meeting throughout this period. When democratic ideas began to creep into popular circles, the demands for convening the council increased; on the other hand, the king wished to burden the subjects with more taxes to satisfy his instinct of luxury and extravagance, so he wanted to seize the opportunity to issue a decision concerning burdening the people with taxes, since he was confident of the agreement of the two estates of men of religion and nobles to it. When the meeting was requested, many of the people demanded the doubling of the numbers of the representatives of the third estate so that it would be equal with the numbers of the first two estates, justifying [this] by the fact that one third of the council was not enough to represent the whole people, and that it was nothing but unfairness and injustice to the people; so the king agreed to it. Then the people demanded the merging of the three estates into one national council, so that the opinion of their majority would be implemented; but the king was not pleased with it, because he knew precisely and well that the representatives of the people would resist him in what he wanted in taking decisions for his personal benefit. When he refused to do so, the people rose up and established for themselves an assembly, disregarding the estate of the men of religion and the nobles, and called their assembly the National Assembly (National Assembly) (and this was the first use of this word that later became famous in political circles), and demanded its meeting.

[p. 96] When the king saw that matters had become serious and the affair had grown intense, he tried to contain the situation by ordering the army to close the area in which the members of the assembly were to meet. So the assembly moved its meeting to a tennis court, and adopted there a constitution for France laid down on the basis of Montesquieu's theory concerned with the separation of powers and their division into three: legislative, executive and judicial. They also laid down in it a chapter on the basic rights necessary for the freedom of the individual. They did not abolish monarchy in it; rather they acknowledged its existence, but they narrowed the powers of the king and confined them to the executive power only, so that the two powers, legislative and judicial, would be completely independent of it.

This was the first constitution of France, which is regarded as the first state with a liberal democratic character. Coinciding with the session of the National Assembly, a great crowd rushed towards the royal palace and forced the king to attend the session of the assembly; so he attended it against his will to adopt the new constitution that the assembly had prepared, contenting himself with the presidency of the executive after the two bodies of legislation and judiciary had been taken from him.

Reacting to the danger that surrounded the king, and regarding it as a first step towards removing him from office, and fearing deprivation of what remained of his meagre official authority, the king strengthened his connection with his German counterpart so that he could obtain military support from him if he needed to use violence and force to repel the popular currents against him. The German king agreed to that, for fear of the worsening danger spreading to him if it succeeded in the heart of the French system. To implement this plan of theirs, conspiracies were arranged between the two kings on matters. The revolutionaries managed to expose this conspiracy and learned of the alliance that had taken place between the two kings against them. Although the king denied it and regarded it as an accusation and propaganda, the revolutionaries insisted on their position, until they imprisoned King Louis XVI, which led to the suspension of the constitution that the king had adopted, since it had been based on the continuation of the monarchical system. So they convened another conference of the National Assembly, which led to the adoption of a new constitution that delegated the king's authority and powers to a committee called "the National Convention" (Convention).

[p. 97] This government put Louis XVI on trial for his treason against the state, and the National Convention convicted him of the crime of treason (alliance with Germany to launch an attack on the people of France), and sentenced him to death, which was carried out on him publicly before the eyes of witnesses. With this the monarchical system came to an end.

And since the leaders of the revolutionary current were devoid of experience in running the system — despite the sharpness and zeal they had in abundance — they also lacked the principles for securing coordination and discipline. This led to the winds of disagreement blowing and conflicts exploding among them, which resulted in the outbreak of long civil wars, the prevalence of anarchy and the spread of disturbances and the absence of the rule of law, to the point that the people remembered the monarchical system, preferring it to that state of tension and wishing for its return to them. In the end they resorted to seeking help from the military forces to intervene in administrative affairs; their commander at that time was Napoleon Bonaparte, so he took up the reins of government, and the people accepted him when they saw the failure and fiasco of the National Convention. So another dictatorial government was established; but the credit goes to Napoleon for strengthening democratic ideals, even though the people itself had conferred legitimacy on his government and granted him all authorities and powers. It relied on the fact that the zealous, sharp revolutionary current and its deteriorating method were what had led to the sacrifices of the people as mentioned earlier. He succeeded at first in eliminating the civil wars, and fought the other states, and defeated the army of Austria (Austria) until he sailed to Egypt; and on the other hand, to support democratic ideals, he established an assembly from his companions for this purpose.

He achieved victories and won the wars that broke out between him and many states, until he launched an attack on the forces of England at the place "Waterloo", but suffered a bitter defeat in it as a result of the enemy's enormous numerical superiority; after that Napoleon died. However, his companions, who enjoyed the revolutionary idea, established democracy after him — and this is what is called "the Third Democracy" [sic: the Third Republic]. Following the French Revolution that ended with the establishment of the [p. 98] democratic system, revolutions followed one another and changes succeeded each other in other European states. The values and ideals that the French Revolution supported and strengthened are embodied in three principles: (1) freedom of the individual; (2) separation of powers; (3) separation between religion and the state. As a result of it these theories were spread in the other states of Europe, and the liberal democratic system became widespread in the world.

This, then, was a brief sketch of the two revolutions, American and French, which played their part in consolidating liberal democratic ideals in the world.

### Organizations and institutions arising from democracy (organizations working to realize democratic principles)

After the passing glance at the history of the development of democracy, we would like to turn the reins of our attention to explaining the institutions and organizations that are established to realize the demands of democracy and attain the goal intended from it. In this regard we single out for mention four of those organizations, namely: (1) political parties; (2) elections; (3) the legislature; (4) the judiciary.

#### (1) Political parties

Among the most important principles and ideals of democracy is that it guarantees citizens the freedom to establish parties and associations without imposing any restriction on a party or group. Proceeding from this, the organizations established under democracy and in its countries can be classified into three types:

**(a) Interest groups (Interest Group)**

That is, the group that is founded to attain the common interest and achieve the common goal among a particular faction of the people: for example, men from a particular professional sector undertake to establish a group working to look after its rights under mutual cooperation among its members. It does not aspire to possess authority and ascend the seat of government; it only aims at non-political goals. This association [p. 99] is called among political scholars (Interest Group) "interest group" or "association of common interests".

**(b) Pressure groups (Pressure Group)**

This group also does not aspire to government and does not aim with its efforts at attaining authority; its function is only pressure on the government to make it meet its demands, or to integrate its policies into a specific government policy.

**(c) Political parties**

"A group of citizens who believe in common political and ideological goals and organize themselves with the aim of reaching power and realizing their programme."

The method of forming a political party in democracy is that people first think about the programme or the economic and social method that they consider most beneficial and most reforming for the state, then they issue a statement containing that programme, aiming to put it into effect if they gain power. This political statement is a working document considered by a political party, including its goals and plans for running the wheel of government if it succeeds in elections and is able to form the government. Then this party calls people to join it and come to an understanding on its political programme, then all of them devote their efforts to reaching power so that they can bring it into the realm of implementation. If it reaches the seat of government, it is then obliged to act according to its programme with which it attracted the attention of voters and enticed them to give their votes to its candidates. And the programme is what distinguishes between two political parties: one political party adopts a particular ideology and special visions, while the other adopts others like it. If there were no difference between the policies of two parties and their programmes, there would be no justification for forming the second party while the party possessing the same ideology and seeking the same goal exists. This, then, is democracy's view of political parties.

[p. 100] The prevailing systems concerning political parties differ among states. Some of them have implemented the one-party system (One Party System), which is the prevalent system in most communist states, where they impose a legal ban on the establishment of another political party. Meanwhile, many states have had the two-party system prevail, where their law allows the establishment of political parties alongside these two parties; but the results of elections have proved more than once the failure and fiasco of the small parties, which in its wake leads either to the vanishing of those parties or their becoming of little importance, such that it becomes clear in fact that the state is governed only by the two parties — which alternate in power — nothing else. It is the prevalent system in the United Kingdom and America.

In some states the system of "party pluralism" has prevailed, in which there are many political parties, small and large, striving to reach power, and it is difficult to predict in advance which party will win the largest number of seats in the legislature.

Although the formation of political parties is not a necessary part of the democratic system, it has become established that it actually exists everywhere democracy has prevailed. And although many experts in political science see political parties as negatively affecting the demands of democracy and as not beneficial to its interests, it has become impossible to find in the external world a democratic state free of a political party!

#### (2) Elections

Elections in the democratic system are regarded as one of the most important political practices as a peaceful means of assuming authority and taking up the reins of government. First, elections are held to choose candidates for membership of the legislative bodies; then the parliamentary system permits the party that has won the largest number of seats in them to form the government. Elections in the democratic system are usually conducted by one of the following two methods:

**(1) Direct elections:** meaning that every adult citizen is granted the right to vote [p. 101] and cast a ballot — male or female, young or old, educated or ignorant — so that he may elect, and then the candidate who obtains the largest number of votes succeeds.

**(2) As for indirect elections**, they mean that the people elect people who in turn undertake the election of members of the legislature. In detail: the people undertake to choose persons qualified to elect members of parliament, to carry out the task of choosing them. This was the prevalent method in Pakistan in the era of President Ayub Khan, who implemented the electoral college system, meaning that society would choose the central council, and this council would choose whoever was to hold membership in parliament. The prevalent method in the world today is the method of direct elections.

**Qualifications of the voter**

The systems of states also differ in determining the conditions that must be present in persons so that they have the right to vote. Switzerland can be taken as an example of that: women did not have the right to vote until five or four years ago. When voices were raised about their right to vote, it was withheld from them, first by rejecting it, on the argument that granting them this right would only lead to family disputes on the basis of differing political orientations between spouses, since the husband might give his vote to someone while the wife casts her vote for another person. But that argument of theirs was not accepted, and they were finally granted the right to vote and cast ballots.

Today, however, the system of voting for every adult citizen has become widespread in the world.

**Single-member election in one round**

It is the method prevailing in the world for elections today, and it is based on the basis of one winner from each electoral district, represented by the one who obtains the highest number of votes in that district, whatever the percentage of his election — and he may not represent the opinion of the majority in that district. For if we suppose there are many candidates for one seat, and one of them obtains 15% of the total [p. 102] votes in that district while no one else reaches this percentage, he wins, even though he represents only a very small percentage of the inhabitants of his electoral district.

**Single-member election in two rounds (Second Ballot)**

For this reason many states have chosen other electoral methods that guarantee them protection from that harm. France implemented the system of single-member election in two rounds, which means: any candidate who obtains 50% + 1 of the votes of his candidacy district is regarded as the winner. If no candidate obtains 50% + 1, elections are held in it again, and it is stipulated for candidacy that one obtained 12% in the previous elections; and the winner is regarded as whoever obtains the highest number of votes in it.

**Proportional representation (Proportionate Representation)**

The forms of applying proportional representation differ in the states that follow it. Among them: that the ballot paper is empty of the names of candidates; rather it carries the names of political parties and their symbols, and the voters are charged with marking on the ballot paper the party they choose. When the elections end, the percentage of votes each party won is looked at, and it is granted a number of seats in the legislature in the same percentage. If a party, for example, won 30% of the votes, it obtains 30% of the number of seats in the legislature. Then that party is asked for a list of candidates to fill those seats.

However, some states follow in this regard the system of preferential voting, whose summary is: every political party presents a list of its candidates for membership in the legislature, to be acted upon according to the ascending order that that party set.
