
[p. 179] Perhaps those infatuated with modern political theories, whose sight does not go beyond the glitter of matter, are astonished at the connection of prayer and ablution with politics and government. But those expert in the Sharia and acquainted with its method know very well that Islam tries to keep pace between the firm bond with God and spiritual awareness, and between politics, economics and other fields of life, side by side; and from this starting point its teachings in those fields cannot be separated from the aspects of spiritual awareness latent in them.

The Muslim, when he stands five times daily before his Creator in the prayer with the utmost humility and abasement, keeping present his standing before his Lord on the Day of Resurrection — this very state continues into what is after the prayer too, and pervades all the moments of his life. So he rises in the morning and enters the evening carrying within him an abiding sense of his responsibility for everything his hands earn and his feet lead him to. So do not ask about the extent of the role of this state in forming his personality and coordinating his life and organizing his deeds and his times. From this God the Exalted said: **"Indeed, prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing, and the remembrance of God is greater. God knows that which you do"** [al-ʿAnkabūt, 29:45].

So prayer prohibits wrongdoing by virtue of its reminding a person of God, glorified and exalted, and the feeling is awakened in him that God knows what he does and none of his deeds is hidden from Him. So a person does not dare to commit wrongdoing while he keeps present the existence of God with him and that He sees him and knows his doing and his deed?! No!

As for the secularists who do not acknowledge the prayer and its aforementioned role in principle, they deny this effect of it, relying on some exceptional cases of a few individuals, saying: the prayer of so-and-so did not prohibit him from immorality and wrongdoing; rather he was one who prays and commits such-and-such of the major sins at the same time.

We say to them: if we turned a blind eye to the exaggeration that usually accompanies the mention of these examples, and granted that their prayer does not prohibit them from immorality and wrongdoing, we would say that the ruling on a thing is not made by looking at one or two of its individuals; rather the few and the many in it must be taken into account. So if we were today to compare between those who are regular in establishing the prayer and others besides them, we would find [p. 180] the first group the most adorned of them with virtuous qualities of truthfulness, justice, trustworthiness and other virtues, and the furthest of them from indecencies, wrongs, lying, treachery, betrayal and other vices. This in an age in which there is no coordinated system for the spiritual awareness of those who pray and their religious education — so how would it be under the shade of an Islamic government that officially provides them with the possibilities of religious learning and culture, and makes available to them the opportunities for purifying the soul and refining character, which are among its functions as a government that strives to fulfil the needs of the people and its religious requirements?

At any rate, because of the aforementioned standing of the prayer and its effect on reforming society, the Qur'an gave precedence to mentioning it among the purposes and aims of Islamic government.

#### 2 – Paying the zakat

The second function the Noble Qur'an mentioned for Islamic government is that it see to the payment of zakat. That is so that it becomes clearly evident that it does not aim at the luxury and ease of the life of rulers and princes and at piling up wealth in their treasuries and accounts; rather it strives — in compliance with the ruling of God — to improve the condition of the poor economically and help them in providing the necessary needs for them. Second, because zakat plays its role in uprooting the motives of selfishness and love of the world — which is the head of every sin — from the one who pays it on one side, and creating sentiments of altruism in him on the other. Third, because zakat is a purification of man's wealth and an attempt to straighten his conduct in the world of wealth, just as prayer purifies his very self and is a way to straighten his manner of life.

#### 3 & 4 – Commanding right and forbidding wrong

The third and fourth functions of Islamic government: that it undertake commanding what is right (*al-maʿrūf*) and forbidding what is wrong (*al-munkar*). Let it be known that the Qur'an made the undertaking of these two matters the function of every Muslim and his duty in general, saying: **"You are the best nation produced for mankind: you enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong and believe in God"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:110].

But it is in different degrees and many ranks, which the Prophet ﷺ explained by his saying: "Whoever of [p. 181] you sees a wrong, let him change it with his hand; if he is unable, then with his tongue; if he is unable, then with his heart — and that is the weakest of faith."¹

The jurists have detailed these ranks with elaboration and explanation, whose upshot is: that the attempt to change the wrong with the tongue is the function of every Muslim, on condition that he not fear that there will befall him — in its wake — a harm he cannot bear or endure. As appears in another hadith, which al-Haythamī mentioned attributing it to al-Bazzār and al-Ṭabarānī, that Ibn ʿUmar said: "I heard al-Ḥajjāj give a sermon, and he mentioned words which I disapproved of, and I wanted to change [it]; then I remembered the saying of the Messenger of God ﷺ: 'It is not fitting for the believer to humiliate himself.' He said: I said: O Messenger of God, how does he humiliate himself? He said: He exposes himself to affliction for what he cannot bear."²

As for disapproving with the heart, it is obligatory on every Muslim as well; and it is the weakest rank of faith.

Some of the scholars held that disapproving with the heart is not a rank among the ranks of changing; rather its outcome is changing with the tongue itself, and the meaning is that he disapproves of it in the heart with an intense disapproval that brings about anxiety and distress in it, which urges him and carries him to try to change it with the tongue by continuous attempts until he eliminates that wrong and annihilates it.

As for changing it with the hand, it is the function of the commanders (*al-umarāʾ*), as appears in *al-Fatāwā al-Hindiyya*: "And it is said: commanding right with the hand is for the commanders, and with the tongue for the scholars, and with the heart for the common people; and it is the choice of al-Zandawaysatī; thus in *al-Ẓahīriyya*."³

What is meant by "the commanders" here is everyone who has obtained some authority and influence, to the point that the head of the family, if he saw a wrong in it, must use that authority of his to put an end to that wrong. Whoever has influence in an institution must undertake changing the wrongs that occur in it, using that authority of his. But the greatest responsibility in it falls on the shoulders of the head of state, because of what he has

In this category fall the matters in ruling on which the Sharia has taken human customs and traditions into account.

This makes clear the difference between Islam and democracy: democracy has entrusted the ruling on all things to human reason, to choose in every age by its majority what it sees as appropriate for it and achieving its interests; whereas Islam — as is its way — has put matters in their proper places: it enacted explicit legislation in the matters that are beyond the grasp of human reason, and which would be likely sites of slip and error for it if it were left to itself in ruling on them — this on one hand. On the other, it did not bind it with explicit rulings in the matters that fall within the scope of its experience; rather it gave it free rein [p. 184] in them to choose by consultation what it sees as appropriate to it and to its circumstances and interests.

#### 5. Establishing justice and fairness

The fifth function of Islamic government in the light of the teachings of the Qur'an is: establishing justice and fairness. God the Exalted said to David, upon him be peace: **"O David, indeed We have made you a vicegerent upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth and do not follow [your own] desire, as it will lead you astray from the way of God"** [Ṣād, 38:26].

So He commanded David, upon him be peace, in his capacity as a vicegerent, to judge among people in truth — which indicates that judging among the subjects in truth and establishing justice and fairness among them is among the duties of Islamic government and the aims sought from it. Judging in truth covers observing justice and fairness both in judicial rulings and administrative matters together.

It is necessary to draw attention here to what we have presented more than once: that justice and fairness are two terms which each person can explain according to what he desires. But the truth is that justice in the terminology of the Qur'an is nothing but what God and His Messenger regarded as justice. As for the interpretations of justice other than that which clash with the rulings of the Sharia, they are not justice; rather injustice, aggression and the following of desire only. From this God the Exalted mentioned — in the same verse — the following of desire in the context of contrast with judging in truth, and drew attention to its leading astray from the way of God.

So the judicial ruling, if it clashes with a ruling of God's rulings and contradicts it, is not justice; rather it is injustice and tyranny, as God, glorified and exalted, said: **"And whoever does not judge by what God has revealed — then it is those who are the wrongdoers"** [al-Māʾida, 5:45].

He commanded the Prophet ﷺ to judge with equity (*al-qisṭ*), saying: **"And if you judge, judge between them with equity. Indeed, God loves those who act equitably"** [al-Māʾida, 5:42]. After that He commanded him to judge by what God revealed, saying: **"So judge between them by what God has revealed and do not follow their inclinations"** [al-Māʾida, 5:48] — which indicated that judging with equity is nothing but judging by [what] God [revealed], and whatever is other than that is only the following of desire.

The gist of the discussion: you do not see a government among governments but it openly claims [p. 185] to establish justice and equity and fulfil their requirements according to its own invented notions of them. The truth is that justice and judging in truth and equity and fairness — all these words convey one meaning, namely: that the ruling be in accordance with the rulings of God the Exalted which He revealed Himself in the Qur'an, or the Prophet ﷺ taught them, so they reached us by way of his hadiths.

As an example of that let us take democracy: it does not cease claiming that it strives to establish justice and fairness, and that all are equal before it in the implementation of the law on them without difference between one and another. But it is a claim contradicted publicly by the observed external reality: at the same time there are many democratic societies that still grant the head of state, by an exception in the constitution, that he not be tried nor be a litigant at all in any court. So what justice is this, then?! But they still defend this position, which contradicts all the requirements of justice, and do not regard it as anything of injustice!

It also clearly and explicitly contradicts the concept of justice from the Islamic point of view; for Islam does not reserve for anyone — head of state or other — the right to exception from the law, as the Prophet ﷺ said: "Those before you were destroyed only because when a noble among them stole, they would leave him, and when a weak one among them stole, they would apply the ḥadd to him. By God, if Fāṭima the daughter of Muhammad stole, I would cut off her hand."¹

## Chapter Three: A Study of the Issues of Forming a Government from an Islamic Perspective

### Introduction

In this chapter we would like to set out the basic principles that Islam has granted concerning the formation of the government that will aim at its goals and perform its functions mentioned a little earlier. We shall study in it many of the issues that relate to the aforementioned heading from an Islamic perspective.

### 1 – Islam's position on seeking authority

Since it has become clear from what we mentioned at the end of Chapter Two that Islam sees authority as a grave responsibility and not a right, it does not permit a person to seek authority and ask for it and exert his efforts and spend his attempts in attaining it. As is indicated by the prohibition in a hadith which Imam al-Bukhārī included in his *Ṣaḥīḥ* from Samura ibn Jundab, may God be pleased with him, that the Messenger of God, God bless him and give him peace, said to him: "Do not ask for authority; for if you are given it as a result of asking, you will be left to it [alone]; and if you are given it without asking, you will be helped in it."³

> ³ *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (8/127), Book of Oaths, 6622.

[p. 191] A hadith has also come concerning seeking the judgeship, which is like an explanation and clarification of this hadith mentioned above, namely what al-Tirmidhī included from Anas, may God be pleased with him, as marfūʿ [attributed to the Prophet]: "Whoever seeks the judgeship and asks for intercessors in it is left to himself; and whoever is compelled to it, God sends down upon him an angel who guides him aright."¹

So this hadith indicated that the meaning of "he is left to himself" is that whoever obtains these posts and reaches these ranks by seeking and attempts on his part through seeking intercession for them and other things, God does not help him nor support him in performing the tasks of his post in anything; rather He entrusts his affair to himself, so that it takes him to whatever watering place it wills and leads him astray in whatever valley it wills! May God protect us from it. It is a severe threat, the full severity of which — and hence the seriousness of this man's predicament — can hardly be grasped except by one who believes in God, glorified and exalted, and believes that a person does not conclude a matter nor accomplish a work except if success accompanies him in it and help from Him, mighty and exalted, goes with him in it.

It is narrated in another hadith from Abū Mūsā al-Ashʿarī, may God be pleased with him, that he said: "I entered upon the Prophet ﷺ, I and two men of my people. One of the two men said: Make us commanders, O Messenger of God; and the other said the like of it. He said: 'We do not appoint over this one who asks for it, nor one who is eager for it.'"²

Here is another hadith which al-Bukhārī included from ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿAmr, may God be pleased with him, that the Messenger of God ﷺ said: "And you will find among the best of people the most intensely averse of them to this matter until he falls into it."³

So these hadiths indicated clearly and explicitly that asking for authority and seeking office are not permissible, and that it makes whoever asks for it and aspires to it a person unfit for it.

[p. 192] While some held the permissibility of asking for authority and seeking office, arguing by what the Noble Qur'an mentioned of Joseph, upon him be peace — that he sought it from the king of Egypt, saying: **"Appoint me over the storehouses of the land. Indeed, I will be a knowing guardian"** [Yūsuf, 12:55].

The matter went further with some, in that they argued by it — in view of Joseph, upon him be peace, having sought it and expressed his competence and fitness for it by explaining his qualities and qualifications — that it is permissible for a person today to stand as a candidate in elections and disclose before people what he has of qualities, virtues and qualifications, so as to draw them to cast their votes for him at the time of balloting and voting.

The answer to this argument is from two aspects:

**The first of them:** that Joseph, upon him be peace, came to know of the king's resolve to appoint him — on the king's own initiative, not at Joseph's request — to one of the posts of government; he knew this from the king's words which God mentioned just before this verse: **"And the king said: 'Bring him to me; I will appoint him exclusively for myself.' And when he spoke to him, he said: 'Indeed, you are today established [in position] and trusted with us'"** [Yūsuf, 12:54]. So when he knew that the king was determined on accomplishing that resolve of his, the question remained about specifying the post which he would entrust to him. So at this stage he asked him to make him over the storehouses of the land. So it is not correct, then, to say that he asked for office or post in the meaning that the Sharia has forbidden; for he did not send the king a request from prison asking to be appointed to a post, nor did he spend money for it or seek intercession for it. So how then is it correct to argue by it for the permissibility of what goes on today in political circles at the time of elections?!

**Second:** what we presented of the prohibition of asking for authority and seeking office is the original ruling of the Sharia, while the Sharia itself permitted seeking it in some special exceptional cases, as we shall clarify shortly, God willing. So it is possible to interpret the action of Joseph, upon him be peace, as having been in that exceptional case in which seeking office is permitted.

There is a hadith by which some may argue for the permissibility of seeking office, namely what Abū Dāwūd narrated in his *Sunan* from Abū Hurayra, may God be pleased with him, from the Prophet ﷺ: he said: "Whoever seeks

[p. 193] the judgeship of the Muslims until he obtains it, then his justice prevails over his injustice, for him is Paradise."¹

But we say: leaving aside what is in its chain from the words of the hadith scholars, it can also be interpreted as that exceptional case in which the Sharia permits seeking office.

The detail of that is that the hadiths have conflicted in this issue: some of them show that asking for authority is not permissible — and it is what we presented earlier; and among them is what indicates its permissibility — and it is the hadith we mentioned just now. The scholars of the Sharia have put both these kinds into effect by reconciling them: that the principle is the prohibition of asking for authority and the prohibition of seeking office. But perhaps there arises a case in which a person feels with certainty that if he did not seek office, those who are not competent in it would take charge of it, which would result in usurping the rights of the Muslims and wronging them. So in such a case it is permissible for him to ask for it, on condition that he have a desire in it for establishing the truth, and fear that someone undeserving will take it on, and that his intention not be status and display. This is the position which al-Qāḍī Abū Yaʿlā al-Ḥanbalī (may God have mercy on him) held in *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya*, and a group of the later scholars supported it, among them ʿAllāma Ẓafar Aḥmad al-ʿUthmānī (may God have mercy on him) in his valuable book *Iʿlāʾ al-Sunan*.

The upshot of what we presented is that in the general run of cases it is not permissible to seek authority; and excepted from it is when it has become incumbent on a person to seek it, namely if he fears that if he did not seek it, it would go to one who does not deserve it nor is qualified for it, which would lead to corruption and the loss of the rights of the Muslims. So in this exceptional case it is permissible for him to seek it with the aim of reform and establishing the truth, not of obtaining status and rank.

### The alternative form to candidacy in elections

The foregoing detail has raised in turn a question in the mind of every reader: that since it is established that seeking authority is a matter not permissible in the Sharia in the general run of cases, what then will fill the place of candidacy for parliamentary membership, on which the electoral structure in this age of ours stands? For if there is no candidate, then in favour of whom will the voting be, and how?

> ¹ *Sunan Abī Dāwūd* (3/299), chapter on the judge who errs and is correct, hadith no. 3575.

[p. 194] Before going deep into answering this question, it is fitting for us to draw our attention to an important point which may remove the veil from our eyes in many issues that have a connection and similarity with this issue of ours, namely that whenever a system or method has prevailed on the international scope and found currency and acceptance in all or most of the world, it becomes rooted in people's minds such that they do not look to other than it — what could be made an alternative that substitutes for it and takes its place in its work; rather they see it as not worthy of thinking about and not capable of implementation at all. Whereas if they freed themselves in thinking about it from the influence of the environment in which they live, they would find it as capable of implementation as its original, with all ease and simplicity.

Candidacy for elections is likewise. First, it is not universal throughout the world; rather there are several other formulas which — even in this age of ours — flourishing states have adopted for elections with ease and success: in one state there is the system of proportional representation; in another two-round voting (Second ballot); and in another: the transferable vote. So it is not correct, then, to regard the prevailing method as a necessity whose place nothing else can fill at all. Rather, if it were rooted in the mind once that seeking authority — besides its being impermissible in the Sharia — is an evil that has brought down our political system, then offering an alternative to it would no longer seem as difficult as people imagine.

We propose here an alternative to the system of contemporary candidacy, which can be adopted with ease on one hand, and which will eliminate the social and economic evils that human society faces as a result of it on the other. Its detail: that the inhabitants of every electoral district be asked to nominate in their turn, to represent them in the membership of parliament, persons possessing specified qualifications for membership: scholarly attainments, social service, practical experience, moral conduct and so on. Let the elections committee undertake to specify a special number — of 500 or 1,000 or other — so that if the number of nominators from the inhabitants of an electoral district in favour of one of the candidates reaches that specified number, let the committee review whether this nomination is genuine or falsified, through examining and discovering several matters: do the nominators

[p. 195] actually inhabit that electoral district or not? Did they truly reach the specified required number or not? Is the person whom they nominated qualified to stand in the elections, having the required qualifications, or not? After ascertaining these matters, let the committee accept his candidacy papers for membership. So if those for whom the nomination reached the specified number were more than one, then for choosing one of them let it call people to vote and ballot in favour of one of these.

Instead of the candidate himself undertaking to call people to cast their votes — which usually drags him into self-praise, false promises about his future after success, disparaging the competitors among the other candidates, and spending exorbitant sums in that, as is prevalent today — let the elections committee itself undertake the task of publicizing them and introducing them to the voters using the media of all kinds, and let it arrange interviews and meetings for them if it feels a need for them. Thus people will become acquainted from close by with the candidates in their electoral districts with a true acquaintance not mixed with praises and claims; and their scholarly qualifications, and their conduct in their past, and the extent of their understanding of the matters of their present and the issues of their people, and their being adorned with the noble qualities of truthfulness, trustworthiness and so on.

As for today, the candidate does not content himself with deceiving people and raising their hopes with false promises only; rather he spends on publicizing himself and introducing himself to the voters exorbitant sums and vast amounts of money. So its consequence was that it no longer remained possible for anyone to stand as a candidate except one who has surplus of these exorbitant sums beyond his needs on one side; and on another side, the one who spends it usually sees it as a profitable investment that yields him, after winning, more than what he spent, by fraud and embezzlement of state funds.

In view of this, in our prevailing political environment and system it has become almost impossible for truthful, trustworthy men of sound thought and sincere intention to ascend the thrones of government through elections.

From this I am confident that if this proposed alternative to the system of contemporary candidacy were accepted, and publicity were carried out by the elections committee alone, without allowing anyone

[p. 196] to use its prevailing widespread means, it would go a long way towards eliminating the evils we have just mentioned.

On the day of balloting, let it likewise be entrusted to it — not to the candidates — to run the camps outside the polling stations from which voters collect their papers, so that the last door is closed through which public opinion could be negatively influenced by the mechanisms of pressure on voters, encouragement and inducement.

The system (CONVINCING) [sic: canvassing] has actually been abandoned in some states; rather they have come to content themselves, after registering the names of the candidates, with conducting interviews and dialogues with them and broadcasting them on the visual and audio media only, without conducting election campaigns and meetings, nor blackening the walls of the public and distorting them with advertisements and banners full of praise and false promises.

It will also serve, as a successful alternative system, to enable the people to exercise their right to vote with full freedom and independence — a right the feudal lords had taken from them, so they became subject to them and to their wills, such that no role remained for them in voting except using their fingers like a machine programmed to put the ballot paper in the special ballot box only, without that being by consent from them and voluntarily!

What adds insult to injury is what has prevailed in our country of the single-member system, in the sense that from an electoral district — regional or national — only one candidate is elected, represented by whoever obtains the largest number of votes even if they are very few relative to all the voters in that district. An example of that: if the candidates from an electoral district were ten persons, and nine of them obtained a thousand each, while one of them obtained two thousand votes, he is regarded as the winner, even though the opinion of most voters in that district goes to the opposite of it. By contrast, if wide electoral districts were taken, and the system of multi-member representation were chosen — that from a large district four members are elected according to their ranks in obtaining votes, whether by the transferable votes or otherwise — that would go a long way towards ending this evil.

[p. 197] We repeat here what we presented earlier: that Islam in the field of politics contented itself with legislating basic principles only, and entrusted the forms of their application and the formulas of their practical implementation to the scholars of the Sharia and those expert in it in every age and city. So the principles of Islam in the field of forming the government are embodied in these two principles: the prohibition of seeking authority, and founding it on consultation (*shūrā*). But it gave them flexibility that makes them fit to keep pace and go along with every age and every time. So there is scope for the scholars of the Sharia and the people of insight among the Muslims to elaborate on the alternative we have just proposed and to work out more of its details and particulars in the light of their seasoned experiences and their expertise in the conditions of contemporary politics.

At any rate, it is clearly not impossible to present a practical alternative capable of implementation for the system of candidacy in contemporary elections, which agrees with the principles of the Sharia on one side and is free of the evils that are born of the prevailing system on another side.

### The conditions and qualities that qualify a ruler for authority

Modern democratic systems usually do not specify special descriptions and conditions that must be present in the head of the country or the members of the legislature; rather they usually content themselves in that with what has been written in the constitutions: that he be a citizen of the country, holding its nationality, registered by name in the list of voters with their conditions, including that he has reached a certain age. They do not concern themselves with other conditions and qualities such as scholarly qualifications, practical experiences, moral conduct or anything else. Rather obtaining the largest number of votes is the only element among them that qualifies a person for the presidency of the country. From this we have often seen an ignorant person of bad character and conduct who ascended the throne of the presidency merely by his obtaining the majority of the voters' votes; so he becomes head of state even though his immersion in immoral activities is no secret; indeed, stories of it continue to circulate openly long after he has left office. Take for that, for example, John Kennedy, the first American president who made Russia surrender to his policies: the stories of his interest in sex and his immersion in its activities are still printed and prevalent to this day.

[p. 198] Whereas Islam is distinguished among those systems in that from the very beginning it attends to specifying conditions and descriptions that must be present in a person who intends the presidency of the country, and does not permit anyone to take charge of the presidency of the country and the authority over the Muslims as long as those conditions are not present in him and he has not obtained those descriptions. The scholars have in this subject extensive studies and long discussions; we spare you their trouble by presenting a brief summary of them in what follows, God willing.

#### The first condition

That he be of sound mind, adult, with sound senses.

#### The second condition

That he be a Muslim. That is because God regarded polytheism as a great wrong, saying: **"Indeed, association [with God] is great injustice"** [Luqmān, 31:13]; and He mentioned in another verse that the wrongdoer does not attain God's covenant latent in the authority over the Muslims and the government of their country, saying: **"My covenant does not include the wrongdoers"** [al-Baqara, 2:124]. The upshot of this is that the polytheist does not attain the covenant of God, glorified and exalted, at all; so it is not correct to make him Commander of the Faithful and ruler of their country.

It is self-evident that the Islamic state must have a Muslim head; because government from an Islamic perspective has its special functions and duties, and the aims which Islam seeks from establishing it — and these are matters and works which it would be futile to expect an unbeliever to perform and carry out properly.

Despite its being a self-evident matter, those who have wrapped themselves in the cloak of democracy and have come to look at all matters with a democratic view only have raised doubts and objections about it too, as was witnessed at the time of laying down the constitution for Pakistan. For the clause that means stipulating Islam for the office of the presidency of the country was among the clauses that stirred up discontent and poured out anger in democratic circles, as being an injustice to the non-Muslim citizens.
