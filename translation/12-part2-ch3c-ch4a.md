
[p. 219] covenanted to Yazīd only because of what I saw of his merit, then bring him to what I hoped for and help him; and if it was only love of a father for his child that carried me, and he is not worthy, then take him before he reaches that."¹

The truth is that this action of his had dire consequences that brought woes upon the Umma; for his action became a followed precedent in the Umma, which those who followed him of the imams of tyranny and the caliphs of seizure and greed held fast to, and the caliphate came to be inherited like property and goods. The Umma was deprived of that blessed Sunna adopted for choosing the imam in the days of the Rightly-Guided Caliphate, except in very rare exceptional cases. I have spoken at length on this discussion in my Urdu book *Ḥaḍrat Muʿāwiya aur Tārīkhī Ḥaqāʾiq* (Our Master Muʿāwiya in the Light of Historical Constants).

**The third point:** the scholars of Islamic political jurisprudence differed concerning the status of this covenant and designation of a successor: is it binding on the Muslims after the death of the imam, or does it depend on the approval of the people who loosen and bind? Some held that the covenant by which the rightful imam who combines the conditions of the imamate covenanted is regarded after him as binding on the Muslims, with the reasoning that it is among the totality of the rulings which he issued in his lifetime in the period of his imamate; so obedience to him in it is obligatory like his other rulings. While others held that his covenant is nothing but a proposal and counsel, and its implementation depends on the approval of the people who loosen and bind for it: if they approve it, it is in force; otherwise not. It is what ʿAllāma Abū Yaʿlā al-Farrāʾ (may God have mercy on him) chose in his book *al-Aḥkām al-Sulṭāniyya*, saying: "That the imamate of the one covenanted to is concluded after his death by the choice of the people of the time."²

ʿAllāma Ibn Taymiyya (may God have mercy on him) clarified it, saying: "And likewise ʿUmar, when Abū Bakr covenanted to him, only became imam when they pledged allegiance to him and obeyed him; and had it been supposed that they did not implement the covenant of Abū Bakr and did not pledge allegiance to him, he would not have become imam."³

What Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him, did also supports it: when he wanted to conclude the caliphate for ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him,

[p. 220] after him, he called first ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf and second ʿUthmān ibn ʿAffān, may God be pleased with them both, and consulted them in the matter. When they advised him to [do] it, he looked out upon the people from his balcony and said to them in what he said: "And I have appointed ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb as successor, so hear him and obey." They said: "We hear and we obey."¹

Thus Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him, did not act by his opinion alone in the designation of a successor; rather he covenanted to ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, after consultation on it and the consent of the people who loosen and bind with it, seeking in it justice, truth and the interest. And even if we granted that his designation alone was binding on the Muslims, does anyone today reach his level in the soundness of opinion, the correctness of judgement and the sincerity of intention? No!

So what is correct is that designation of a successor does not go beyond being a proposal from the imam and counsel from him to the Muslims that they appoint the one he appointed as successor as imam over them after his death, and the reference in implementing it or not is to the people who loosen and bind among the Muslims; and they, concerning this proposal, have the two choices: if it seems right to them to implement it, they implement it; otherwise they reject it and choose someone else as imam.

The answer to what the people of the first position said — that the designation of a successor must be implemented as being a ruling of the rightful imam — is: that what is most correct is that the caliphate of the imam and his imamate end with his death; so if he died and his caliphate ended by it, his commands no longer have to be obeyed. ʿAllāma Ibn ʿĀbidīn (may God have mercy on him) devoted an independent heading to this discussion in *Radd al-Muḥtār*, and among what came in it: "It is inferred from the words of *Sharḥ al-Munya*, 'it was in their time…', that the command of the caliph does not survive his death or his removal, as was stated explicitly in *al-Fatāwā al-Khayriyya*; and built on it is that if he forbade hearing a claim after fifteen years, his prohibition does not remain after his death."²

### 5 – Seizing power by force (taghallub)

The third form for the conclusion of the imamate is: that a person seize power and authority and prevail over it against people's will by using force.

It is obligatory on the Muslims to resist and oppose him as far as they are able, acting by the words of God the Exalted: **"But if one of them oppresses the other, then fight against the one that oppresses until it returns to the ordinance of God"** [al-Ḥujurāt, 49:9].

But if his authority is established and his control is consolidated and his power stands firm, then he — even if he committed a great, heinous sin like rebellion and insurgency against the rightful imam, concerning which severe threats have come, like what the Prophet ﷺ said: "Six I have cursed, and God curses them, and every prophet [whose prayer] is answered… and the one who seizes power by tyranny to exalt thereby whom God has humbled and humble whom God has exalted"¹ —

nevertheless the jurists regarded this imamate of his as concluded and that leadership as standing; so his commands take on the character of commands issued from the caliph in terms of the necessity of obeying them and the obligation of complying with them, with their established conditions. The upshot is that his affair fluctuates between two aspects: as for one aspect, the Muslims should try to remove him and turn him away from the imamate on condition that there not be in it the stirring of a strife (*fitna*) that cannot be borne; and from another, they regarded his rulings as in force on condition that they not clash with the Sharia nor contradict it. The evidence for this second matter is the saying of the Prophet ﷺ: "Hear and obey, even if an Abyssinian whose head is like a raisin is appointed."²

The wisdom in that is that if we did not recognize those of his commands which do not contradict the Sharia, that would lead to sweeping anarchy among the Muslims. This is as is established in the world today, that rulers are of two kinds:

The legitimate constitutional ruler (De Jure): he is the one to whose authority the constitution of the country has given legitimacy, so he has become a legitimate ruler of it.

The actual ruler in reality (De Facto): i.e. the ruler who has actually taken up the reins of rule,

[p. 222] even if his rule has no legitimacy.

The meaning of that: that if a person took up rule and took charge of command over the state by rebellion against the legitimate ruler, or seizure, or other than that of unconstitutional ways, then the commands which he issued during his rule and the days of his command are granted legal validity, to protect the state from being swept away in the torrent of disturbance and sweeping anarchy which floods a state only to sweep it away and destroy it utterly. For rejecting his commands entirely and denying them totally entails that there be no weight for the judges whom he appointed and the governors who took charge from him of rule — from which follows the cancellation of their rulings by which they judged in the disputes of the subjects and their trials. So the consequence of all this would not be other than toying with the state and throwing it into the mill of anarchy, disturbance and disorder.

So to protect the Muslims from disturbance and anarchic life, the scholars of Islam held that the imamate is concluded in the case of seizure and domination, and consequently that his commands are valid and to be implemented as long as he is in control of authority — even though his audacity in this is among the major sins, and removing him by peaceful means is a duty incumbent on the Muslims.

### 6 – On the unity and multiplicity of the caliph

The majority of the scholars have agreed that the imam should be one for all the Muslims, and that there not be for every region of the regions of the Islamic state and every territory of its territories an independent imam. Al-Māwardī (may God have mercy on him) said: "And if the imamate is concluded for two imams in two lands, their imamate is not concluded; because it is not permissible for the Umma to have two imams at one time — even if a people deviated and permitted it."¹

They also argued — besides other proofs — by his ﷺ saying: "And there will be caliphs, and they will be many." They said: What do you command us? He said: "Fulfil the pledge of allegiance to the first, then the first."²

But Islamic government, if the areas of its influence widened and the extent of its authority stretched such that it became impossible for one imam to manage its affair, then in such a form it is permissible to distribute

## Chapter Four: Islamic Rules for the Administration of Government

### Introduction

[p. 225] Islam has wise teachings and right directives which are like the bright lamp, lighting the way for the government that has been formed on the basis of the principles we explained earlier. The Muslims must take them with regard and compliance in administering the helm of government, so that it is of an Islamic character and is dyed with a religious dye — not tyrannical and despotic, nor wrongful and oppressive. We would like to devote this chapter to explaining those principles and foundations which the imam follows in administering administrative affairs and social questions.

### 1 – Submission to the rulings of the Qur'an and Sunna

The basic principle for the imam of the Muslims is that he be, in all his commands, his affairs and his measures, subject to the rulings of the Book of God and the Sunna of His Messenger ﷺ and following what the Umma agreed upon and its practice continued upon. He has no scope whatever to lay down a law or take a measure that contradicts the clear rulings established by the Book and the Sunna; and that is because — proceeding from the principle of faith in rule belonging to God, glorified and exalted, alone — he is a vicegerent of God in His rule over His servants, so he is subordinate to what He revealed and legislated for him in His firm Book and the purified Sunna of His Messenger. The vicegerent may not oppose the one who appointed him as vicegerent, and the branch is not permitted to contradict its root. Hence it was natural that the first constitution and original source for the imam — while he is in the course of administering his command over the Muslims and performing his functions — be these two primary sources of Islam: the Book of God and the Sunna of His Messenger ﷺ. Hence any command he issues or legislation he enacts that contradicts the Sharia or its fundamentals carries no weight; rather it is the right of the Muslims to try to cancel that by way of the court or other safe constitutional ways.

### 2 – Consultation with the people who loosen and bind

The role of shūrā (consultation) is not confined to its being a means for appointing the imam and designating the caliph only;

[p. 226] rather Islam emphasizes it as an obligation on whoever takes the office of the imamate: that he administer its affairs and perform its obligations on the basis of consultation with the people who loosen and bind — to the extent that the jurists regarded the imam as deserving removal if he did not attend to consultation in the affairs of government. Its standing and importance become clear from God's commanding the Prophet ﷺ with it, saying: **"And consult them in the matter. When you have decided, then rely upon God"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:159] — despite his being infallible from error, guided by revelation directly. So it indicated that it is obligatory, all the more so, on others than him among the commanders of the Muslims and their rulers.

The Prophet ﷺ kept this matter constantly in view in public affairs and the tasks of government, attending to it carefully, as Abū Hurayra, may God be pleased with him, said: "I never saw anyone who consulted his companions more than the Messenger of God ﷺ."¹

ʿAllāma Ibn Kathīr said in the commentary on the aforementioned verse: "And for that reason the Messenger of God ﷺ used to consult his companions in a matter when it occurred, to please their hearts, so that they would be more energetic in what they did."

He listed for that several examples of the Prophet ﷺ's consulting them on the day of Badr about going to the caravan, and fighting the polytheists of Mecca and ransoming their captives in it; and he consulted them at Uḥud about whether to stay in Medina or go out to the enemy; and he consulted them in the Battle of the Confederates about digging the trench; and he said in the story of the Slander: Advise me, O assembly of the Muslims.

He transmitted, attributing it to Aḥmad, from ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Ghanm that the Messenger of God ﷺ said to Abū Bakr and ʿUmar: "If you two agreed in counsel, I would not oppose you."²

ʿAllāma al-Haythamī commented on its chain, saying: "Narrated by Aḥmad, and its men are trustworthy, except that Ibn Ghanm did not hear from the Prophet ﷺ."

But that does not impugn it in anything; for it does not go beyond being mursal [with a missing link], and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Ghanm is a Companion³, and the mursal of a Companion is an argument by consensus.

> ¹ *Musnad Aḥmad*, al-Risāla ed. (31/244), Musnad of the Kufans, hadith of al-Miswar ibn Makhrama and Marwān ibn al-Ḥakam, 18928.
> ² *Tafsīr Ibn Kathīr*, ed. Salāma (2/150).
> ³ *Al-Iṣāba* 4/350, biography no. 5185.

[p. 227] Here too the Sharia has not specified, for the manner of consultation, its people, and whom the imam consults, and what its basic structure will be, a special method that must be taken always and absolutely in every age and city. Rather it entrusted its details also to the people of insight among the Muslims in every age, to choose to act by it a formula that agrees with the requirements of their age and harmonizes with their reality. So it is not necessary that there be a special committee or a council made up of specific, limited members whom the imam is charged with consulting in every matter; rather anything that fulfils its purpose and meets the need is permitted in the Sharia, whatever its form. So a special council for shūrā may be adopted in one age, just as the imam may be left free to choose for it people of insight and opinion in his time without forming a special council. It is also permitted that he consult some at one time and others at another; just as it is permitted that his consultants differ with the difference of the matters on which he consults them: in one matter he consults expert specialists in it, and in another other people who have connection, expertise and skill in it.

In the era of prophethood and the Rightly-Guided Caliphate it was practised with complete simplicity, without there being a parliament or a legislative body as prevails in this age of ours. Rather the caliph used to consult the notables of the people and the eminent among the Muslims in the matters of the Umma and the affairs of government, and would proceed with a matter which they advised him [upon] after his ascertaining and assurance that it represents the social conscience of the Umma. But nevertheless, the founding of a special council for shūrā also falls within its general meaning and its comprehensive Sharia concept.

It is in truth a feature by which the rulings of Islam in the field of politics are distinguished: where it contented itself in it with legislating many principles without specifying their details and particulars; rather it entrusted that to those of opinion and insight among them in every age and city. The wisdom at work behind that is: that Islam is a general and comprehensive religion which all humanity, in all places and times until the Hour comes, has been called to embrace; its teachings must be implemented and its laws are binding on everyone at every time, without confining them to one people to the exclusion of another, nor narrowing their scope to one place to the exclusion of another —

[p. 228] this on one side. On another, it is tangibly witnessed that needs and requirements differ and change with the change of places, times and temperaments. So if the Sharia had legislated concerning it a special method and a specified manner, it would not have been far-fetched that its adoption and acting by it would be, in relation to a society, impossible or nearly so, or contrary to the interests of the Umma in view of the nature of that society and its circumstances and so on; while another Islamic society would be in the most pressing need to be obligated with this specified formula, because of what is feared of it, that leaving it entirely to its own devices in the matter of shūrā would result only in harming its purposes and the interests of the Umma.

The upshot is that the Sharia, has left this door wide open for everyone to enter according to the requirements of his circumstances and the nature of his age, we are nevertheless confident that if the ruler today were entrusted with its matter wholly such that the choice would be his, and the reference to his will in whom he consults in matters and to what extent, that would make us fear that it would drag woes and evils upon the Umma and dangers and many evils concerning its interests. So it appears that it is necessary today that a special council for shūrā be formed whom the imam is charged with consulting. It has details that will come shortly, God willing.

### 3 – The powers and competences of the shūrā

The second important issue in this discussion revolves around the extent of the competences of the shūrā and its powers, and the ruling of what they advised the imam with: is it binding on him to follow it at all times, or is it permissible for him to disagree with it? In the form of disagreement, by which of the two matters does he judge: by the counsel of the people who loosen and bind, or by his own opinion opposing them? The issue is of considerable importance in the discussion of Islamic politics; we summarize for you its explanation in what follows.

The position which the generality of the jurists chose, and especially those of them who attend to political jurisprudence, is that obeying the shūrā is not obligatory on the imam; for its import and meaning are nothing but that all the aspects of the issue be uncovered before the imam, and all its dimensions become clear such that no hidden aspect of it is concealed from him nor does a far corner of it remain, so that he becomes acquainted with all

[p. 229] the possible courses he could take in undertaking a matter, with their proofs and details, from the mouths of their proponents, and so arrive at the soundest and most correct decision — a decision in which he is independent, not necessarily bound by the shūrā nor obliged to submit to what they said.

This is what the majority of the jurists held: that the imam has the choice in choosing whatever saying or opinion he wishes if he saw it as the strongest of them in argument and the clearest of them in proof, even if it is an opinion which the minority of them saw, or a saying which none of them held. They have in that the following arguments:

**First**, the saying of God, glorified and exalted: **"And consult them in the matter. When you have decided, then rely upon God"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:159] — since He did not restrict in it the decision on the matter in which consultation has taken place to its being in accordance with the shūrā and its agreement entirely.

**Second**, the practice of the Prophet ﷺ and his Rightly-Guided Caliphs: that they were not committed to the matter of the shūrā in every case; rather it is established from them that they decided by a matter contrary to what the majority of those consulted [advised]. Thus the Prophet ﷺ consulted his companions concerning the captives of Badr: ʿUmar advised him to kill them, and Ibn Rawāḥa to kill and burn them, and Abū Bakr to spare them; and the people divided in that into two groups: people said: he will take the saying of Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him, and people said: he will take the saying of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him.¹

So the Messenger of God ﷺ decided by the opinion of Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, may God be pleased with him, and ordered taking the ransom from them, even though it was not — as it appears — the opinion of two thirds of his companions. Even if the Qur'an reproached [him] for that, its reproach was not for rejecting the opinion of the majority, but because God was not pleased with releasing them at that time.

Likewise Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq's dispatching of the army of Usāma was, it appears, contrary to the opinion of the majority at that time. These, then, are the arguments of those who hold that the imam is not bound to choosing the saying of the people of the shūrā in every case; rather he is charged with sifting the opinions and probabilities and following the strength of the argument and the proof.

While the others relied on [the fact] that consultation is only completed, and its benefit only obtained, if we obligated the imam to choose the saying on which the people of the shūrā agreed definitively, without there being for him room for deviating from it. It is also supported by a hadith which ʿAllāma Ibn Kathīr mentioned in the commentary on this verse, attributing it to Ibn Mardawayh from ʿAlī, may God be pleased with him, that he said: the Messenger of God ﷺ was asked about the decision (*al-ʿazm*); he said: "Consulting the people of opinion, then following them."²

So this hadith — after granting its soundness and its freedom from what impugns it — indicates that the meaning which the people of the first position mentioned for the decision in His words (*then when you have decided*) — that in his ruling the imam follows only the strength of the argument and the proof, not the saying of the people of the shūrā — is not definite and decisive; rather it also admits that its meaning be: that the imam decide on a matter on which the people of the shūrā have agreed, and not turn away from what they unanimously agreed upon. Indeed, if the chain of this hadith were sound, this would be its definite meaning, not merely a possibility.

Al-Ḥāfiẓ Ibn Kathīr here also brought from Imam Aḥmad what we mentioned earlier, that the Prophet ﷺ said to Abū Bakr and ʿUmar, may God be pleased with them both: "If you two agreed in counsel, I would not oppose you."

In addition to that, the Prophet ﷺ himself left his opinion in many places for the opinion of the majority of the Companions, may God be pleased with them, teaching the Umma to take consultation with regard; for if the Prophet ﷺ, though guided by revelation, set aside his opinion and acted on the shūrā, then the other rulers and commanders of the Muslims are all the more obliged to do so.

[p. 232] An example of that is what happened in the Battle of Uḥud: that the Prophet ﷺ consulted his companions whether to go out to Quraysh or stay in Medina and fight them in the streets; and his opinion was that they not go out of Medina (and the text of Ibn Hishām: "And the Messenger of God ﷺ disliked going out").¹

But many of the Companions, and especially the young among them, insisted on going out; so he resolved on going out, leaving his opinion for their opinion.

Al-Dārimī narrated in his *Sunan* a hadith in which Maymūn ibn Mihrān spoke at length about the conduct of Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him, in what came to him of disputes; and among what came in it: "So if it was too difficult for him to find in it a Sunna from the Messenger of God ﷺ, he gathered the heads of the people and their best and consulted them; and if their opinion agreed on a matter, he decided by it"² — which clearly indicates that he used to decide by a matter on which the opinion of the people of the shūrā agreed.

It does not contradict what we presented of his dispatching the expedition of Usāma despite the opposition of most of the Companions to him; for their disagreement was in the stage of consultation only. As for after they saw his steadfastness in his position and his firmness in his opinion with all resolve and strength, it was not long before they were all convinced by it; and it was not long before their opinion turned to his opinion, as is narrated from ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, that he said in the issue of fighting those who refused zakat — and he had at first seen in it an opinion other than the opinion of Abū Bakr: "By God, it was nothing but that God had opened the breast of Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him, and I knew that it was the truth."³

So what Abū Bakr carried out was not his opinion alone; rather it became in the end the opinion of all after their being convinced by it when they saw his steadfastness and firmness upon it. So it is not correct to argue by it that he was despotic in his opinion against the opinion of the Companions, and especially the opinion of the people of the shūrā among them.

Here we have finished the brief explanation of the two positions and their proofs in this issue; we follow it with explaining what is preponderant of them, so we say: what appears to us is that the principle is what the people of the position

For the word "*al-shūrā*" itself indicates that the saying of the adviser is not necessarily binding on the one consulted; and it is so self-evident that even a slave-woman of the Muslims, Barīra, may God be pleased with her, grasped the difference between it and a command: when the Prophet ﷺ asked her to go back to Mughīth — after she had left him by exercising the option she gained on her emancipation — she asked him: "Are you commanding me?" So she refused to accept his intercession, saying: "I have no need of him."¹ And the Prophet ﷺ did not reproach her; which established it as a Sharia rule that intercession does not have to be accepted by the one to whom intercession is made.

The gist of the words: that God commanded the commander with consultation by the text of the Noble Qur'an, and it was established from this hadith that counsel by its nature requires that it not be binding such that he must accept it; rather its role lies in enabling the imam to know all the dimensions of the issue and its aspects and its probabilities, so that he undertakes, consequently, sifting them and choosing what he sees as soundest and most correct in view of the strength of its argument and the firmness of its proof — even if it was the opinion of the minority, or was not the opinion of any of them definitively and at all. Here too it is necessary to note several points:

**The first:** what we mentioned of not regarding the saying of the shūrā as binding is only in relation to the imam of the Muslims and their commander who was chosen by the people who loosen and bind among them on the basis of his qualification for the office of the imamate by combining its qualities and the presence of its conditions; and not for a democratic ruler bare of those qualities and conditions, whom obtaining the majority of votes enabled to ascend the throne of authority with defects and deficiency in his character and his conduct. Such a ruler is outside the scope of our discussion here, which concerns permitting the imam to carry out matters by his opinion alone, even at the cost of opposing a matter on which the opinion of the people of the shūrā has agreed.

**Second:** the imam of the Muslims and their caliph is not an absolute ruler, despotic in his opinion and independent

[p. 234] in his command like kings and emperors; rather he is a believer in the principle of rule belonging to God, so he is subject to His fixed rulings and His revealed laws, restricted necessarily to following His Book and the Sunna of His Messenger ﷺ and the way of the believers. All this forms an impregnable barrier for him that blocks him from deciding by a matter that contradicts these original sources of reference or opposes them in anything.

Since undertaking justice and judging among people with equity is among the totality of the rulings which those sources have strongly emphasized, if the imam transgressed it and ruled with tyranny, that would not be accepted from him; because it is disobedience to the Creator in which obedience to a creature, whoever he may be, is not obligatory. Then it is possible to cancel and nullify it by way of the judiciary and the court. If the imam persisted in his errors without desisting from what he does, he would be a transgressor (*fāsiq*) deserving his removal from this grave office. So, then, not obligating him to choose the saying of the people of the shūrā does not mean that he has become an absolute ruler and despotic in his opinion; rather he is restricted by following the rulings of the Book and the Sunna. In the form of his not fulfilling this commitment and his opposing them or one of them in anything, his subjects may sue him, and then remove him and turn him from this office of his.

The upshot is that what we said of not obligating him with the saying of the shūrā — its scope is limited only to his consultation of the people who loosen and bind in the permissible matters in which Islam has not decided by a ruling. So in these alone he is permitted to depart from the opinion of the people of the shūrā in favour of what his own opinion settles upon as a result of the strength of its argument and its proof. This is what distinguishes him from kings and emperors, since they are so independent and despotic that they do not see anything else — not even divine revelation and heavenly law — above them in sovereignty and authority!

**Third:** since among the most important things that are obligatory on the imam is consultation in the general matters of the Umma, and especially in its political affairs — and the shūrā is only completed if the imam attended it and listened to what is presented and discussed in it of opinions and their proofs, not biased towards one side or a special opinion — it is therefore not correct what has prevailed today: that the shūrā takes place in the absence of the imam, and what was decided in it of opinion is only presented to him; and he cancels it with a single stroke of the pen, without looking closely at what

[p. 235] went on in it and without deliberation in what was presented to him.

In sum: the principle is permitting the imam who combines the conditions of the imamate and its qualities to disagree with the people of the shūrā by his special opinion and to decide by other than what their opinion agreed on — on condition that it be within the scope of these three points and with their observance.

On another side, it is permitted also that the imam and caliph be restricted — in special exceptional cases and circumstances — to taking the opinion of the shūrā within the limits of a special scope. The reason for that is that rarely is there found among the Muslims one who combines in completeness and perfection those conditions and qualities necessary for the imamate in the Sharia, because of the weakness of the religious motive in them and the decline of their state in attending to religious matters — which compelled the scholars to lighten the conditions of probity until its concept among them was confined to a person's good deeds being predominant over his bad deeds only! For witnesses, strict and severe conditions were originally stipulated; then when Imam Abū Yūsuf took the judgeship and found that a witness meeting those conditions had become all but impossible to find, he relaxed them, to the point of accepting the testimony of the transgressor if he had manly virtue (*murūʾa*) that deters him from lying. Likewise among the conditions of the imamate first was that he had reached in knowledge the level of ijtihad; then when attaining this became far harder than before, the condition was relaxed, and it ceased to be a condition for the imamate. So it became possible for someone who is not a mujtahid scholar to take up the reins of the imamate. It is entirely unreasonable that such an imam — not a mujtahid, and not meeting the other conditions of the imamate — be given the powers and authority that the Rightly-Guided Caliphs had — among them independence of opinion and rejection of the saying of the people of the shūrā.

So in such a case there does not appear any harm in choosing the second saying and not permitting the imam to reject the saying of the people of the shūrā definitively and decisively; rather, given the lack of confidence in his expertise and trustworthiness, he should be bound to adopt the view on which the shūrā council has agreed, without there being for him room for opposing it or deviating from it.

There is a formula which can be chosen to define the scope of restricting him in this regard, namely that

[p. 236] rulings and commands are of two kinds: among them what is like legislation and law-making, whose nature is that as soon as they are issued they take the character of law; and among them what are commands related to administrative and executive affairs. As for the first part, it has been established concerning it definitively that the imam is never allowed to legislate what contradicts the Sharia or opposes it in anything. As for permissible matters (*mubāḥāt*), in which the Sharia has left the door of legislation open to the imam, he can be bound in these to follow the opinion of the people of the shūrā.

As for executive and administrative commands, if we charged him in all of them with following the opinion of the shūrā council, that would threaten to obstruct the administration of the helm of government entirely; so here the original principle applies: the imam may follow his own opinion, without being bound at all to follow the opinion of the people of the shūrā.

An example of that is "the emergency tax" (*al-ḍarība al-nāʾiba*): the jurists permitted the government to impose a tax on the subjects if the public treasury fell short of the expenditures necessary for the state, or if a calamity befell the Muslims. The method of that is that the imam put the draft decision concerning it before the shūrā council; so if it did not agree with him on it and did not see a pressing need justifying burdening the subjects with taxes, then the imam may never impose the tax as long as he has not obtained the agreement of the people of the shūrā on it. The task of specifying the matters in which he is bound by the opinion of the shūrā council, and those in which he is not, can be entrusted to the constitution of the Islamic state today.
