## Chapter Four: Examples of Democratic Systems of Government

### Introduction

[p. 123] Having finished introducing the constitution and its contents in a brief glance, we follow it with a look at the forms that democracy has assumed in its actual application in various states of the world. We single out among them four states that have played a tangible role in drawing the picture of democracy and strengthening its principles and supporting its ideals, so that their systems are regarded as the most solid and well-established systems of democracy, namely: Britain, America, France and Switzerland.

### The parliamentary system in Britain

Since we, the sons of the Indian subcontinent, spent a period of time under the control of British colonialism and in the shadow of subjection and servitude to it, British principles became rooted in our minds, and hence we began trying to follow its method, imitate its model and follow in its footsteps. Proceeding from this, and as an imitation of the British system of democracy (called the parliamentary system: Parliamentary System), the parliamentary system has also become widespread among us, and our constitution was laid down along the lines of the constitution followed in the parliamentary system.

By the parliamentary system is meant: the system of government that regards parliament as the sole supreme power in whose hands lies actual rule of the country. [Such that the two powers, executive and legislative, are intertwined in such a system: the head of government is, by the nature of the case, both the executive head and the chief of the parliamentarians alike.]

The researcher never ceases to wonder when he sees, on the one hand, that the theory of "separation of powers" — which requires separation between the two powers, legislative and executive — is one of the most important principles of democracy, and sees, on the other hand, their fusion in a state that has a leading position in democracy and in founding its structure and strengthening its principles, Britain! For parliament, which represents the legislative power, in reality performs the task of enacting laws, just as it is what forms the executive power [p. 124] and possesses actual rule in the state, through its forming the government and appointing the council of ministers and its head first, and its holding the authority to remove him from the presidency of the government and dismiss his ministers whenever it wishes and wants second. So, from the point of view of the unity of the source of these two powers, it can rightly be said that they have intertwined and actually united — which amounts — despite the separation of the judicial power from them — to demolishing and undermining the democratic principle concerned with the separation of powers.

The parliamentary system is built on the theory of separation between the head of government and the head of state. In Britain the head of state is a king or queen, and the government is headed by the prime minister. The element at work behind this theory separating the two heads is: that the head of government is usually from the political party with the majority in parliament; so he represents his own party and implements its particular policies and programmes; whereas the head of state does not belong to a political party, so he does not represent a particular faction nor a defined party nor take its side; rather he represents the whole people, and hence he undertakes to supervise the government and urge it to implement the plans and policies aimed at taking account of the whole people impartially, away from political affiliation, and offers it advice and counsel if he sees a need for it. If the term of the ruling party ends, he directs the invitation to form the government to the party that follows it in terms of seats in parliament. To this the functions of the head of state are limited.

Although the system of government existing in the United Kingdom is the constitutional monarchy headed by the king or queen, it possesses no actual authority except offering advice and counsel to the government that the policies it wants to implement are not, for example, in the interest of the people and the state. The saying of Queen Victoria became famous in this regard, addressing the prime minister: "Go ahead with what seems right to you; I will sign it even against my will — except that I want to alert you that this decision is not right."

The system of this monarchy in Britain is based on inheritance, not election. Some may have criticized this system on the grounds that it is unnecessary after authority has been transferred entirely to parliament. But [p. 125] it is the habit of the English not to touch the existence of their organizations and their ancient circles as long as they do not form an obstacle in the way of their progress. (From this we see Europe almost devoid of towering buildings in the manner of the American style.) So just as they left the "Big Ben" clock as it was, since it does not stand in the way of their progress, likewise they left the queen and the monarchy as they were; for it has never happened within three centuries that the queen refused to sign one of the decisions of parliament, in keeping with the prevailing custom among them — otherwise there is no clause in their constitution obliging the queen to it and compelling her to it. So since they were safe from the queen's opposition and her lack of interference in their affairs, they regarded it as not harmful and kept its system as it was. Hence the monarchical system does not harm them in anything except the meagre percentage (1%) of the budget the queen costs them; but they do not regard it as anything in comparison with the opportunities to benefit from the wisdom and counsel that the queen offers them, by virtue of her experience and her being adorned with the experiences she has acquired after long occupation of her office and exercise of its functions. From this they revere the queen and regard her as their elder who strokes their heads with the hand of affection and tenderness.

It is curious to mention that the monarchy, although deprived of any authority in rule and the system, yet all the matters concluded in the kingdom and the decisions taken in it are taken only in the name of the queen and on behalf of her government. So every law that is legislated and implemented in the kingdom must be legislated and implemented in the name of "the British Crown" (which is a name for the aforementioned monarchical system). The queen used in the past to undertake herself the signing of the law so that it became law; but now she has appointed a representative for herself to undertake the task of signing on her behalf, and at the end of every law he signs is written: "This law has been issued by order of the Crown of Britain [the British royal throne]."

This organization enjoys great sanctity among them, to the point that it has become a current proverb that "the king of Britain does not die" — meaning by the king here: the aforementioned monarchical system — just as their saying in this regard has become famous: "The king is dead, long live the king." The first means the departed king, and the second refers to the monarchical system.

[p. 126] This organization (the British Crown) enjoys many ceremonial entitlements, such as presiding over celebrations and occasions, corresponding with the heads of the states subordinate to the kingdom, and other symbolic, ceremonial matters that have no connection with authority and actual rule.

### The British Parliament

The representative authority in Britain consists of two chambers, one called "the House of Commons" (House of Commons) and the other "the House of Lords" (House of Lords). As for the House of Commons, it resembles our National Assembly in Pakistan, whose members are elected on the basis of voting by every citizen who has reached the age of 18 years, and which undertakes to elect the executive head of the kingdom from among its members. Its method is that the queen asks the head of the party with the majority in the representative chamber to undertake the formation of the government; if he obtains the confidence of the majority of the House of Commons he becomes prime minister. Then he appoints for himself ministers of both types: the pure minister and the minister who is also a member of the council of ministers. So he appoints 70 ministers, and the council of ministers is formed from 20 or 25 of them. The council of ministers is what determines government policy in general and takes most government decisions, and its members are like the right arm of the prime minister in running the wheel of government and managing its affairs, in addition to the administration of the government departments whose ministries have been entrusted to them. From this each of them has the right to give his opinion on any matter of government affairs, whether inside the framework of his ministry or outside it.

As for the other ministers, they have no share in government decisions; rather their functions and responsibilities are confined to what has been entrusted to them of government departments, and from this they are not allowed to attend the meetings of the council of ministers (unless one of them is sent a special invitation). (This category of ministers is called among us the federal minister.)

When the Speaker of the House of Commons has been elected, he is the one who presides over meetings in the British representative chamber, where the members of the ruling party sit on his right and on his left [p. 127] the members of the opposition. (From this arose the naming of political parties as right and left [the right wing and the left wing]. It happened at the beginning that the parties calling for capitalism were in government, and so they filled the seats on the right of the Speaker of the chamber, while the parties calling for communism were in opposition, and so they sat on his left. So capitalist parties were named "right-wing parties" even if they were in opposition, and communist parties "left-wing parties" even if they became the government. The term has developed today, so that the name "right-wing party" is applied to the party that calls for the old governmental order, while "left-wing" is applied to the party that opposes the old order and supports change in the prevailing order.)

### Legislation in the British system

The House of Commons occupies a high position in legislation and law-making. The order of that is that the government presents a draft law in it, which then passes through three readings:

(1) The first reading: in this round the text of the law is read out before the members of the chamber so that they may become acquainted with it, and also so that they may express their general opinions on it.

(2) The second reading: after initial approval of the draft is obtained, in the second stage it is referred to committees, which study it professionally, show its aspects of correctness and weakness, and propose the amendments necessary to reform it. Among these committees: the standing committee (Standing committee) and the select committee (Select committee). These committees prepare reports on the draft and present them to the chamber, whose members study it to propose amendment to it in the light of those reports; whatever a member proposes is presented in the chamber for voting, and thus the draft of the bill is ratified — amended or unamended — according to the opinion of the majority of the chamber.

(3) The third reading: in this round only the correction of verbal errors in the draft is allowed, and no other amendment is allowed besides that; then the bill is finally decided in this [p. 128] stage (the stage of the third reading). (It should be known that the law, as long as it is under discussion in the representative chamber, is called a bill (Bill), and after being decided by the chamber it becomes: the law (Act).)

At any rate, after the bill has passed through those three stages in the House of Commons, it is referred to the House of Lords.

### The House of Lords

It is one of the two chambers entrusted with the task of enacting laws in the parliament of Britain, and its system and powers are among the things that arouse wonder and astonishment in the mind of the researcher. For a large number of its members are chosen by inheritance: if a person is made a member of it, this membership extends in his family for long centuries, the successor in it becoming a member in place of the predecessor, and so on generation after generation, like the monarchy established among them on the basis of inheritance. The one who obtains it in the family of the deceased member is the one who deserves his inheritance from the Christian point of view; and since the woman does not inherit anything among the Christians, she cannot obtain this membership on the basis of inheritance.

Part of this chamber consists of nobles whom the queen chooses for this membership, and they are called "Peer" (meaning: the nobles), since the queen among them enjoys the authority to choose members of this chamber whatever their number. Usually those who enjoy this right are men who have distinguished efforts in the fields of business, civil service and the like, and the word (Technocrat) is applied to them. They also especially attract men who are specialists in the field of law, because of the pressing need for them, since this chamber is regarded as equivalent to the final court of appeal as well. These two types of members remain members for life, and proceeding from this concept it is said: "This chamber has an entrance and no exit" — that is, there are ways to enter it, but there is no way to get out of it!

Its original function was to review the laws approved by the House of Commons: if it approved them they were implemented; otherwise they were returned to the House of Commons a second time to review and reconsider them, and it was not possible to implement a law as long as it had not been approved by the House of Lords. In this regard [p. 129] a curious story occurred, namely that the queen sometimes used her powers in choosing the members of this chamber as a weapon to pressure them: if the House of Lords postponed approving a law that she wanted to hasten in implementing, she would threaten them by adding other members equal to them in number, since there was no numerical restriction on her regarding the choice of members. She would say: "If you do not approve this law I will add to the chamber other people equal in number to you, who will undertake to approve this law."

Accordingly, this is regarded as a strange organization with no parallel in the world, and it is said that a number of the nobles have never attended the meetings of the chamber. It had at first broad powers almost equal to the powers of the House of Commons, but they began to be stripped from it one after another, until the matter came to the shrinking of its powers, which made it a symbolic, ceremonial chamber. At first it was able to postpone a law approved by the House of Commons; but today it has been deprived of that — rather the law is regarded as automatically in force one year after it is put before the House of Lords.

Usually what is criticized about this chamber is that it has become a burden on the state without having any usefulness or benefit, so it ought to be ended and its system dissolved. But the British people still keep it for the sake of benefiting from the experiences of its members and the studies they present in its meetings. For these people find opportunities available for study and reading, then they present the result of their wide reading and the essence of their deep study in the discussions that take place in the meetings of the chamber about some subject. And there is no doubt that the studies of this chamber usually distinguish themselves with an excellent scholarly and literary standing.

What is striking in the political system of Britain is that it has no written constitution; rather it follows a set of customs, habits and beliefs. Let us give an example of that with the queen's signing of a law approved by the House of Commons: there is no clause in the constitution obliging her to it; but the traditions and customs have proceeded thus, so the queen is committed to signing every law approved by the House of Commons. Likewise there is no text in the constitution obliging the queen to ask for the formation of the government [p. 130] from the head of the majority party in the representative chamber; but she commits to that according to the customs and traditions that have proceeded, continued and been followed in practice. These customs and traditions have taken on a constitutional character, and their standing in the kingdom does not fall short of the standing of any constitution in any state; they are the matters on the basis of which the courts decide.

In sum: Britain has no written constitution like other states, apart from some constitutional laws approved by parliament, such as the Bill of Rights (Bill of Rights). Since it has no constitution, and on the other hand parliament enjoys extremely wide powers, no law approved by parliament or measure taken by parliament can be challenged. Accordingly, if parliament approved a law that allows the prime minister to detain someone and arrest him without needing to explain its justification, it is not possible to object to this law in court on the argument that it contradicts fundamental rights; rather the judge himself is compelled to rule by it, by virtue of the subjection of the judiciary among them to the laws of parliament. This is entirely the opposite of what is in America, since it allows every citizen to challenge any law he regards as contrary to human rights. But despite that, it has never happened that the British parliament implemented a law the people regarded as a violation of civil rights, and therefore demanded its cancellation on that basis¹ — whereas America has encountered that more than once, as parliament issued a law the people opposed in the Supreme Court on the basis of its contradicting civil rights, and so the court proceeded to cancel and nullify it.

> ¹ But this is a story of the past. Today, and specifically after the incident of 11 September 2001, the parliament of Britain issued several laws that allowed the government to arrest and detain a person on the basis of an accusation directed at him concerning terrorism.

The British representative chamber has continued to be dominated by two parties (the Conservative Party and the Labour Party), one of which forms the government while the other forms the main opposition. The opposition between them has continued for a long period: if the Labour Party is in government, the Conservative Party must occupy the seats of the opposition, and conversely, if the Liberal Party [sic] were [in government], it would in turn undertake its opposition. But all this [takes place] in the shadow of observing the established traditions and continuing customs [p. 131] and the principles of respect for others and tolerance, such that neither of the two sides touches them with violation however severe the opposition becomes and however many the conflicts. From this, Britain has the right to be regarded as the pioneer of the parliamentary system and the leader of the world in it.

Many states have taken to following the parliamentary system, and it is the system preferred by the political parties among us in Pakistan, which seek to imitate Britain in it — despite its being surrounded by a series of contradictions and inconsistencies. Among them is that it grants the opportunity to form the government to the political party that has obtained the confidence of the majority of members in parliament; and this confidence can be obtained only in one of these two ways: that the party wins the majority of seats in parliament, i.e. a percentage of 51% and above. In such a form this party must form that government. This is the case in which the two powers, legislative and executive, are intertwined, since parliament legislates while its members representing the majority have become part of the executive power as well, which includes some of those members. And when these two powers have intertwined and merged, it follows from that that parliament's undertaking to endorse government policies and its programmes, whatever they may be — since parliament is the one that has the right to hold the ruling party to account and review its policies; and since the ruling party has become the voice that is heard and the word that is accepted in parliament by virtue of its having obtained the majority of its seats — it was natural that it should endorse the policies its political party implements, and not form an obstacle to it nor stand as a barrier before it in the way of achieving them. Thus there remains no path that can be followed to change government policies and its programmes.

The second form: that none of the political parties obtains the majority of votes or seats that would enable it to form the government, such that no party has been able to obtain 51% of the seats. Then the coalition government (Coalition Government) is formed, meaning that the dominant party in parliament unites with other political parties or independent members who do not belong to any political party. The attempts at union to form the coalition government sometimes result in astonishing situations, where [p. 132] two parties unite that differ vastly in policies, programmes, visions and orientations, after their failure to obtain the majority of seats; so they have no choice but to unite to form the government. How can they accompany each other when one of them faces east while the other has headed west?! Even if they make peace on mutual support and solidarity between them, this too does not result in great benefit and cannot be sustained for a long period, by virtue of the difference of the policies, programmes and plans they follow. So no sufficient solution can be found in such a case except in one of two forms: either that one of them gives up its policies for the interests of the other, or that it proceeds on the principle of "take some and leave some", such that each of them gives up some of its demands. Thus, usually, the wheel of the coalition government cannot be run except by sacrificing some principles and giving up some policies and programmes. Proceeding from this, forming the coalition government takes a long time, and after the process of formation it still remains on the verge of falling and dissolving, as a series of conspiracies begins in its wake to dismantle this government and seek to create a gap between the two parties. Take Italy, for example: 300 governments were formed in it in the period between 1954 and our day [sic], and the reason for that is the change in the state of confidence in parliament over the days and limited periods, alongside the failure of political parties to win the majority of seats. Conditions with them deteriorated to the point that a government had hardly been formed before the movement of withdrawal of confidence began to confront it — which is regarded as sufficient to eliminate the prime minister's government even if it is not based on a serious and acceptable accusation. Nothing happened except that the members' wish turned to another person, so they presented a decision of withdrawal of confidence from the prime minister, which ultimately led to toppling his system and overthrowing his government.

But Britain has been able to continue the parliamentary system with all precision and solidity, owing to the factor of awareness and political insight with which the British people was adorned, which we can appreciate through the fact that Britain has no written constitution; its affairs are run only according to customs and habits, yet there is no conception there of violating those customs and contradicting them!

### The presidential system in America

In contrast to the parliamentary system in Britain, America follows the presidential system, which differs in [p. 133] its form, its characteristics and its features greatly from the parliamentary system. The first difference between the systems of the two states lies in the fact that while the constitution of America is federal, Britain has a unitary one. And second, Britain does not give the principle of separation of powers — which is regarded as one of the most important characteristics of democracy — its due, as we discussed in detail a little earlier: that the two powers, legislative and executive, are intertwined in it to some extent; while America still holds it fast with its molars, since it classified the powers into three powers, each power regarded as entirely independent of the other two powers. The three powers are: the executive power, headed by the head of state; the legislative power, represented by Congress, which consists of two chambers: (1) the House of Representatives "House of Representatives" (2) and the Senate (Senate); and the third power: the judicial power, entirely independent of the first two powers.

The head of state is the head of the executive apparatus, and the legislature or parliament does not elect him; rather he is elected by a system surrounded by much ambiguity and complexity. First, people in each state elect several members forming a body known as the electoral body (Electoral College), which is made up of representatives of each state; the number of members of the electoral body for each state is equal to the number of that state's representatives in Congress. (America is regarded as a federal state comprising fifty-two [sic] states, so there must be representation of each state in the election of its president, this being one thing; and because the American president actually enjoys extremely important powers, which naturally requires that his election not be by the opinion of every Tom, Dick and Harry from the rabble and riff-raff of the people, but that he be elected by men with qualifications and virtuous competences from each state — from this source the idea of multi-stage election in appointing the president of America emerged.)

It is stipulated for whoever stands for the office of the American presidency that he be an American citizen, aged 35 years, who has lived in America for at least 14 years. When [p. 134] such a man is elected as head of state, all executive responsibilities fall on his shoulders by virtue of his being head of the executive apparatus, and hence he enjoys wide powers. He appoints the council of ministers — in contrast to what passed in the British system, that the party winning the majority of seats forms the government and appoints the prime minister, who appoints the council of ministers from among the members of parliament. The presidential system does not require that a member of the council of ministers be a member of Congress as well; rather the president appoints for his council of ministers whom he wishes, whether a member of Congress or not. That person then becomes director of the government department whose ministry has been entrusted to him. These are not called ministers among them; they only call them secretaries: among them the secretary of the interior, another of foreign affairs, another of the treasury and transport and others. The secretaries of these government departments in America are equivalent to ministers in other states, and the collection of these is called "the council of ministers" [the Cabinet], without its having those powers it enjoys in the parliamentary system, as the ruling body that runs most government policies and implements its laws by consultation among its members. Rather its standing in the presidential system does not go beyond being equivalent to a consultative council for the president, while the president has the right to exercise the right of veto (Veto power) over its decisions, and to take the decision by his opinion alone — meaning that the council of ministers, if it unanimously agreed on an executive matter contrary to the opinion of the president, the president may reject their opinion and rule by his opinion alone. It is thus established that the president has the supreme authority in the executive affairs of the state, and he is the one who appoints the leaders and senior officials in the civil and military fields; but in enacting laws and policies he needs to present them to Congress and obtain its ratification of them.

The American president is regarded as head of both the state and the executive apparatus, in contrast to the parliamentary system concerned with distinguishing between the presidency of the state — which is occupied by the head of state — and the presidency of the government — which is occupied by the prime minister.

The manner of removing the president and the prime minister also differs in the two systems, parliamentary and presidential. [p. 135] In the parliamentary system the prime minister is removed if the parliament agrees by majority on a decision of withdrawal of confidence from him. Britain has previously seen that if the prime minister proposed a bill to the representative chamber and did not succeed in obtaining its agreement to it, but the majority of the chamber rejected it, his failure is equivalent among them to the withdrawal of the members' confidence in him. Hence it is incumbent on him from the moral point of view to resign from his office; if he does, [well and good]; otherwise he and his council of ministers are regarded following that as removed and stripped of the presidency, even if there was no coordinated movement for withdrawal of confidence from him. So it is not necessary for removing him from his office that he have committed a crime or engaged in treason or done anything else; rather merely the majority of members' showing withdrawal of confidence from him, explicitly or implicitly, suffices to topple his system and depose him from the seat of government. The presidential system, by contrast, never rules the president removed merely by Congress deciding to withdraw confidence from him. (For his election did not take place on the basis of the opinion of Congress, but by the direct vote of the people, according to the detail just given.) Rather, the removal of the president among them has a special method, namely that if the president becomes exposed to accusation of negligence, treason or violation of the constitution and the like, then in such a case the constitution allows a movement of impeachment (impeachment) to be undertaken against him. In detail: the majority of the votes of Congress, if they issue a decision that his trial be conducted by the Senate, the Senate undertakes it in its capacity as the supreme court (and in the presence of the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court of the United States as its president). The president is informed of the charges directed at him, and given the opportunity to explain his position on them; and after hearing his position and the testimonies against him, the Senate decides the case: if two thirds of the members of the Senate vote for his conviction, it rules that he be removed from his office; otherwise it declares that the charges directed at him are not correct, and he remains president of the state. One case of impeachment of the president has occurred throughout America's history, and it too did not result in the removal of the president, on the grounds that the charges against him were not correct. There are decisions to which the Senate agreed against the vice-president and judges, so they were removed following that from their positions; as for the head of state, it has never happened that he was removed from his office as a result of charges directed at him. Nixon almost faced this ugly fate had he not submitted his resignation himself.

[p. 136] The upshot of what we have presented is that the administration of the government, the management of foreign policies, and the declaration of war — all these responsibilities are entrusted to the American president, and from this he is regarded as possessing extreme power and might; yet in all his policies and laws he is responsible before Congress, in the sense that he needs, in their implementation, their adoption by Congress.

The legislative power in America (called Congress) is divided into two chambers:

**The subsidiary chamber**, called the House of Representatives (House of Representative), which consists of 435 members, and the representation of these members is subject to the number of inhabitants in each state; they are elected for a period of two years only, and it has the same powers that the lower representative chamber usually has, of imposing taxes and fees and enacting its special laws.

**(2) The upper chamber:** it is the Senate (Senate), which consists of one hundred members. Each state has two members representing it regardless of the size of the state and the number of its inhabitants. The period of service of each member is six years, and they are elected on the basis of voting by every adult citizen. The American system differs greatly from the British system regarding the upper chamber, since the British House of Lords has come to be an organization that no longer has a large role in government or state policies; rather it has become "a discussion forum", to the extent of what some have said about it. The American Senate, by contrast, enjoys wide powers not matched by any other senate in all the states of the world. Usually its members are elected on the basis of their qualifications and abilities, and the general public gives this aspect in electing them more attention than in electing the members of the House of Representatives. In enacting laws it has powers equal to the powers of the lower chamber, except legislation in imposing taxes and arranging the budget, as in other states of the world, where it is established among them that this power is exclusive to the House of Representatives, not the Senate. Many states see no need for presenting the budget to the upper chamber and obtaining its adoption. On the other hand, it is the only body that ratifies the appointment of the ministers and heads of departments whom the head of state recommends for occupying federal leadership positions, civil or military; and [p. 137] no one is officially appointed except after the ratification of the Senate. It is also the only body that approves or rejects all treaties concluded by the United States of America.

There is another important aspect in the American system, namely that while Britain regards parliament as the highest official authority — in the sense that the decisions parliament has agreed to are not subject to any other authority, and no court can review, cancel or nullify that law whatever it may be — in America the courts are regarded as the body that has the authority to consider any legislative works and executive orders and declare their conformity or contradiction with the constitution. This is what is called judicial review (Judicial Review); it is an additional right granted to every citizen to undertake objection to any law issued by Congress in the Supreme Court.

There is another feature by which the American system is distinguished from its British counterpart, and this feature concerns political parties. As you have noticed, political parties have a large role and effective activities in the administration of government in Britain, since the head of the majority party undertakes the formation of the government; whereas in America the president is elected directly, and political parties have no interference in forming the government. So political parties do not have a large role; rather their task is confined to registering candidates and carrying out mass propaganda campaigns for elections. And it is not necessary that the helm of government be steered by the party with the majority in Congress; rather it is possible — and has actually happened — that the president belongs to one political party while the majority of members in Congress are from another party. The president is also not a member of Congress, nor is he obliged to attend its meetings; consultations between them take place by correspondence. But nevertheless, in such a critical situation the divisions and conflicts between the president belonging to a political party other than the party that has obtained the majority in Congress do not rage to a degree that obstructs the course of government or stands as a barrier before its activities.

The reason for that is that the method of the members of Congress and their manner differ entirely from the members of parliament in the parliamentary system. For the member of parliament in the parliamentary system is completely compelled [p. 138] to submit to the policies of his party and its programmes, without deviating from it a hand-span, whether in expressing opinion or casting his vote; whereas the member in Congress can express what is in his conscience and express his opinion even if it departs from the party to which he belongs and its policies. Rather, the representatives link their connection with their electoral districts more than with their political parties; from this they take account of the interests of their districts more than the interests of their parties, and are concerned with the policies they see as in the interest of the state in general, whoever their proposer and presenter may be. From this it is no longer necessary that if the president is not from the party dominant in Congress he will face problems and obstacles, just as there is no conception of opposition like what is prevalent in the parliamentary system.

### The parliamentary system in France

The third system that I would like to undertake a passing look at to introduce is the parliamentary system of France. It has passed before us that the credit for strengthening democratic principles and consolidating its pillars in Europe goes only to France and its revolution arising from the source of the ideas of its three famous philosophers, which first produced the birth of the idea of liberal democracy, and second its consolidation and influence in the other European states. But despite its role, France continued to suffer constitutional conflict for a long period, which resulted in the laying down of many constitutions over short periods. The reason for that was that at first they disputed over the type of constitution — whether it should be parliamentary or presidential — until their choice settled on the parliamentary constitution like that in force in Britain. This account goes back to before 1958 CE: all executive authority was in the hands of the prime minister, while the head of state's presidency was only symbolic and ceremonial, like the monarchy in Britain, and lacked authority and powers to the extent that he no longer could use his powers in which he was regarded as independent without obtaining the attesting signature of the prime minister or one of the ministers. From this it became famous in describing him that he needed attestation in everything except the request for his resignation — meaning that he could not execute anything without [p. 139] its being attested by the signature of the prime minister or one of the ministers, except his resignation: it took effect merely by the executive head's acceptance of it, even if not accompanied by an attesting signature.¹ Just as another anecdote became famous: that the queen in Britain, even if she had no authority, at least enjoys the monarchy; and the president of America enjoys the presidency without the monarchy; while the president of France is deprived of both: he neither enjoys wide authority like his American counterpart nor enjoys the privileges of monarchy as the queen in Britain has.

> ¹ It is the system that Zulfiqar Ali Bhutto followed in Pakistan following his assumption of the reins of power there in 1973; the president at the time was Chaudhry Fazal Elahi.

At any rate, this parliamentary system prevailed in France for a long period. But it is known that the parliamentary system is built on the distinction between the presidency of the state and the presidency of the government; from this it can be applied in a good manner only if the powers and authorities of both presidents are balanced. If one of them possesses the authorities and uses the powers on a wide scale while the other is deprived of them except for a minimum, that results only in raging partisanships and continuing rifts between the two offices. The situation in France was like that, until de Gaulle took up the reins of the presidency of the state, and saw that the source of anarchy and constitutional collapse in the state was nothing but the lack of balance between the powers of the two presidents, since the presidency of the state had remained a merely ceremonial post, and the president had only to see what the prime minister implemented of policies without criticizing or opposing them. So the idea of laying down a new constitution was born in his mind, and he actually undertook it in 1958, striving with his effort to create balance between the two powers. Instead of presenting his project in the National Assembly, he put it before the people for a general referendum, and its results resulted in its obtaining popularity on a wide and great scale. It has remained the constitution in force in the state from 1958 until this day of ours, and is called the constitution of balance and equilibrium (Check and Balence).

He included in it something of the merits of both systems, presidential and parliamentary: in its origin and spirit it is a parliamentary constitution, emphasizing that the government is formed by the political party with the majority in [p. 140] parliament; but it did not leave the president neglected and abandoned either; rather it granted him something of powers and authorities too. First, it established concerning him that he is regarded as head of the state and of the states subordinate to it (which are the African states that France occupied, and Algeria and many territories were of this type; France later withdrew from them, but some of them are still under its control and occupation, and hence they are subject to its system and its constitution), and the president of the Republic is elected by the members of parliament. Among the powers the new constitution granted him is that he presides over the meetings of the council of ministers. From all this he came to have interference and influence in running the executive apparatus as well.

On the other hand, if he saw in a law issued by parliament a defect or deficiency, and then felt the need to amend it, he can send it again to parliament for review, just as he can present it to the Constitutional Council to undertake examining the law and declare the extent of its conformity or non-conformity with the spirit of the constitution.

The president has also been delegated authority in appointing the commanders of the military forces and some heads of civil government departments, without his needing in that the counsel of the executive head; and he was also granted the right to dissolve the National Assembly if he saw it deviating from the constitution — and if he consulted the executive head in that, that is his right, but his counsel is not binding on him. He was also granted other supreme powers: if the Republic is struck by a state of emergency and exposed to danger to its security and stability, he may take whatever steps to resist that situation he sees as appropriate and sufficient to guarantee the peace of the Republic.

These, then, are the differences between the systems of Britain and France, and between the French presidential system and its counterpart in other states, embodied in delegating many executive matters to the president of the Republic. As for other matters relating to politics and government, the system of France in them resembles the systems of other states: the legislature consists of two chambers, and between the two chambers are the same ties and connections as between them in other states.

### The Swiss system of government

[p. 141] The fourth state whose system of government I wanted to acquaint you with is Switzerland, whose system of government differs from the systems of all states of the world entirely. Researchers and authors in political science have declared that democracy in all its meanings and import is in force only in Switzerland. It is a state small in area, perhaps not exceeding the area of the province of Sindh in Pakistan, and its inhabitants number no more than five to six million souls. This small state is divided administratively into 22 units, each called a canton (Canton), and each canton enjoys self-government, with its own local assembly and special council of ministers.

The inhabitants of this small state belong to three nations (*aqwām*): German (71%), French (21%) and Italian (6%). As for religion, 57% belong to the Roman Catholic Church, 41% to the Protestant Church, and 0.5% are Jews. From this its inhabitants differ from one another in every way in terms of ethnicities, religions and languages; but they did not divide the cantons on the basis of language, ethnicity or religion; rather they divided the state into 22 administrative parts, and from their collection they formed a federal republic in which political authorities are divided between the central government and the governments of the cantons (the states). It is also built on the basis of clear distinction among the three powers: legislative, executive and judicial. As for the executive power, it is run by a federal council of ministers consisting of seven members whom the legislature elects for a period of four years. They are a substitute for the head of the single executive body, and this council elects from among its members a president for one year only, to preside over its meetings throughout the year; but beyond that his authority is equal to the authority of the other members. Usually in electing them an attempt is made, as far as possible, that they be from the various cantons and belong to the different ethnicities and religions. These seven are the ones who run the wheel of the executive apparatus for the whole Republic; but since it is divided into 22 states, each of which enjoys broad self-government, [p. 142] federal affairs are few among them and the burdens on the federal council are light. And to observe the principle of separation of powers, no sooner are these seven elected as members of the federal council than they are deprived of their seats in the National Assembly; but they are compelled to obtain the assembly's ratification of their policies and plans, so they attend its meetings without having in them the right to vote.

The woman has also been granted the right to vote in elections a few years ago; before that the woman did not enjoy this right of hers.

Among the most important features of the Swiss system: the popular referendum on issuing many laws and ratifying government policies and programmes. Accordingly referendums are numerous among them, and there are matters on which the government must undertake a popular referendum before concluding them, such as amending the constitution for example, while in some matters it is optional and not obligatory.

Likewise, if one hundred thousand persons propose holding a referendum on the constitution, the government must then undertake it.

This is the situation that the constitution of the Republic imposes; as for external reality, the people often does not know its president, since he is elected for one year and followed in the next year by another; and whoever has tasted the sweetness of this presidency usually does not wish to ascend its seat another time, because of the grave responsibilities it involves, alongside the paltry financial and material interests that his counterpart obtains for this service of his.

Usually this president does not enjoy the pomp and might that the presidents and members of government in other states have, to the extent that the minister is not allowed to put the flag of his political party on his house or his car, nor do ceremonial rites accompany his reception. One of the citizens told me this during a visit I made to it a few months ago: that they have no conception that the minister is accompanied by his servants, his entourage of guards and others; he lives only as one of the subjects.
