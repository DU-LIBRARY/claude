
[p. 103] list according to the sequence the party set, from the highest to the lowest: the first candidates are elected and the rest are left. But it is clear that this method can be followed only if the elections are on party bases, i.e. the names of the candidates appear with an indication of their party affiliations.

Sometimes the single transferable vote system (Single Transferable Vote) is followed; in summary: "The voters give the candidates listed on the ballot paper numbers according to the order of their choice. After counting the total number of ballot papers, those responsible for the elections set — by a mathematical method — the electoral quota, or the minimum number of votes for winning. The candidate who obtains the quota that qualifies him from the first choices is declared the winner. Then all the votes this candidate obtained in excess of the quota are distributed to the candidates whom the voters chose in second place, after excluding the candidate who obtained the lowest number of the votes that this candidate obtained among the candidates listed in second place. If the second-rank candidate has been elected, the vote is transferred to the third rank, and so on. This process continues until a sufficient number of candidates obtains the electoral quota to fill all the seats."

At any rate, this was a sketch of the alternative methods that several states have followed in order to overcome the problem of winning by a minority vote, to which the simple-majority system leads. However, the systems mentioned above have also not been successful in finding a fully convincing solution to that problem, since there are still possibilities for a candidate to win by a minority vote relative to the total votes in that electoral district. In addition, these systems are equally extremely complex, which has led to increased possibilities of fraud in elections and a negative effect on their integrity, alongside what they cost the state in exorbitant sums, and the continuation of a state of ambiguity and instability for a long period. Despite all this, it cannot be decided that they guarantee the election of members on the basis of serious, accurate representation of the various components of society.

#### (3) The legislature (Legislature)

[p. 104] The third democratic organization, which in itself holds great importance that makes us give it attention through research and study, is the legislature. It is a deliberative body that has the authority to enact and make laws in democratic government, and is called parliament and the national assembly (Assembly) as well. As for parliament (Parliament), it means: a place in which people gather for discussion and negotiation over some issue; and the meaning of (Assembly) is a reference to gathering and its place. The function of this organization is to lay down the laws and approve them for the state. Its method of legislating laws is that a draft law is presented to it, its members take it up by discussion and negotiation, and if it obtains the agreement and ratification of their majority, they adopt it and order it implemented in the state. In addition, it also discusses government policies. It is the highest official authority in the democratic system, and specifically the parliamentary one.

There are two systems followed in democratic states regarding the legislature:

**(1) Unicameral legislature (Unicameral)**

It is the simplest form of law-making bodies, and its meaning: that there is in the state one chamber to exercise legislative authority. It in turn undertakes the task of making laws, and every law it approves is implemented in the state without the need for another chamber to ratify it.

**(2) Bicameral legislature (Bicameral)**

In this system the legislative authority is divided into two separate groups called chambers; one of them:

The lower chamber: like the National Assembly in Pakistan, the House of Commons in Britain, the "House of Representatives" in the United States, and the "Lok Sabha" in India.

The second: the upper chamber, like the "Senate" (SENATE) in both America [p. 105] and Pakistan, the "House of Lords" in Britain and the "Rajya Sabha" in India.

As for the rationale for the multiplicity of legislative chambers, they say: the members of the lower chamber are elected by the vote of every adult citizen, usually on the basis of party affiliations, not qualifications and scholarly and practical abilities. Hence enthusiasm for their parties and submission to their policies take complete hold of them, so it is not unlikely that these will issue a law that harms the state and its interests. Therefore there was a pressing need for an upper chamber whose mechanism of formation differs from that of the lower chamber, to review the decisions of the lower chamber, discuss them and ratify them after ascertaining their seriousness and positivity and that they are in the interest of the state and the people. So the conception motivating the multiplicity of the legislative chamber is in reality that parliament, as long as it lacks specialists in the subject of the decision proposed for discussion in it, feels the pressing need for another chamber that includes a number of experts in various fields of life, so that they can study all dimensions of the laws from a professional point of view, then propose amendment to it if that seems right to them, or ratify it and adopt it for issuance and implementation. This rationale is accompanied by reasonableness, and is therefore worthy of acceptance — provided that the members of the upper chamber are elected in view of their professional qualifications and scholarly abilities. If that is not the case — as is common today, where they are not elected on the basis of the aforementioned criterion but by other varied and differing methods — then there is no need for it, nor does any fruit appear from it, nor does it bring any benefit or use to the state.

They have sometimes said, in explaining the need for the upper chamber, that the federal state that includes many provinces and states that differ among themselves in culture, civilization, language and traditions — some of its provinces may have a greater population density than others, like Punjab for example relative to Baluchistan and Sindh among the provinces of Pakistan; and since the seats of the lower chamber are distributed on the basis of population ratio, it has become possible for the representatives of one province in the lower chamber to be more numerous than the representatives of another province, and hence fears have increased that the representatives of the large province [p. 106] will agree to a decision leading to harm to the interests of the small provinces. Hence there should be above the lower chamber another chamber in which the number of representatives from each province is equal, regardless of the number of its inhabitants, so that this guarantees achieving balance and equilibrium among the interests of all the provinces of the united federal state, and prevents the representatives of the large province from turning the paths of general government decisions in the direction of the interests of their province only. This is the point that has led to the multiplicity of the legislative chamber in most states with two legislative chambers.

But on the other hand, one never ceases to marvel that the powers granted to the upper chamber will never be sufficient to achieve the aim intended from its formation — I mean protecting the rights of the small provinces in the united federal government. For it is an upper chamber in name only; in external reality the powers of the lower chamber are greater and stronger than its powers, as is the case in most states, where the law approved by the lower chamber is presented to the upper chamber: if it agrees with it [well and good]; otherwise, the most it can do is send it back to the lower chamber to reconsider it with more deliberation and thought about it. But even so, if the lower chamber persists in its position of adopting that law, the law is implemented, and the upper chamber does not represent any obstacle to its implementation.

As for our state, Pakistan, the situation there differs from other states, since the upper chamber there is regarded as the stronger and greater in authority and powers, especially after the Eighth Constitutional Amendment, which provided for expanding its powers. The Senate (Senate) in Pakistan aspires to both of the aforementioned aims: equality of the number of representatives of all provinces, and the allocation of many seats to skilled specialists who are elected on the basis of their scholarly qualifications. In addition, it has been granted the authority to initiate the process of enacting laws as well, so that a draft law can be presented to it first and independently, exactly as it can be presented to the lower chamber — in contrast to what obtains in other states, where the draft law is presented to the lower chamber only, and after its approval is presented to the upper chamber for consultation [p. 107] and the offering of advice only.

The other thing that distinguishes the Pakistani parliamentary system is that the law, after its approval by the National Assembly, is presented to the Senate. If it approves it as it is, it is implemented; and if it proposes amendments to it, it is sent a second time to the National Assembly to know its position on those amendments and obtain its agreement on them; otherwise a joint meeting comprising the members of both chambers is held, in which the draft law is presented so that its members vote on it, and that law is implemented if a majority of the members' votes support it. At any rate, it follows from this that our Senate in Pakistan enjoys greater powers than it does in any other state.

Most parliamentary states today follow the system of a bicameral legislature, and many researchers in the field of political science are not convinced of the usefulness of this system and its suitability for achieving the interests of the state and looking after it; but at the same time they acknowledge its continuation and persistence, as they cannot reject it categorically by virtue of its prevalence and spread in most states of the world.

As mentioned earlier, the democratic system regards the legislature as the highest official body, and confers on it infallibility from error; but the two systems of Britain and America differ in that. The British system, by virtue of its belief that the legislature is infallible from error, does not allow any organization or authority to review any law issued by parliament or any action it takes; whereas the American system, despite believing parliament to be an independent body, subjects all laws to judicial review, so it allows every citizen to challenge a law and bring it before the court if he considers it contrary to the constitution or in conflict with fundamental rights. If the court agrees with him on that and regards the law as a violation of the constitution, it orders its cancellation and nullification. This is what is called the system of judicial review (Judicial Review).

It is the system in force among us in Pakistan, where it allows objection to a law that parliament has approved if it is contrary to a clause of the constitution or to fundamental human rights, and the judicial system enjoys the right to cancel it if it sees fit; and after its cancellation by the court it is regarded [p. 108] as abrogated and void.

The Pakistani constitution also provides for permitting a challenge to a law approved by parliament if it is contrary to the Qur'an and Sunna, in the "Federal Shariat Court", which is the court established specifically for this purpose, to cancel, in its capacity, a law it regards as contrary to the Qur'an and Sunna. The ruling of this court can be appealed before the "Shariat Appellate Bench", which is a branch of the Supreme Court, to undertake in its turn the study of the decision of the Federal Shariat Court in the light of the Qur'an and Sunna; if it agrees with its decisions and sees that the law parliament approved is contrary to the rulings of the Qur'an and Sunna, it orders its cancellation within a specified period. When the late General Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq undertook the establishment of this court specific to reviewing the laws of parliament in the light of the rulings of the Qur'an and Sunna, many secularists expressed their anxiety about it on the grounds that it amounts to subjecting parliament — which is the highest official authority — to these two courts (the Federal Shariat Court and the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court), and that granting them the right of veto (the veto) results only in eliminating the sovereignty and authority of parliament.

The truth is that it is an objection that has no share of sound reasoning, nor even of seriousness. For just as many states — Pakistan among them — have followed the system of judicial review, which means subjecting parliament and its laws to the constitution and fundamental human rights, what harm then is there in subjecting it to the divine constitution and His rulings revealed in the Qur'an and Sunna?! If the subjection of parliament to the constitution is tolerated and is not regarded as in any way compromising its sovereignty and authority, why then is that not tolerated if its subjection is to the divine constitution?

#### (4) The constitution

The constitution is the prominent element that draws a dividing line between the democratic system and the absolute monarchical system.

People usually do not distinguish between the constitution and law, despite there being a great difference between them. The constitution is the name of a document that brings together the systems and laws upon which the state proceeds; [p. 109] it is the supreme law by which the government is run. It covers the competences of the three powers: the legislative power, the judicial power and the executive power, alongside its covering the competences of each of the head of state and the prime minister, and the form of the legislative power — whether with one legislative chamber or two legislative chambers — and how the state's expenditures are directed, the method of forming the judicial system and its competences. All these matters and others — the relations among the powers and the limits of each power and its functions — are matters the constitution deals with. From this it can be said that the constitution is a law the government is obliged to follow, while "the law" is what the people must follow (i.e. what the government has imposed on it).

The constitution is then divided into two types:

**(1) The unitary constitution (Unitary)**

It means: that the state, with its parts and political units, has one unified legislative chamber, and its units — provinces, states and regions — do not have a special legislative chamber; rather, the unified central government runs all the affairs of the state, represented in unified judicial, legislative and executive powers. As for the political units, their affairs are run by employees of the central government and in subordination to it, such as the governor of the city (commissioner: *qūmisīr*), for example, or his deputy. Although he undertakes the administration of the city's system, he cannot properly be called a ruler; he is only an employee of the central government. Likewise, if the state is divided into regions and states, the central government appoints persons who play their role in steering the helm of their system as employees of the government, not as independent rulers, since those regions do not have for themselves legislative bodies and councils, nor regional assemblies, nor councils of ministers.

This is a quick review of the system of the unitary constitution. It is usually followed by states not divided into many states and provinces, or states whose people all embrace the unified point of view. For since everyone in them believes that there is no conflict of interests among the members of the people, they prefer the system of the unitary constitution.

[p. 110] We can give an example of this with Britain, which is divided into many provinces that differ in their cultures, dialects, ways of life and so on; yet they nevertheless follow the system of the unitary constitution, on the argument that its whole people believes in a unified intellectual system and adopts the unified point of view.

**(2) The federal constitution**

This system is followed only by states divided into many local units. The idea behind establishing this system was that each local unit first enjoyed its own authority and independent government, such that it could properly be regarded as an independent state. Then those governments came together, and they created covenants and an alliance among themselves — what was later called the union (federation); and their central government was called the federal government, while each region had its special branch government. They divided governmental powers between these two bodies (the central government and the local government).

Sometimes this idea is not what is behind the establishment of the federal system: namely when the state from the beginning was governed by a large, unified government, but was divided into local political units of regions and states. So the federal system was preferred in order to convince each state and region that it would have its own authority, and its voice would be heard and its identity tangible. In this system, political authority is divided between the central (national) government and what each region has of local government. Pakistan is regarded as an example of this: here there is a central government that controls all the regions subordinate to it, while each region enjoys its local government as well. Punjab has its own government, and Sindh, Baluchistan and Sarhad [the Frontier] their own governments, and these governments have their defined powers, and possess legislative assemblies and councils of ministers, all of which is administered by its executive power.

As for the task of defining the competences and powers of both governments — central and local — the constitution of the state undertakes it, which contains a full explanation of the matters in which the authority [p. 111] to legislate belongs to the central government, and those matters in which the regional government undertakes the enactment of laws as its right. To meet this purpose, the constitution sometimes includes many lists: a list including the matters on which the central government legislates, and another for those in which the task of legislation belongs to the regional government. Such is the constitution of Pakistan, which contains two lists: a list of the matters in which the central government alone has the right to legislate, and another for the matters and issues in which both governments share the right to legislate. As for what lies outside them — the matters that do not come within one of the two lists — the authority to legislate in them belongs to the local government specifically. In view of this detail, the regions that demand regional autonomy in fact mean by their call that that list of shared matters be struck from the constitution, and that the authority to legislate in them also be delegated to the local governments alone, without the central [government].¹

So this was a brief discussion of the two types of constitution: the unitary and the federal.

#### The basic components of a constitution

A constitution does not have a particular prescribed form that states must follow when laying down their own constitution; rather, each state has been given free rein to lay it down in whatever arrangement and codification it sees most fitting to its circumstances and most successful for its interests. Nevertheless, there are several basic components of which a constitution is composed, and rarely is a state's constitution devoid of them. Below we summarize the explanation of those components that play the role of the common measure in the forms of states' constitutions.

**The preamble of the constitution**

It is the first thing with which the constitution of every state opens, and the English term "Preamble" is specifically applied to it, meaning: the introduction to a legal document. From this it becomes clear that this word is not applied to the introduction of a book or otherwise; it is specific to the introduction of a legal document. And since the constitution [p. 112] is also a type of law — as we stated earlier, it is a law the government follows — it is correct to apply the term "preamble" to its introduction. If you happen to read a law, you would find it begins with their saying: "Whereas such-and-such…, the General Assembly issues this law." This opening phrase is the preamble of the constitution.

The prevailing general principle regarding this preamble is that it is not regarded as part of the core and text of the law; it is only an introduction preceding it to explain the reasons that called for the making and implementation of the law. So, from the law's point of view, it is equivalent to wisdom and interest (*ḥikma* and *maṣlaḥa*) among the scholars of uṣūl [legal theory]: just as the ruling does not turn, among them, on the interest, likewise one cannot object to a government measure on the grounds that it contradicts the preamble of such-and-such law. Yes, it is permissible to draw on it in interpreting the text of that law and clarifying what is general in it and explaining what is ambiguous; for the preamble aims at explaining the purposes for which the law was laid down. Hence, if a law admits two interpretations, it is natural that the interpretation that suits those purposes and harmonizes with those aims be preferred. Let me clarify it for you with an example:

The Pakistani parliament approved a law on the Sharia ḥudūd [prescribed punishments], in which it defined the zinā [unlawful sexual intercourse] that requires the ḥadd by saying: "that a man and woman engage in sex without any valid marriage between them." We faced in the Supreme Court the problem of interpreting the valid marriage mentioned in the text of the law. In such cases recourse is usually had to the other laws in force in the country, since it is established among them that the marriage those laws permit is regarded as valid marriage, and what they do not regard as such is not permissible. And since the law in force among us in this regard is "the Family Laws Ordinance", which includes several non-Sharia clauses, among them: that the husband, if he divorces his wife, is not regarded as having divorced her legally as long as the husband has not given notice (Notice) of it to the chairman of the local council (Union council); if he did so his divorce is regarded as legal, otherwise not. The consequence of that was that if the husband divorced his wife and did not send notice of it to the chairman of the local council, and the woman relied on the written document of divorce signed by the husband and married another husband, [p. 113] this marriage of hers is valid and permissible according to the Sharia, while it is not permissible in the view of the law, since she is still a wife of the first husband, because his divorce is not regarded [as valid] given that he did not give notice of it to the local council. So when her marriage became impermissible in the view of the law, it was natural that she be punished with the punishment for zinā according to the Ḥudūd law; and such cases actually occurred, and some courts sentenced the woman in such a case to the punishment for zinā, which caused women to fall into many problems and to be exposed to fierce aggressions.

When a petition in this regard was presented to us in the Supreme Court, and the case was delegated to me, I ruled in it that the preamble of the Ḥudūd law states that it was implemented only to make the law of zinā entirely in accordance with the rulings of the Sharia, as it contained what means: "Whereas it is in the interest to make the laws relating to zinā in accordance with the Sharia… this law is laid down that…"

Since what is intended by laying down this law, on the one hand, is the application of the rulings of the Sharia, and, on the other hand, there is a text in the law itself that it shall have supremacy and dominance over other laws conflicting with it, what counts in interpreting the words in it is the Sharia and its rulings, not the other laws in force in the state. And since this woman's marriage is permissible according to the Sharia, it is not correct to judge her guilty of zinā nor to punish her for it.

Thus the preamble of a law can be drawn on in explaining the law, interpreting what is general in it and clarifying what is ambiguous in it, but it is not part of the text of the law, and hence it is not treated as law.

Usually, the preamble of a constitution alludes to the goals and purposes to which the state aspires, as well as the aims it seeks to achieve through laying down the constitution. Perhaps you have often heard of the "Objectives Resolution" in the constitution of Pakistan. That resolution was in reality a preamble to the constitution, adopted by the Constituent Assembly in 1948 [sic; 1949] after attempts and mighty efforts undertaken by Shaykh ʿAllāma Shabbīr Aḥmad ʿUthmānī (may God have mercy on him) and his companions. In detail: the resolve of the official bodies to undertake the process of laying down a constitution for the state had stirred debate over the type of constitution, and the secular circles began exerting every effort to turn its course towards secularism [p. 114] and irreligion. So Shaykh Shabbīr Aḥmad (may God have mercy on him) and his companions fought them with mighty efforts and serious attempts in a decisive battle that ultimately resulted in parliament's ratification of the "Objectives Resolution" after the then Prime Minister Liaquat Ali Khan presented it. This brief resolution defined the direction of the state in general and showed its condition and purposes. Here below is the text of this resolution:

**Translation of the text of the Objectives Resolution, 1949**

[Tr.: What follows is an English rendering of the Arabic version as given in the book. The official English text of the Objectives Resolution (1949) differs in wording; researchers quoting it should use the official text.]

"Whereas God alone — without any partner or rival — is the true, absolute Sovereign over the whole of this universe, the authority and power that He has granted to the people of Pakistan as a sacred trust are rightly to be exercised only within the framework of the limits laid down by God, glorified and exalted.

This, then, is the translation of the text of the "Objectives Resolution" which the national parliament approved, and which became, and still is, a preamble to the constitution of the state through the passing of ages and the succession of constitutions. Hence it is not part of the text of the law, and is not treated as such; it is only drawn on in interpreting and explaining the law, as the courts have declared in many cases. As a logical consequence of this, a law contrary to this resolution is not regarded as cancelled and void on the basis of being a violation of the constitution.

After the courts issued decisions to this effect, the religious bodies in the state expressed their anxiety about the standing of this important resolution, and demanded that the government take the necessary measures to regard it as part of the basic law and not a preamble to it. The late President Muhammad Zia-ul-Haq responded to them, as he undertook the Eighth Constitutional Amendment by adding to it clause 2-A, which provides that the "Objectives Resolution" is part of the core of the law and is treated as the other parts of the law, not as a preamble and introduction. Because of this amendment it became possible to object to government policies if they deviated from the principles mentioned in the "Objectives Resolution". But regrettably, the Supreme Court made clear in one of its decisions that although this resolution is part of the law, it has no supremacy over the other parts of the law; rather it and the other parts of the law are all of one rank. This ruling once again introduced ambiguity regarding the standing and rank of this resolution, and it is likely that the court will again clarify the actual effects that follow from regarding it as a necessary part of the law. I also find myself confident that if the court reconsidered it and gave it the aspect of deliberation, it would necessarily reach — God willing — a mechanism justifying objection to [p. 116] a law on the grounds of its contradicting this resolution.

**Fundamental human rights**

The second part of the constitution usually consists of the declaration of the protection of fundamental human rights (Fundamental Rights). Democratic constitutions take great pride in guaranteeing to all humanity the protection of these fundamental rights, especially in comparison with the individual despotic governments in which no conception of these rights was possible. Hence every constitution laid down in a state with a democratic character must provide for these fundamental rights guaranteed to the people, their protection and provision. Among those rights: the state's guarantee that the sanctity of a person's life, property and honour will not be violated; and its being provided in the constitution means that the government may not lay down a law or take an administrative measure that touches these rights by violation. Among those rights also: freedom of expression, meaning that every citizen can express his opinion with full freedom and without finding an obstacle hindering him from it. Among them also: freedom of religion, so every citizen is free to profess whatever religion he wishes and embrace whatever school he is pleased with. Among them likewise: freedom to form associations, so every citizen has the right to establish whatever association or union he wishes. Among them: the protection of property, so no one may violate its sanctity. And among them: freedom to elect representatives and rulers by using his right to vote with full freedom.

As for the development of the idea of fundamental rights, it is believed that the king of England "John the Second" [sic: King John] was the first to ratify a legal document in which he pledged to protect human rights, in the thirteenth century CE. That document is called "the Great Charter" (Magna Karta). It has attained such fame and acceptance in political circles that it has become the original source to which the discussion of civil fundamental rights is always attributed, and England regards it as a source of pride, claiming that it was the first to lay the first brick in the edifice of the call to fundamental rights. But on the other hand, if we examine the text of this document, we find it devoid of focus on civil rights, except for what appears in it [p. 117] in the clause providing that no citizen shall be detained except in the cases defined by law, and no one shall be convicted until a regular judicial body establishes his conviction. This brief sentence is what relates to civil rights among the clauses of the document called the Magna Carta, which clearly shows the worsening situation regarding human rights before the signing of that document, as it was common to arrest and detain a person without there being a pressing need for a legal justification that would allow and permit it! As for the remaining clauses, some of them concern the independence of the Church, and some aim at explaining the government's entitlement to impose taxes on the people and so on. So although there is nothing in it — apart from that single clause — relating to civil fundamental human rights, they have raised an uproar by claiming the precedence of humanity in protecting and safeguarding human rights. By contrast, the fundamental rights the Prophet ﷺ declared in the Farewell Sermon are adorned with comprehensiveness and clarity, which the Magna Carta document does not reach in its dust nor attain in its course, let alone surpass or approach it!

On the other hand, the Noble Qur'an too has declared the civil fundamental rights of man since the first dawning of Islam, and its teachings began to fill the universe with light and brilliance; but they close their eyes to it, trying to conceal it out of what has taken hold of them of tendencies of feigned blindness and feigned ignorance born of blind religious partisanship.

Hence the question posed still forms a brilliant challenge to them. It is thus established that subjecting man's freedoms to these things means nothing but a trick to strip them from him, and enabling those in authority to use the umbrella of the subordination of rights to the rule of law — which reverts to them in laying it down and implementing it — as an instrument for depriving man of them whenever they wish.

Hence we must acknowledge this established fact: that the world has no philosophy forming a decisive, definitive criterion for imposing restrictions and laying down conditions on human freedoms. So no means remains for it other than for man to submit to that Power capable of granting him his creation and endowing him with the power of opinion and depositing in him the faculty of thought; and consequently he must resort to Him concerning the restriction of the freedom of his opinion and his thought and the narrowing of its scope. It is thus established from all this that the freedoms granted to man by the documents of civil rights must be restricted in the light of the divine rulings and in subordination to His revealed law. Whatever agrees with His law and rulings, there is no harm in expressing; and what does not agree with them is the true site of restriction by which the opinion ought to be bound.

As for human philosophy and human law, they will never succeed in establishing a decisive, definitive criterion that can be followed always and absolutely.

At any rate, this has been a brief introduction to civil / fundamental rights, which are regarded as among the most important components of democratic constitutions in our age.

After the preamble and the statement of rights, the constitution is usually tripartite in explaining the three powers of government (legislative, executive and judicial), the competences of each of them, the methods of their formation and so on.

As for the legislature, we have spoken about it before in detail, so we refrain from [p. 120] mentioning it again, turning to cast a passing glance at the two powers, the executive and the judicial, and the constitution's position on them.

**The executive**

The executive is divided into two types:

(1) The political executive: meaning the executives who ascend the seat of government through elections and obtaining voters' votes, and who undertake to steer the wheel of government affairs throughout a specified electoral cycle — such as the prime minister, and likewise the ministers themselves.

(2) The non-political executive: it comprises government employees, such as secretaries of ministries and directors of government departments. They are not employed on the basis of elections, and hence not limited by an electoral cycle; they are government employees who manage its affairs before the elections and after them as well, without revolving with the governments and the parties forming them.

The constitution provides the principles and rules for both types of executives, such as the formation of the government and the definition of its authorities and powers, the employment of administrative officials and the definition of the conditions of their employment, and other necessary matters.

The constitution usually guarantees administrative officials that they will not be removed from their positions except for a reasonable cause justifying that for the government. Accordingly we see them as permanent over its ruler, while the government is overturned and passes from one person to another and from one party to another; and sometimes they acquire complete control over the government such that they influence government policies and commands. This is what is called bureaucracy (in English: Bureaucracy).

**The judicial system of the state**

Among the essential characteristics of democratic government is the existence of an independent judicial body. From this, the constitution in the democratic system gives it attention by specifying its activities in the manner of selecting judges, and mechanisms that greatly strengthen its independence, so that it is able [p. 121] to exercise its tasks with complete freedom and without being affected by any pressure from any direction. From this, democratic systems focus a great deal on freeing the task of appointing judges and installing them — and specifically for judges of the higher courts — from any political or governmental element. Take America, for example: it stipulated for the appointment of judges the ratification and approval of the Senate (Senate), while many states do not stipulate this condition; rather, they allow the head of state or the head of government to appoint whom he wishes as a judge without needing to obtain the approval of the legislature. This is regarding the appointment of judges and installing them in judicial positions. As for their removal from judicial positions, it has become firmly established in all states that the ruling body has no authority at all to remove them and depose them from their positions — so that the judge does not deviate from ruling in cases out of fear that the government will remove him and depose him from his position if it needs to rule against the government and its policies. Accordingly, the constitution usually includes legitimate ways to remove the judge and dismiss him from his position if need calls for that. America has delegated the authority to remove them to the Senate (Senate), and Pakistan has formed for this purpose a special council named "the Supreme Judicial Council", which undertakes investigation into the case of judges if accusations are brought to it against them; if it ascertains the soundness of the accusation, it can remove the accused judge and dismiss him from his position. These are attempts that democracy makes in order to preserve the integrity of the judicial system and protect it from veering right and left under political pressures or interferences of the executive.

The constitution also provides the principles and rules relating to the affairs of the military forces, and the state's expenditures and budget.
