## Chapter Five: Defence and Foreign Affairs

### Introduction

[p. 283] As becomes clear from the heading mentioned above, this chapter has been held for studying the issues and questions which Islamic government faces concerning international relations, and clarifying the rulings of Islam which it will follow in laying down foreign policies. And before plunging into the depths of the details of the subject, it is fitting for us to give a brief account of the explanation of two terms which will often knock at our ears during our talk in this chapter, namely Dār al-Islām and Dār al-Ḥarb, into which the jurists confined all the states of the world.

### Dār al-Islām and Dār al-Ḥarb

**Dār al-Islām** (the Abode of Islam): it is the state which is under the hand of the Muslims, and in which complete control has been obtained for them such that their word is manifest in it and their rulings are implemented in it. And ʿAllāma al-Sarakhsī (may God have mercy on him) defined it with what is synonymous with this, saying: "For Dār al-Islām is a name for the place which is under the hand of the Muslims."¹

And he defined it in *Jāmiʿ al-Rumūz*, attributing it to *al-Kāfī*, by saying: Dār al-Islām is that in which the rule of the imam of the Muslims runs, and they are safe in it.²

So it is concluded that the abode only becomes Dār al-Islām if authority in it has been obtained for the Muslims, even if all the rulings of Islam are not running in it in implementation and under activation. (This is another matter: that implementing the Sharia and applying all its rulings is in itself an important duty and a grave responsibility on the shoulders of the Muslims, especially in an Islamic state in which they have control over it and influence in it.)

And the matter became confused for some, as they thought that the word "the rule of the imam of the Muslims" which came in the text of *Jāmiʿ al-Rumūz* mentioned just now [means]: implementing all the rulings of the Sharia; and from that they went

> ¹ *Sharḥ al-Siyar al-Kabīr* (1/1253).
> ² *Jāmiʿ al-Rumūz* 4/556.

[p. 284] to [holding] that the state in which the Sharia and its rulings are not actually implemented is not of Dār al-Islām in anything.

But it is an illusion that has no share of soundness; for the only element that makes the state Dār al-Islām is that its authority and its control be in the hands of the Muslims such that they are able in it to implement the Sharia and apply all its rulings. Then, after their ability over it, if they did not actually undertake it out of slackness and negligence, they sin for that a severe sin, and it is obligatory on them to abandon it immediately and to undertake their responsibilities and perform their duty towards implementing the rulings of the Sharia in the areas of their influence and to the extent of their powers.

And we do not see any contradiction between the two definitions of al-Sarakhsī and *Jāmiʿ al-Rumūz*; for the principle is what al-Sarakhsī (may God have mercy on him) said: that the state be under the hand of the Muslims — what was expressed in *al-Jāmiʿ al-Rumūz* by the running of the rule of the imam of the Muslims in it — regardless of whether its rulings and commands are in conformity with the Sharia or not. And since it was difficult at that time to conceive the Muslims' slackness to the limit of carelessness about implementing the Sharia in a state that is under their hands and in their authority, the earlier jurists therefore did not undertake to state its ruling expressly; rather they contented themselves in that with the affirmative side of the definition of Dār al-Islām: that it is the state in which the rule of the Muslims runs. As for the later ones among them, they stated its ruling expressly also after they witnessed this deteriorating situation and saw this bad day for Islam: its children slack in it from implementing it in their control and their authority! And among the express statement concerning it is what ʿAllāma Ibn ʿĀbidīn al-Shāmī (may God have mercy on him) said: "And by this it became apparent that what is in Syria of the mountain of Taym Allāh called Jabal al-Durūz and some of the lands belonging to it are all Dār al-Islām; because even if they have Druze or Christian rulers and they have judges on their religion, and some of them openly insult Islam and the Muslims — yet they are under the rule of our holders of authority, and the lands of Islam surround their lands from every side; and if the holder of authority wanted to implement our rulings among them, he would implement them."¹

And what we said also becomes clear from it: that what counts in Dār al-Islām is that the Muslims have

[p. 285] complete control over it and full influence, even if they did not actually implement in it all the rulings of the Sharia out of negligence and laziness in performing their duty and undertaking their responsibilities.

**And on the contrary from this, Dār al-Ḥarb** (the Abode of War) is the state which is under the hands of the unbelievers and in their control and their authority, as came in *Jāmiʿ al-Rumūz* also: "And Dār al-Ḥarb is that in which the command of the head of the unbelievers runs."¹

And from this, if authority over a state were established for non-Muslims and their influence in it were completed, then it is Dār al-Kufr (the Abode of Unbelief), even if the Muslims were allowed in it to establish their rites and set up their judicial courts; because what counts — as we said — is authority, influence and control, and it is obtained here for the unbelievers.

And from this starting point, ʿAllāma Shaykh Rashīd Aḥmad al-Gangohī (may God have mercy on him) gave a fatwa that India is Dār al-Ḥarb, even though the Muslims were free in it in establishing the Friday prayer, the ʿĪds and the other religious rites.

But it is necessary here to draw attention to the fact that the jurists often apply the word "Dār al-Ḥarb" to every state over which the unbelievers have taken control and in which they have extended their authority and in which their rulings are implemented. And the truth is that it is not necessary that every state like this be Dār al-Ḥarb in the technical meaning also; and the detail of that is that the states of the unbelievers are of two kinds:

**Dār al-Ḥarb:** it is that between which and the Muslims there was no peaceful contract nor truce agreement, and it was not permitted for the Muslims in it to establish their religious rites. And this kind is Dār al-Ḥarb in every meaning of the word, and all its rulings apply to it — among them that the Muslims residing in it must emigrate from it to an Islamic state, or another unbelieving state in which they are able to establish religious rites and act by the teachings of the Sharia in a safe, peaceful atmosphere.

**And Dār al-Amn** (the Abode of Safety): it is a state ruled by unbelievers, but the Muslims are able in it to establish their religious rites and follow the commands of the Sharia in their personal and social life with all

### Relations of the Islamic government with other states

And since it has become clear from the detail that has just passed that all states, in relation to the Islamic government, are divided into three kinds — Dār al-Islām, Dār al-Amn and Dār al-Khawf — we shall clarify below the details of its relations with each one of these three kinds.

### Relations with Muslim states

We mentioned earlier, in the discussion of the multiplicity of governments and their unity, that the ideal method from the point of view of Sharia consideration is that all the Muslims throughout the world and its breadth have one government administered by one imam and caliph. But — regrettably — they have divided and separated in external reality into more than fifty units or more of states. So first, all these states should strive to bring about unity among the Muslims of the world and to incorporate them into the string of the unified Islamic government. So every state must give attempts at establishing unity the place of precedence in its foreign policy, as a contribution from it with its share in founding a unified world Islamic state that gathers them all

[p. 288] on one pavement.

And God has given the Islamic states great privileges which He did not give to others than them among the holders of other religions. For all their states — beginning from the Far Maghrib and ending with Indonesia — form a chain in which some of its links support others; just as they occupy the best geographical location in the world, where they are located in its heart, possessing the most important main international routes like the Bosphorus Strait, the Suez Canal, the Gulf of Aden, the Strait of Hormuz and so on — in addition to their containing in their interior the most important oil reserves, to the point that the current saying became famous about them: "Oil is found wherever the Muslims are found"; and likewise their wealth and their enormous human heritage. And add also their tremendous material wealth which no Eastern [state] reached throughout its past history: billions upon billions of dollars which they pumped and piled up in the banks of America and the other Western states, so that those states enjoy them with all enjoyment and draw profits with them, even by lending at interest to the Islamic states if they are in need of them for fulfilling their necessary needs. So in view of these situations, Islamic unity does not guarantee for these states the loftiness of their standing politically only; rather it provides for them their interests from both aspects: economic and defensive also. So if God decreed for it that it be completed, it would stand out on the screen of the world as a nation with power and a lofty standing among the states of the world in defence and economy.

But, as we presented also, there is the fact that the formation of this Islamic unity perhaps cannot be completed and obtained except by the shedding of blood among the Muslims and their fighting one another — which would drag the Umma to a far worse and more evil situation. So until God enables their kings to sacrifice for the sake of unifying their word, so that they form from their states a unified Islamic state, or at least form among them a coalition and union, we find ourselves compelled to consider these dismantled units as abodes of Islam, because their rulers are Muslims. So at the least, there should be among these Islamic states connections of affection and relations of mutual support among one another.

And from this starting point, the point that occupies precedence in forming the foreign policy of the Islamic state is that it try to bring about a relationship and establish a good brotherly connection with its sister states

[p. 289] of the other Islamic states, built on the basis of solidarity and mutual support.

And it enters into it that one of those states, if the unbelievers launched a raid against it and attacked it, its sisters must support it and strengthen its position — indeed supply it with military support if it fell short of what it has of possibilities for protecting it and defending it. And the teachings of the Qur'an and the directives of the Prophetic Sunna and the texts of Islamic jurisprudence are explicit and clear in this field. God, glorified and exalted, said: **"And cooperate in righteousness and piety, but do not cooperate in sin and aggression"** [al-Māʾida, 5:2].

And He, Mighty is the One who said, said also: **"The believers are but brothers"** [al-Ḥujurāt, 49:10].

And the Prophet ﷺ said: "The Muslim is the brother of the Muslim; he does not wrong him nor hand him over [to the enemy]."¹

And he, upon him be blessing and peace, said also: "The believer to the believer is like a building, part of which supports another part."²

And also: "You see the believers, in their mutual mercy, their mutual love and their mutual sympathy, like the body: if one limb complains, the rest of its body calls out to it with sleeplessness and fever."³

And from this the jurists stated expressly that the unbelievers, if they attacked a town of the Muslims and there was not sufficiency in them for protecting their land and repelling their enemy, then it is obligatory on whoever is next to them of the Muslims to fight their enemy with them all together. ʿAllāma Ibn Nujaym (may God have mercy on him) said: "And it is an individual obligation (*farḍ ʿayn*) if the enemy attacks: so the woman goes out without the permission of her husband, and the slave without the permission of his master… And what is meant by its attacking is upon a specific town of the lands of the Muslims; so it is obligatory upon all the people of that town, and likewise whoever is near to them if there is not sufficiency in its people; and likewise whoever is near to whoever is near, if there was not sufficiency in whoever was near, or they were lazy and disobeyed — and so on until it becomes obligatory on all the people of Islam east and west."⁴

So we explain this place with an explanation that clarifies the import of each of alliance (*muwālāt*) and kindness to someone and goodwill towards him, and distinguishes some of them from others, and sets some apart from others.

The truth is that there are several degrees of relations and connections between two persons or two groups. Among them: alliance (*al-muwālāt*), or heartfelt affection; and it is specific to the Muslims. It is not permissible for the Muslim definitively to form with an unbeliever the like of this connection.

And among them: goodwill (*al-muwāsāt*), and it is the relationship of mutual sympathy and sincere counsel and conveying benefit and use to someone. And it is permissible for the Muslim to establish it with all the unbelievers except those waging war among them; and its detail came in the eighth verse of Sūrat al-Mumtaḥana, where God, glorified and exalted, said in it: **"God does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them"** [al-Mumtaḥana, 60:8].

The third degree: it is courtesy (*al-mudārāt*), and its meaning: gentleness, good character, companionship and social intercourse. And bringing it about is also permissible with all the unbelievers, if the aim from it was benefiting them religiously, or hosting them if they asked to be his guests, or protecting oneself from their strife and their harm. And this third rank is what is meant in His words, the Exalted: **"Except when taking precaution against them in prudence"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:28] — "unless you guard against them

[p. 292] with precaution" which came within the aforementioned verse of Sūrat Āl ʿImrān; i.e. alliance with the unbelievers is not permissible except in the form where you intended by it protecting yourselves. And since courtesy is an alliance in form, its exception from it in the verse was correct.

The fourth degree: it is transactions (*al-muʿāmalāt*); and its meaning: that transactions be concluded with the unbelievers in the sector of trade or leasing or employment, profession and industry and so on. And concluding them is also permissible with all the unbelievers — except if it causes harm to the generality of the Muslims. The action of the Prophet ﷺ and the dealing of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs and others than them of the Companions, may God be pleased with them all, bear witness to that. And from this the prohibition came from them of selling weapons to the unbelievers, while they permitted besides it trade and leasing, and employing them or being employed with them in their factories and their institutions.

So it became known from the foregoing detail that it is never permissible for the Muslim to conclude with an unbeliever alliance or heartfelt affection; while goodwill and sincere counsel are permissible with all of them except those waging war among them; and likewise the Sharia permits him to be courteous to them and to have good character towards them if it was for hosting them, or for the purpose of benefiting them religiously and providing religious information to them, or protecting oneself from their evil and their harm."¹

And the gist of the words is that Islam has forbidden alliance and heartfelt affection, which cause the erasure of the difference between its two parties in the purposes of life with the unbelievers. As for other than that — of truce, the conclusion of peaceful agreements, human goodwill, being kind to them and counselling them on the basis of humanity, and cooperation and mutual support exchanged for realizing the common human interests — all this is permissible — indeed preferred and beloved — in Islam. We see it as best to present here several texts of the Qur'an and the Sunna related to the subject, and many of the forms and positions of the two eras of the Message and the Companions in this regard.

### Keeping covenants and agreements

[p. 293] As for first, here for you is the Qur'anic text which indicates the legitimacy of making peace and reconciliation with non-Muslim states if they do not conflict with the interest of the Muslims, and it is His words, the Exalted: **"And if they incline to peace, then incline to it [also] and rely upon God"** [al-Anfāl, 8:61].

And if the truce agreement was completed, it strongly emphasized fulfilling it, saying: **"O you who have believed, fulfil [all] contracts"** [al-Māʾida, 5:1].

And: **"And do not let the hatred of a people for having obstructed you from al-Masjid al-Ḥarām lead you to transgress"** [al-Māʾida, 5:2].

Even in the form where the Muslims feared from their enemy the breaking of the covenant, the Sharia also did not permit them to take any measure except after cancelling the treaty and throwing it back openly. God the Exalted said: **"If you [have reason to] fear from a people betrayal, throw [their treaty] back to them, [putting you] on equal terms. Indeed, God does not like traitors"** [al-Anfāl, 8:58].

And the history of the two eras of the Message and the Companions is full of splendid stories and pioneering events which clarify for us their carrying out the compliance with this matter and the application of this principle, the truest carrying out and the best of it. And among those stories is what Muslim narrated in his *Ṣaḥīḥ* from Ḥudhayfa ibn al-Yamān; he said:

"Nothing prevented me from witnessing Badr except that I set out, I and my father Ḥusayl. He said: The unbelievers of Quraysh seized us; they said: You want Muhammad. We said: We do not want him; we only want Medina. So they took from us the covenant of God and His pledge that we would go away to Medina and not fight with him. So we came to the Messenger of God ﷺ and told him the news; he said: 'Go away; we will fulfil their covenant for them, and we will seek God's help against them.'"¹

So let us estimate the gravity of the position by our measures: the first battle in which the Muslims raided to defend their religion, by which God decreed to judge between truth and falsehood, distinctive. Apart from that, the Muslim participants in it will obtain of it the reward, pride and honour in Islamic society which those after them will not obtain

> ¹ *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* (3/1414), Book of Jihad, chapter on fulfilling the covenant, hadith no. 4603.

[p. 294] after them: the struggle of 313 persons, unarmed, against a thousand heroes fully armed — which makes them in the most pressing need of human strength, even one person. And from another side, the justification for Ḥudhayfa and his father's breaking the covenant was permissible, since they had not taken the covenant and the pledge upon themselves willingly from them; rather it was under compulsion from the unbelievers and coercion. But the Prophet ﷺ forbade them from breaking this covenant — if it is correct to call it a covenant — with clarity and firmness, in avoidance of the slightest blemish of disloyalty and treachery. And by that he set up a bright lamp and a pioneering model example for his Umma for avoiding treachery even in a tense position and a deteriorated situation like this.

And likewise Abū Dāwūd narrated with a sound chain from Abū Rāfiʿ, may God be pleased with him; he said: "Quraysh sent me to the Messenger of God ﷺ. When I saw the Messenger of God ﷺ, Islam was cast into my heart. So I said: O Messenger of God, by God I will never return to them. So the Messenger of God ﷺ said: 'I do not break the covenant, nor do I detain messengers; but return, and if what is in your soul now is in your soul, then come back.' He said: So I went; then I came to the Prophet ﷺ and became Muslim."¹

And despite that there was not at that time any contract nor covenant between the Prophet ﷺ and the polytheists of Mecca, he nevertheless ordered him to return to them so that it would not be a breach of what is regarded as a covenant by custom and implication. And that is because of what ʿAllāma al-Khaṭṭābī (may God have mercy on him) said: "It resembles that the meaning in that is that the message requires an answer, and the answer does not reach the senders except upon the tongue of the messenger after his departure; so it became as if a covenant had been contracted for him for the duration of his coming and his return — and God knows best."²

As for the era of the Companions, read with me the following story which Imam Abū Dāwūd (may God have mercy on him) narrated: that there was between Muʿāwiya and the Romans a covenant, and he was travelling towards their lands so that when the covenant ended he would raid them. A man came on a horse or a nag, saying: God is greatest, God is greatest — fulfilment and not treachery! They looked, and it was ʿAmr ibn ʿAbasa. So Muʿāwiya sent to him and asked him; he said: I heard the Messenger of God ﷺ say: "Whoever has between him and a people a covenant, let him not tighten a knot nor untie it until

[p. 295] its term ends, or he throws [it] back to them on equal terms." So Muʿāwiya returned.¹

And let us notice that Muʿāwiya, may God be pleased with him, did not issue a breaking of the covenant; because he entered the land of the enemy and attacked them after the end of the period of the ceasefire and the halting of war. But his travelling to them before it — since ʿAmr ibn ʿAbasa, may God be pleased with him, regarded it as contrary to the requirement of scrupulousness and caution — Muʿāwiya, may God be pleased with him, yielded to him in compliance, for fear that it might be disobedience issued from him and opposition to the saying of the Prophet ﷺ. So perhaps it is the unique example in which the conqueror gave up what he had conquered of lands merely for fulfilling the requirement of caution and scrupulousness!

Just as the story indicated that what plunged them into the depths of the battles was not the greed to take control of lands nor the desire to prevail over servants; rather their aim from all that was attaining the pleasure of God, glorified and exalted. So when they saw it in giving up the conquered lands, they did not delay in giving them up immediately and at once.

And Muʿāwiya, may God be pleased with him, also has that splendid story which al-Qāsim ibn Sallām narrated with his chain as follows: "The Romans made peace with Muʿāwiya on [condition] that he pay them money, and Muʿāwiya took hostages from them and put them in Baalbek. Then the Romans acted treacherously. Muʿāwiya and the Muslims refused to make lawful killing those in their hands of their hostages, and they let them go, and opened [the war] with that against them, and said: Fulfilment in [the face of] treachery is better than treachery for treachery."²

### Pacts of joint defence

And alongside ceasefire agreements, there are found examples of the pacts which the Prophet ﷺ contracted and concluded with the unbelievers concerning common defence and common protection.

Among that is that Medina, when the Prophet ﷺ came to it, was dominated by the tribal system, and many tribes inhabited it — among them tribes of the Jews also — each one of which had its master and its head; and there was no central government gathering them on one pavement. So the Prophet ﷺ called them to

> ² *Al-Amwāl* of al-Qāsim ibn Sallām (1/211), paragraph 446.

[p. 296] join the string of a unified federal government — a matter which these welcomed. So at that time the Prophet ﷺ undertook laying down the constitution of the city-state in Medina in the form of a written document in which he included the rights and duties of all the citizens; and it is the first written constitution in human history, according to what Dr. Ḥamīdullāh (may God have mercy on him) verified. And it contained 43 clauses, and clauses 37 and 44 of them were devoted to the alliance between the Muslims and the Jews concerning mutual support if an enemy attacked Medina. Here for you are their texts:

(37) "And that upon the Jews is their expenditure and upon the Muslims their expenditure; and that between them is help against whoever wars against the people of this document; and that between them is sincere counsel and good conduct, not sin."

(44) "And that between them is help against whoever attacks Yathrib."¹

And likewise it was in the Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya: the contract between the Prophet ﷺ and the polytheists of Mecca was concerning ceasefire only; as for the Banū Khuzāʿa, there was between them and the Muslims an alliance concerning common defence, as a continuation of what they had been allied upon with the Banū Hāshim in the Jāhiliyya — and most of them had not yet become Muslim.

And the detail of that is that when the Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya took place between the Messenger of God ﷺ and Quraysh, among what they stipulated was that whoever liked to enter into the contract of the Messenger of God ﷺ and his pledge, let him enter into it; and whoever liked to enter into the contract of Quraysh and their pledge, let him enter into it. [So the Banū Bakr entered] into the pledge of Quraysh, and Khuzāʿa entered into the pledge of the Messenger of God ﷺ. And that did not mean except the alliance concerning common defence: that the polytheists and the Banū Bakr, if they attacked one of these two allies, it would be binding on the other to help his ally and support him in protecting him and defending him.

Then the Banū Bakr rose and attacked Khuzāʿa by night, and struck from them a man; and they argued and fought, and the Banū Bakr were reinforced by Quraysh with weapons, and some of Quraysh fought with them at night in hiding.

And when the Banū Bakr and Quraysh supported each other against Khuzāʿa [and struck from them what they struck] and broke what was between them and the Messenger of God ﷺ of covenant and pledge, ʿAmr ibn Sālim al-Khuzāʿī went out — Ibn Saʿd said: among forty riders — until he came to the Messenger of God ﷺ in Medina; and that was what stirred up

### Goodwill and sincere counsel

And God loves goodwill and sincere counsel and good dealing with the unbelievers on the basis of humanity. As for individually, it is [shown] by what came in the Qur'an of emphasizing good companionship and good social intercourse with the polytheist parents, except obeying them in polytheism and unbelief, where God, glorified and exalted, said: **"But if they endeavour to make you associate with Me that of which you have no knowledge, do not obey them but accompany them in [this] world with appropriate kindness"** [Luqmān, 31:15].

And His general command in this regard has passed in the verse of Sūrat al-Mumtaḥana: **"God does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them"** [al-Mumtaḥana, 60:8].

[This is] what indicates the good companionship of the polytheists and keeping ties with them individually and socially at the level of the state; and the researcher tracing the Prophetic biography, upon its owner be blessing and peace, stumbles upon abundant examples in it of ties of this kind. Among them is what al-Bukhārī narrated with his chain from Asmāʾ bint Abī Bakr, may God be pleased with them both; she said: My mother came to me while she was a polytheist in the time of the Messenger of God ﷺ. So I asked the Messenger of God ﷺ for a fatwa; I said: And she is desirous [of my kindness]; shall I keep ties with my mother? He said: "Yes, keep ties with your mother."³
