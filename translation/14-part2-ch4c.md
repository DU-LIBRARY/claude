
[p. 259] collecting it however they wished by imposing heavy taxes and crushing levies on the subjects, and spending it on whatever object they wished; and it no longer had a role except to guarantee them luxury of living and provide them with the satisfaction of desires.

And Islam from the very beginning regarded it as a public trust in the hands of the imam which he must spend on the subjects and expend in their interests. Then it did not leave the imam independent and despotic in collecting funds and spending them; rather it imposed heavy restrictions on him in this regard: it defined first the special resources and sources for them — namely zakat, the tithe (*ʿushr*), the land tax (*kharāj*), the *fayʾ*, lost property and so on. Then it divided them into kinds and specified for each kind of them a special object of expenditure second. So the funds of zakat and charities have with Islam their special object of expenditure, and it is not permitted to spend them in other than it; and likewise for the land tax and the jizya, and for others than them of the means.

And proceeding from this, the jurists of Islam undertook codifying its rulings from both these aspects — collecting funds and spending them — with all elaboration and clarity in the light of the rulings of the Qur'an and the teachings of the Prophetic Sunna and the conduct of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs. So you see the Islamic library flourishing with many books in which the eminent scholars devoted works to this chapter — among them *Kitāb al-Kharāj* of each of the two eminent imams Abū Yūsuf and Yaḥyā ibn Ādam (may God have mercy on them both), and *Kitāb al-Amwāl* of Imam Abū ʿUbayd Qāsim ibn Sallām (may God have mercy on him) — which have remained original sources and principal references for whoever came after them of jurists and scholars.

And it is narrated from ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, that he said in one of his sermons, explaining the manner of the public treasury: "And I do not find that this wealth is set right except by three traits: that it be taken by right, and given in right, and withheld from falsehood. And I and your wealth are only like the guardian of the orphan: if I am rich I abstain, and if I am poor I eat in what is right (*bi-l-maʿrūf*)."¹

And in a report of Ibn Saʿd this addition also came: "If I become well-off, I repay."²

### The ruling on taxes in Islam

The Islamic Sharia has regarded imposing taxes on the subjects in the general run of cases as injustice and tyranny, and severe threats have come against it — contenting itself with what it specified for the public treasury of resources that yield funds upon it, like zakat, charities, the land tax, the jizya and so on. But the Muslims, if a calamity struck them and emergency states befell the Islamic state, so that its funds

As for the emergency tax which the jurists permitted and which ʿAllāma al-Shāmī mentioned, it is not correct to draw an analogy with that, by virtue of its aiming at meeting the pressing need of the Islamic state and observing the clear interest of the subjects; so it does not enter into "*al-maks*", and from that the threat of the aforementioned hadith does not reach it.

So it is concluded from all this that Islamic government founded on straight foundations will be touched by a need — at least at the beginning of its era — to cover its necessary expenditures by burdening the subjects with "the emergency tax"; except that it — in view of what we presented of the details just now — will be of a low amount, easy to bear, if it undertook imposing it with caution and scrupulousness and succeeded in reducing the royal expenditures and the empty governmental burdens that have no meaning except making the commanders' living luxurious and comfortable. ### 13 – The rights of non-Muslim minorities in Islam

There has been broadcast in the world today propaganda against Islam that it does not protect the rights of the non-Muslim citizens who inhabit the Islamic states as minorities; and it is only fabricated propaganda that has no share of truth nor portion of seriousness. For Islam, even if it is a universal call to divine unity addressing all the children of Adam to embrace it and submit to it, it calls people to that by the pillar of evidence and the solidity of proof, so that they reflect and deliberate on its teachings and accept it with insight and understanding from them; it has never permitted its children to compel anyone to embrace it and belong to its religion. So here is the Noble Qur'an

[p. 268] calling out with that openly, saying: **"There is no compulsion in religion; the right course has become clear from the wrong. So whoever disbelieves in Ṭāghūt and believes in God has grasped the most trustworthy handhold"** [al-Baqara, 2:256].

And human history bears witness that the Muslims held fast to this divine ruling to the utmost holding; they did not use the weapon of force in compelling people to accept Islam, even in the most flourishing of its ages and the most advanced of its eras. Rather the most splendid example of tolerance is established from them: their judge permitted those who entered Islam compelled to return to their religion if they wished. Look at what Ibn Kathīr narrates to us in his history in the events of the year six hundred and eighty, saying: "And in Shawwāl a council was held because of the People of the Book of the dhimmīs who had become Muslim against their will; and a group of the muftis had written for them that they were compelled, so they had the return to their religion; and the compulsion was established before the judge Jamāl al-Dīn ibn Abī Yaʿqūb al-Mālikī; so most of them returned to their religion."¹

And from this, the unbelievers who inhabit the Islamic state — after its taking the covenant upon themselves for the benefit of the state — become like the rest of its Muslim citizens: they have of civil rights what they [the Muslims] have, and it is upon the state to protect them and defend them as it does for the Muslims. Indeed, it is right for us to say: that the protection of rights and the safeguarding of interests which Islam has granted to citizens not belonging to it and who inhabit its state as a minority — another religion has never equalled it in that nor approached it. And the duty of both parties becomes clear: the state and the non-Muslim minority. For Islam has assigned to it the term "*al-muʿāhad*" (the one under covenant) or "*al-dhimmī*" (the one under protection). As for *al-muʿāhad*, it is for the performance of the meaning obligatory upon it of living in it by safe, sound ways, without causing harm to the state or its interests in anything; and as for *al-dhimmī*, it is in view of what it has taken for itself from the state of protection (*dhimma*) concerning the protection of its life, its property and its honour.

### The jizya in Islam

We grant that Islamic government imposes a kind of taxes on its non-Muslim citizens called "the jizya" — which Western researchers sometimes take as [grounds] for impugning Islam and degrading it, as being a conduct involving discrimination among citizens on the basis of their religions and their schools, with the reasoning that the unbelievers alone are singled out for imposing it and burdening them with it, to the exclusion of the Muslims. And the truth is that if they had looked at it with the eye of fairness, far from blind religious partisanship towards the bad, they would have stumbled upon what is in imposing it of the interests and privileges which the unbelievers obtain to the exclusion of the Muslims. That is because Islamic government receives from the Muslim citizens the zakat of their wealth, whose percentage ranges between 2.5% minimum and 10% maximum — and it is higher than the percentage of the jizya which the unbelievers are committed to paying by much. And second, in return for this small amount, the Islamic state undertakes the responsibility of protecting them — their lives, honour and property; just as it exempts from the jizya the insolvent, needy among them; indeed it provides for them from the public treasury their provisions, as passed in what we narrated of the story of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, with an old Jew, and his giving him provision, and his ordering the removal of the jizya from him and his like.

And there is another point which explains what we have passed more and more clearly, namely that the Islamic state, if the unbelievers attacked it, then it is permissible for the imam to command all his Muslim subjects to contribute to jihad against the enemy and to defend the heartland of Islam and the sanctity of the lands; and it is not permissible for him to charge the unbelievers with defending themselves and their lands — that is, protecting it and defending it; rather their protection and defence of them in such a case is obligatory on the Islamic government towards them, in return for what it receives from them of the sums of the jizya. ### 14 [sic] – Regional autonomy in Islam

Among the burning issues that attract the attention of contemporary governments, and around whose solution the dispute intensifies and the clash increases, is the issue of the autonomy and local government of the regions and provinces belonging to a state. And we would like to list in this regard also several basic matters:

**First** — and before everything — we repeat here also what we said more than once: that Islam has not legislated a decisive ruling concerning the type of government, whether federal or unitary. So the rulings of Islam do not form any obstacle in the way of implementing the method which the Muslims have chosen or will choose in view of the interests of their age and the requirements of their times.
