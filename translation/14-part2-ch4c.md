
[p. 259] collecting it however they wished by imposing heavy taxes and crushing levies on the subjects, and spending it on whatever object they wished; and it no longer had a role except to guarantee them luxury of living and provide them with the satisfaction of desires.

Islam from the very beginning regarded it as a public trust in the hands of the imam which he must spend on the subjects and expend in their interests. Then it did not leave the imam independent and despotic in collecting funds and spending them; rather it imposed heavy restrictions on him in this regard: it defined first the special resources and sources for them — namely zakat, the tithe (*ʿushr*), the land tax (*kharāj*), the *fayʾ*, lost property and so on. Then it divided them into kinds and specified for each kind of them a special object of expenditure second. So the funds of zakat and charities have with Islam their special object of expenditure, and it is not permitted to spend them in other than it; and likewise for the land tax, the jizya and the other sources.

Proceeding from this, the jurists of Islam undertook codifying its rulings from both these aspects — collecting funds and spending them — with all elaboration and clarity in the light of the rulings of the Qur'an and the teachings of the Prophetic Sunna and the conduct of the Rightly-Guided Caliphs. So you see the Islamic library flourishing with many books in which the eminent scholars devoted works to this chapter — among them *Kitāb al-Kharāj* of each of the two eminent imams Abū Yūsuf and Yaḥyā ibn Ādam (may God have mercy on them both), and *Kitāb al-Amwāl* of Imam Abū ʿUbayd Qāsim ibn Sallām (may God have mercy on him) — which have remained original sources and principal references for whoever came after them of jurists and scholars.

It is narrated from ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, that he said in one of his sermons, explaining how the public treasury should be run: "And I do not find that this wealth is set right except by three traits: that it be taken by right, and given in right, and withheld from falsehood. I and your wealth are only like the guardian of the orphan: if I am rich I abstain, and if I am poor I eat in what is right (*bi-l-maʿrūf*)."¹

In a report of Ibn Saʿd this addition also came: "If I become well-off, I repay."²

Come, let us turn our gaze to a story of them which explains to us how the caliph began to take a stipend from the public treasury of the Muslims. Ibn Saʿd narrated in his *Ṭabaqāt* that when Abū Bakr was appointed successor, he set out in the morning to the market with garments on his neck which he traded in. ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb and Abū ʿUbayda ibn al-Jarrāḥ met him and said to him: Where do you want [to go], O successor of the Messenger of God? Thus he began to obtain his stipend from the public treasury. So when I die, take from my property eight thousand dirhams and return them to the public treasury. When they were brought to ʿUmar he said: May God have mercy on Abū Bakr; he has wearied those after him with a severe weariness."²

"I will tell you what I consider permissible of it for me: two garments are permissible for me — one for winter and one for summer — and what I perform the Ḥajj and the ʿUmra on of mounts, and my food and the food of my family like the food of a man of Quraysh,

But he did not sit at ease on this small amount either; rather it is narrated that when the people were struck by a year [of famine] in which ghee became expensive, ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, ate oil, and his belly rumbled. But nevertheless the Islamic Sharia does not obligate every caliph to follow them exactly; rather, looking to what motivated them, it holds that the purpose of all this is that the stipends of caliphs and rulers should not make them a burden on the public treasury, and should not exceed their needs through extravagance and waste. Otherwise the Prophet ﷺ himself assigned as provision to ʿAttāb ibn Asīd, when he appointed him over Mecca, forty ūqiyya [of silver] every year.³

This amount equals one thousand four hundred dirhams annually — which shows that the Sharia did not specify a special amount for stipends; rather decrease and increase in it are possible with the difference of time and place and in view of circumstances and situations, on condition that that not be taken as a means of extravagance and an instrument of waste.

### The ruling on taxes in Islam

The Islamic Sharia has regarded imposing taxes on the subjects in the general run of cases as injustice and tyranny, and severe threats have come against it — contenting itself with what it specified for the public treasury of sources of revenue, like zakat, charities, the land tax, the jizya and so on. But the Muslims, if a calamity struck them and emergency states befell the Islamic state, so that its funds

It is called in the terminology of the jurists "the emergency tax" (*al-ḍarība al-nāʾiba*). It is stipulated for that, first and last, that it be for necessity, and so limited to the extent of that necessity; and that the people not be charged with it except after the rulers have abandoned what they are upon of the life of luxury and extravagant living; and on condition that they be truthful of disposition and sound of intention in that. There is no doubt that what prevailed in the circles of kings and emperors in the Jāhiliyya — of burdening the subjects with heavy taxes and accumulated tolls — was injustice and tyranny and an injury from them against the subjects. The ambitions behind imposing them were nothing but saving money and piling it up in their personal treasuries, so that luxury of living, extravagance and indulgence in their lives became possible for them. The two passages quoted above make clear that the threat in the hadith is aimed at imposing this kind of tax; and it also covers what governments impose today without a Sharia necessity to justify it. As for the emergency tax which the jurists permitted and which ʿAllāma al-Shāmī mentioned, it is not correct to draw an analogy with that, by virtue of its aiming at meeting the pressing need of the Islamic state and observing the clear interest of the subjects; so it does not enter into "*al-maks*", and hence the threat of the aforementioned hadith does not apply to it.

Imam al-Ghazālī (may God have mercy on him) spoke at length on it: after he finished denouncing the rulers who impose taxes merely to pamper the army and make their own living luxurious, he followed it by saying: "As for if we supposed an obeyed imam in need of increasing soldiers to block the frontiers and protect the kingdom after the widening of its extent and the expansion of its territory, and the public treasury became empty of money, and the needs of the army pressed to what suffices them,

Then it is up to him to consider imposing that upon the yields of crops and fruits, so that singling out some people with it does not lead to the filling of breasts with rancour and the alienation of hearts; a small amount taken from much, which does not ruin them and by which the purpose is achieved."

The upshot of that is that Imam al-Ghazālī (may God have mercy on him) permitted imposing this kind of taxes with the following conditions:

- That the imam be among those whose obedience is obligatory.
- That there be a real necessity for protecting the state and defending the territory of the country and its heartland.
- That the public treasury be empty of money that fulfils that.
- That the taxes be in the amount that suffices for their needs at the time, until money appears in the public treasury.
- That imposing that on people be with justice and equity, and that he not differentiate in it between two men of one kind as to profession and work.

The jurists discourage rulers from imposing such taxes only because of what is plainly seen of rulers: that rarely do they observe the Sharia limits in it in completeness and perfection. So we see that the ruling of permissibility in the case of exceptional necessity has been surrounded by a mass of restrictions and a set of conditions, to protect the subjects from the commanders taking it as a prey for their ambitions and a means to attain their ease and their luxury.

Take for that, for example, the story of Shaykh ʿIzz al-Dīn ibn ʿAbd al-Salām (may God have mercy on him): when the Tatars began their attacks on the Islamic lands, the Sultan wanted to take money from the people to help finance jihad against them; and he gathered the jurists, judges and notables to consult them in this matter. Among those who attended was Shaykh ʿIzz al-Dīn ibn ʿAbd al-Salām… and they went at length in the talk; and the reliance was on what Ibn ʿAbd al-Salām said, and the summary of what he said: that when the enemy attacks the lands of Islam, it becomes obligatory on everyone to fight them, and it is permissible for you to take from the subjects what you seek help with in your jihad,

[p. 265] on condition that nothing remains in the public treasury, and that you sell what you have of gilded belts and valuable equipment, and that every soldier confine himself to his mount and his weapon, and they and the common people be equal. As for taking money from the common people while there remain in the hands of the soldiers money and luxurious equipment, then no.¹

Similar to that is what is narrated that when the Sultan al-Ẓāhir Baybars went out to fight the Tatars in Syria, he took the fatwas of the scholars that it is permissible for him to take money from the subjects to seek help with in fighting the enemy. He said: Add your signature to the jurists'. He refused. He said: What is the reason for your refusal? He said: I know that you were in slavery to the commander Bunduqdār, and you have no money. The upshot is that all of these permitted on the whole imposing the tax in real necessity and only to the extent of it. Imam al-Juwaynī went a step further in *al-Ghiyāthī* and permitted that on a permanent and fixed basis also, if the need of the public treasury was fixed and permanent; for he said after discussing this subject in sections: So I say — and God is the one whose help is sought — there must be the imposing of funds which the imam sees as fulfilling the regular provisions, or close to them…

If the jurists were beset by fear on the part of the commanders of tyranny that they would take the ruling of permissibility in itself as a means of burdening the subjects with tolls and unjust levies, how much more must we fear today, when fraud and embezzlement have spread through governmental bodies, state funds are squandered recklessly, a considerable part of the state treasury is devoted merely to the rulers' luxury and comfort, and exorbitant costs and huge expenditures are spent on conferences that yield no benefit and visits that bring the state no return. Add to that the many enormous expenses of the royal palace which take an ample share of the budget, in which — regrettably — the Islamic states have preceded many non-Islamic states!

So if a government undertook reducing these extravagant, empty expenditures, and imposed strict, continuous oversight on the officials of the government and its employees to protect the funds of the state and safeguard its treasury from fraud and embezzlement, that would go far towards making it unnecessary to burden the subjects with crushing taxes and charging them with heavy levies.

Nevertheless, it is an undeniable fact that modern civilization has generated many expenditures which have become virtually necessary for living on earth with honour and dignity, and which cannot be included among luxury, extravagance and indulgence — in addition to governments today undertaking to provide many services to the subjects which in bygone times they did not provide and were not committed to, like the service of electricity and gas for example. There are fields whose expenditures have increased today over what they were before by much, like arming the defence sector and supplying it with modern war technologies and equipment, and constructing paved streets organized on a wide scale, and acquiring the media alongside transport and communications, and the costs of the education and health sectors. As for the jizya — on granting its application — its amount is small and will not fulfil these enormous expenditures. As for booty and the *fayʾ*, funding these sectors from them is almost, if not entirely, impossible; because they are only obtained in the wake of success in wars with the unbelievers, while wars today in themselves leave whoever plunges into them — even if he returned with success — empty-handed, exhausted of strength.

So it is concluded from all this that Islamic government founded on straight foundations will need — at least at the beginning of its era — to cover its necessary expenditures by burdening the subjects with "the emergency tax"; but this tax, in view of the details just presented, will be low and easy to bear, if it undertook imposing it with caution and scrupulousness and succeeded in reducing the royal expenditures and the empty governmental burdens that have no meaning except making the commanders' living luxurious and comfortable. On another side, if it followed the divine rulings and submitted to the law of His Book and the Sunna of His Messenger in complete and perfect submission, it would find blessings from heaven and earth beyond all counting. Is it not enough as evidence of that what the Mighty One said: **"And if only the people of the cities had believed and feared God, We would have opened upon them blessings from the heaven and the earth"** [al-Aʿrāf, 7:96].

### 13 – The rights of non-Muslim minorities in Islam

There has been broadcast in the world today propaganda against Islam that it does not protect the rights of the non-Muslim citizens who inhabit the Islamic states as minorities; and it is only fabricated propaganda that has no share of truth nor portion of seriousness. For Islam, even if it is a universal call to divine unity addressing all the children of Adam to embrace it and submit to it, it calls people to that on the strength of evidence and the soundness of proof, so that they reflect and deliberate on its teachings and accept it with insight and understanding from them; and it has never permitted its followers to compel anyone to embrace it and belong to its religion. So here is the Noble Qur'an

[p. 268] calling out with that openly, saying: **"There is no compulsion in religion; the right course has become clear from the wrong. So whoever disbelieves in Ṭāghūt and believes in God has grasped the most trustworthy handhold"** [al-Baqara, 2:256].

Human history bears witness that the Muslims held fast to this divine ruling with the utmost tenacity; they did not use the weapon of force in compelling people to accept Islam, even in the most flourishing of its ages and the most advanced of its eras. Rather the most splendid example of tolerance is established from them: their judge permitted those who entered Islam compelled to return to their religion if they wished. Look at what Ibn Kathīr narrates to us in his history in the events of the year six hundred and eighty, saying: "And in Shawwāl a council was held because of the People of the Book of the dhimmīs who had become Muslim against their will; and a group of the muftis had written for them that they were compelled, so they were entitled to return to their religion; and the compulsion was established before the judge Jamāl al-Dīn ibn Abī Yaʿqūb al-Mālikī; so most of them returned to their religion."¹

From this, the unbelievers who inhabit the Islamic state — after they have taken upon themselves the covenant with the state — become like the rest of its Muslim citizens: they have of civil rights what they [the Muslims] have, and it is upon the state to protect them and defend them as it does for the Muslims. Indeed, it is right for us to say: that the protection of rights and the safeguarding of interests which Islam has granted to citizens not belonging to it and who inhabit its state as a minority are such that no other religion has ever equalled or approached them. The duty of each of the two parties — the state and the non-Muslim minority — is clear from the terms Islam has assigned to the latter: "*al-muʿāhad*" (the one under covenant) or "*al-dhimmī*" (the one under protection). *Al-muʿāhad* expresses the duty incumbent on him to live in the state peacefully and safely, without causing any harm to the state or its interests; and *al-dhimmī* reflects the guarantee (*dhimma*) the state has given him for the protection of his life, his property and his honour.

Among that is what Imam Abū Dāwūd narrated with his chain from the Messenger of God ﷺ that he said: "Indeed, whoever wrongs one under covenant (*muʿāhad*), or diminishes [his right], or charges him beyond his capacity, or takes something from him without the consent

### The jizya in Islam

We grant that Islamic government imposes a kind of taxes on its non-Muslim citizens called "the jizya" — which Western researchers sometimes take as [grounds] for impugning Islam and degrading it, as being a conduct involving discrimination among citizens on the basis of their religions and their schools, with the reasoning that the unbelievers alone are singled out for imposing it and burdening them with it, to the exclusion of the Muslims. The truth is that if they had looked at it with the eye of fairness, far from blind religious partisanship, they would have discovered what is in imposing it of the interests and privileges which the unbelievers obtain to the exclusion of the Muslims. That is because Islamic government receives from the Muslim citizens the zakat of their wealth, whose percentage ranges between 2.5% minimum and 10% maximum — and it is higher than the percentage of the jizya which the unbelievers are committed to paying by much. Second, in return for this small amount, the Islamic state undertakes the responsibility of protecting them — their lives, honour and property; just as it exempts from the jizya the insolvent, needy among them; indeed it provides for them from the public treasury their provisions, as passed in what we narrated of the story of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, with an old Jew, and his giving him provision, and his ordering the removal of the jizya from him and his like.

There is another point which explains what we are saying more fully and clearly, namely that the Islamic state, if the unbelievers attacked it, then it is permissible for the imam to command all his Muslim subjects to contribute to jihad against the enemy and to defend the heartland of Islam and the sanctity of the lands; and it is not permissible for him to charge the unbelievers with defending themselves and their lands — that is, protecting it and defending it; rather their protection and defence of them in such a case is obligatory on the Islamic government towards them, in return for what it receives from them of the sums of the jizya. Despite that, the kinds of ministries clarified above are not built on a text from the Qur'an or the Prophetic Sunna; they are only the offspring of situations and circumstances and the outcome of events and incidents. The principle in that is that the caliph has the right to grant someone all that he has of powers and authorities, or something of them; just as he has the right also not to delegate to anyone anything of that. The upshot is that employing ministers and appointing them is nothing but an administrative matter on which the Sharia did not decide a decisive matter; rather it permitted the caliph to follow in it the course which he sees as easiest administratively, as long as his government is administered on fixed Islamic foundations and principles.

It is regarded as most appropriate today, in view of contemporary circumstances and situations, that there be defined by way of the constitution or the law all that the kingdom needs of ministries and departments and the extent of their influence and the scope of their powers.

[p. 278] and branches have arisen in response to the needs of the age and the requirements of the time. So it is permitted today also that Islamic government take of them whatever it wishes, on condition that it be out of real necessity and need.

### 14 [sic] – Regional autonomy in Islam

Among the burning issues that attract the attention of contemporary governments, and around whose solution the dispute intensifies and the clash increases, is the issue of the autonomy and local government of the regions and provinces belonging to a state. We would like to list in this regard also several basic matters:

**First** — and before everything — we repeat here also what we said more than once: that Islam has not legislated a decisive ruling concerning the type of government, whether federal or unitary. So the rulings of Islam do not form any obstacle in the way of implementing the method which the Muslims have chosen or will choose in view of the interests of their age and the requirements of their times.

**Second**: as we said in discussing nationalism, Islam does not support the idea of founding a state on the basis of sharing in language, lineage and colour; nevertheless regional autonomy and local government for provinces are not among what contradicts the principles of Islam altogether, if several conditions are observed in it.
