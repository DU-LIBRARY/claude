# PART ONE

## Chapter One: The Origin and Development of the State

### Introduction

[p. 27] Since the study of government and its systems is among the most important subjects that political science undertakes to study, the first thing its scholars and philosophers attended to was the discussion of the origin and development of the state, answering in this several questions: How did authority appear in simple human societies? How did man join the ranks of government? In this regard, from the earliest time that humanity knew political science, several theories have been discovered, which we must review here — even if only briefly — because of their prominent role in creating and shaping the political systems in the world that followed them.

It should be known that the theories we are about to explain are merely the product of the ideas of people who were bare of divine revelation and deprived of heavenly guidance, relying in them on their pure rationalism and on whatever analogies and conjectures they found palatable. Then, when they arrived at some theory on the basis of analogy and conjecture, they searched for evidence for it, and so supported and strengthened it.

### The First Theory: The Social Contract Theory

The first of the theories that prevailed and became famous in political science in this regard is the theory of the social contract, designated in English by the term "Social Contract". In summary: that human beings, in their earliest period on earth, lived an individual life without being governed by a government, founded upon a state, or bound by a law or committed to a constitution; rather, their concern was concentrated on self-interest. This condition of theirs is called in the terminology of political science "the original condition" (State of Nature). (Those who advanced this idea had no evidence for what they said; but since they refused to submit to heavenly guidance, they discovered by the toil of their minds that human beings must have passed a period in which they were not divided into two groups, ruler and ruled — what they called the original condition.) And since this pervasive anarchic condition was a likely site of conflict and collision between [p. 28] the interests of human individuals — such that the interest of one required him to do something contrary to the interest of the other — disputes broke out among them and quarrels occurred. So they gathered to think about establishing a system that all of them would follow, which would protect them and their interests and protect them from conflict when interests clashed. Their thinking led them to agree to live under a mechanism that would impose laws through a ruling authority; and through this social agreement authority appeared in the world.

In sum, this theory holds that human beings themselves, by way of contracting among themselves, established the system of authority to protect their interests. And although this theory — the social contract theory — became famous through its interpreters mentioned below in the fifteenth century CE [sic], the researcher will find its conceptions in the writings and works of the ancient philosophers as well.

Curiously, however, on the basis of this one theory two political systems were later established that conflict clearly with each other and contradict each other explicitly, whose paths diverge and whose effects differ in every way: namely, the doctrine of absolute rule and the doctrine of popular sovereignty.

In detail: many scholars of politics interpreted the social contract theory to mean that when human beings delegated — for their own interest — all their rights, powers and authority to the [ruling] authority, it became, and ought to become, a despotic authority in managing their affairs, possessing absolute sovereignty and authority.

They held to this on the grounds that the social contract was concluded among human beings in the wake of a clash of interests and a struggle of demands; for each individual human being narrows the scope of his thinking and confines himself to what achieves his self-interest and what he considers it to be. So he should not be left free to follow whatever path he wishes in determining good and evil and distinguishing between what is sound and what is corrupt. Rather, since he has delegated authority over himself to the government, its head is the one who determines good and evil and distinguishes right from wrong — not with regard to his own self-interest, but with regard to the interests of the whole people. If he decrees something, then every one of his subjects is obliged and bound to act on it, [p. 29] even if against his will.

The greatest example of the application of this principle was presented by Socrates himself, the teacher of Plato, born in Athens. He had presented in the fields of religion and ethics many theories conflicting with the deep-rooted prevailing theories before him, to the point that he contradicted idolatry and refuted it (except that he did not believe in the Hereafter, as al-Qifṭī says in *Akhbār al-Ḥukamāʾ*, 1/87). So the government put him on trial and charged him with corrupting the youth and insulting religious traditions. Socrates relied on the fact that merely presenting new theories in ethics and opposing what people had previously believed was never considered a crime; yet the government paid no heed to that, but put him behind bars, and the court sentenced him to death.

His disciples and admirers — among them great wealthy men — also arranged for him to escape: first from the prison, and second from the state of Athens, so that its authority would have no power over him; for at that time the city-state system prevailed among the Greeks. But Socrates refused their initiative, explaining that — as a citizen of this city — he had covenanted to follow the commands of authority, whatever it might be, as holding absolute and despotic authority over him. And he added: therefore, in fulfilment of that contract of mine, I prefer to remain in prison and submit to the process of execution, even if I consider it unjust and contrary to the demands of justice and the principles of fairness. And thus he carried out the death sentence on himself by drinking a cup of poison, according to the method of execution prevailing at the time. History has recorded his stance and remembered that he did not seize the opportunities to escape from prison despite their being easily available, regarding that as a betrayal of what he had covenanted with the government.

This, then, is an extreme interpretation of the theory of absolute rule on the basis of the social contract. It was its prevailing understanding until the fifteenth century CE, when France produced three specialist philosophers in the field of politics who have dominated mention and citation in the political sources written after them: Thomas Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau. [Tr.: so in the original; Hobbes and Locke were English, not French, and they belong to the 17th century, Rousseau to the 18th.] Later authors in the field of politics relied on their books so heavily that they occupied first place [p. 30] in the list of political sources and references. Although their times were close to each other, their ideas and interpretations of the social contract theory were different and divergent. Hobbes and Locke held the view of the social contract as we have mentioned, while Rousseau interpreted it in an entirely opposite way. He held that authority did not arise by itself; rather, the people imposed it on themselves through social contracting among themselves. Therefore the source of legitimacy and sovereignty for every ruler is the people itself, and it is the people who granted the rulers authority, power and strength. Accordingly, the rulers must represent the people in managing the affairs of authority by laws expressing the general will of the people. If they do not do so, and manage the system contrary to what the people desire, or in a way that conflicts with its interests and demands, that is regarded as equivalent to breaking the social contract, and the people have the right to depose them and topple their government for breaking this contract.

He rejected the idea that the social contract theory leads to absolute rule, and emphasized that government ought to conform to the will of the people itself. His philosophy helped shape the political events that ultimately led to the outbreak of the French Revolution, whose details will come later, God willing.

### The Second Theory: The Theory of Force

Some philosophers advanced another theory on the origin of the state entitled "the theory of force". In summary: the state arose on the basis of force; that is, before forming communities, people quarrelled among themselves in the absence of authority and state, and some prevailed over others, so that by virtue of his victory one became the ruler and the other the ruled.

It is expressed in English by the phrase "wars begot states". In detail, as mentioned above: the establishment of the state and authority was not born of peaceful contracting; rather, it happened by chance, as a result of the victory of some people over others in the wars that broke out and the conflicts that flared up in the wake of clashing interests.

[p. 31] Of course, the adherents of this idea do not regard obtaining authority by the use of force as in any way unjust or tyrannical. Rather, it is attributed to Aristotle that whoever prevails over someone prevails over him only by virtue of qualities and merits he possesses that qualify him to prevail, and that force is in reality a manifestation through which the dust is cleared from those qualifications of his. Hence, since his acquisition of authority by prevailing in fact goes back to a quality inherent in him, it was in no way contrary to the demands of justice and reason.

### The Third Theory: The Patriarchal or Matriarchal Theory

Its gist: the basis of the state is the family; that is, every family has a head who leads it. Then several families come together to form a tribe whose head is its ruler; then several tribes come together to form a state, and one of their members is chosen to lead it and manage the system of its government.

If we examine this theory closely, we find that it does not differ much from the social contract theory. For people — whether within the scope of the tribe or the state — when they choose one of them as a leader for themselves, choose him only by contracting among themselves; except that this contracting may be concluded verbally, or may take place among them in meaning only.

### The Fourth Theory: The Theory of Divine Origin

If we let our gaze roam over the meaning of the theories just mentioned and the bases on which they are built, we find them bare of any reasonable basis apart from flimsy conjectures and false analogies. They had no evidence whatsoever, nor any proof, that this contract actually took place among human beings, or that wars and conflicts were what produced the origin of the state. Rather, they are theories in whose wilderness they wandered aimlessly, merely because they refused to believe that God is the one who created human beings and sent them into the world to realize certain aims under the shade of rulings and particular directives. Their refusal of faith in God and in His being the Creator made them stumble in the deserts of conjecture and guesswork, until their condition became the best illustration of the words of God, glorified and exalted: **"They have no knowledge of that; they only conjecture"** [al-Jāthiya, 45:24],

[p. 32] and of His words, mighty and exalted: **"They follow nothing but conjecture, and they only guess"** [al-Anʿām, 6:116].

As for those who believe in God — and that He is the one who created the universe and all within it, and sent human beings down to earth for a particular end — they have no need of such conjectures and analogies. Take, for example, the Muslims and the People of the Book, who believe that God created Adam — the first human being — and sent him down to earth only after making him a ruler, and the rest of humanity ruled by and subordinate to him. Hence humanity never passed through a period in which it lived an anarchic life not governed by a government; or, in other words, it never encountered a condition that could be called "the original condition" (State of Nature) according to the interpretation of the philosophers who held the social contract theory. In sum, their faith in God and His appointing of the first human being as His vicegerent (*istikhlāf*) saved them from running after the conjecture and the analogy latent in the social contract theory. This is what scholars of politics designate by the term: divine origin (Divine origin).

And since this theory belongs not only to the Muslims but also to those philosophers who believe in God, their word agreed on the principle that God is the one who created man and established government and authority for him. However, there are other schools that stumbled and wandered aimlessly in interpreting this theory, going in it to the point of extremism, saying: whoever assumes authority does so only on God's behalf, so human beings have no right ever to depose him. Indeed, some of them went even further, saying: when a tyrannical ruler is set over a people who abuses them and brings afflictions and calamities upon them, that is in reality a punishment from God that has descended upon them for their evil deeds. And since it is a punishment from God in which His will is manifested, the servants should not strive to remove him from the seat of government, even by safe, peaceful means; for that would be equivalent to trying to repel God's decree and opposing His will. From this they derived the rule that whoever ascends the throne of authority must be obeyed and submitted to, for in that lies God's will and by it His love is attained.

In truth, this problem arose only from their failure to distinguish between God's religious rulings (*aḥkām dīniyya*) and His cosmic will (*mashīʾa kawniyya*), although there is a great difference between the two. There is no doubt that God the Exalted sometimes [p. 33] sets a people of evil governors over them because of their evil deeds — as God mentioned at the beginning of Sūrat al-Isrāʾ, that when the Children of Israel spread corruption in the land and became exceedingly arrogant in it, twice God sent against them servants of His of great might, who disgraced them and utterly destroyed all that they had taken over.

And as it is reported that the Prophet ﷺ said: "Your deeds are your governors." [Tr.: i.e. "your rulers are a reflection of your deeds".]

However, this is God's cosmic will, and it does not mean that people should be content with it and not strive to replace them with righteous governors. Rather, the religious rulings legislated by God require them in such a case to turn back from their errant ways, trying to reform the system. They must depose the government and topple its system by whatever attempts are possible for them within the framework of safe, peaceful mechanisms. As for fighting (armed rebellion against him), it is forbidden in the general run of cases, because it leads to bloodshed among the Muslims and other evils that are among the most harmful to the Umma, and which the Sharia has forbidden to the Muslims so long as they can find a way to avoid it — as will come in detail in the second part of the book, God willing.

But removing him by peaceful means is among the duties of the Muslims. Its example is thus like an illness that befalls someone: there is no doubt that it is by God's will, and no doubt also that it is often a punishment for sins and evil deeds; yet treating it is in no way opposition to God's good pleasure or contrary to His will. Rather, God, glorified be He, Himself commands, in such a situation, seeking forgiveness and seeking treatment by whatever means are permitted by the Sharia.

So this has been a quick passing look at those political theories that are researched and debated in political science, and which later became the basis of the various systems of government.

## Chapter Two: An Introduction to the Various Political Systems

### Introduction

[p. 35] The second topic to which we would like to turn our attention is an introduction to the political systems the world has witnessed and that have prevailed in it, irrespective of the political theories explained in the first chapter. By political system (Political System) we mean: the system that determines the course of political life in terms of the form of government — whether individual or collective — the merits and practices of the government, the method of its formation, and so on. We can divide all the political systems the world has known up to this day of ours into three categories: monarchy, aristocracy and democracy. From these three, other branches emerged as new forms of government, and we cannot avoid briefly introducing these three systems in order to reach the core of the main subject of the book.

### (1) Monarchy

Perhaps the oldest of the systems of government and the most influential and dominant in the world is the monarchical system of government, for it has continued in human societies from the beginning until this day of ours in its various forms and diverse shapes. Indeed, it had dominance and influence in most periods of human history. Although this word in reality means a kind of government headed by a king, whose government is individual and in which the king alone exercises rule, the patterns of its application in the external world have never been uniform; rather, they have differed and varied with differing times and countries, so that it too has come to have many types:

**(1) The system of absolute monarchical rule**

Among its most important features is that the king in it is not bound by any law or constitution; rather, his tongue becomes the source of law. Whatever he says takes on the character of law, without his needing in that any consultation, agreement or ratification by anyone (and even if he consulted someone in some [p. 36] cases, that was regarded as a favour from him, not an obligation). He is thus the holder of all powers in the state: he issues the laws, interprets them and undertakes their execution, and he himself undertakes judgement in resolving disputes and settling controversies, since the matter of judgement ultimately reverts to him. So his opinion is the [decisive] opinion and his word is the [final] word, whether in civil or criminal cases. In contrast to what prevails today — that the court must, in criminal cases, follow the laws that determine the punishment of the offender — the absolute ruler was never bound by such laws. Rather, if his anger flared against someone, he would order him sewn up in a donkey's skin, or order that someone be walled up alive in a wall, and that would become the law regarding that person.

This despotic absolute system is what bred tyrants such as Pharaoh and Nimrod, whose arrogance through their authority went so far that they claimed lordship!

**(2) Consultative monarchy (Counciling Monarchy)**

Its meaning: that the king himself holds the legislative and executive powers in it, but he has a council (*shūrā*) which he consults in enacting and implementing laws and in other administrative affairs. The kinds of this council and its powers differ: there is what was called "Council" (al-majlis), and there is what was called the senate (majlis al-shuyūkh), and there is what was called by other names. Its forms also differ in terms of whether it is binding or not. In some states the king would consult the shūrā council without being bound to follow their advice as a matter of necessity or obligation, such that nothing deterred him from ruling against it and nothing prevented him from opposing it; while in other states the king was bound and strictly restricted to follow the shūrā and to issue whatever law or decision its members agreed upon.

It should be known that the cities of Greece were the most civilized and advanced cities in the world in ancient times, and the city-state system prevailed there at the time; that is, each city was an independent state with its own independent government. Cities at that time were fortified, surrounded by the walls of a strong fortress, so the inhabitants of one fortress regarded themselves as citizens of one state, [p. 37] and each city of them was self-governing and almost completely independent of the others, to the point that all the cities of Greece did not form a single federal state governed by one unified federal government. The kings of those cities followed at first the system of absolute rule, then converted it into the system of consultative monarchy, and some of them formed councils of shūrā to advise them in their various affairs. The shūrā was then binding in many of those cities, and not binding in some, while others set frameworks for it by dividing commands and issues into two kinds: those in which following the shūrā was binding and those in which it was not. This account goes back to 300–400 years before the Messiah, upon him be peace.

**(3) Religious monarchy (Religious Monarchy)**

It means: a monarchical system of government in which religion plays its role as a basic source of authority and rule. The king was chosen in it on the basis of religion, and was authenticated by the men of religion; so he remained throughout the period of his rule — and in the affairs of government — subject to them in general, in the sense that he was bound to follow the rulings of religion according to the interpretation they gave of them. This kind of monarchy prevailed among the Christians for a long period, whose beginning goes back to 300 [years] after the raising of the Messiah to heaven.

In detail: the followers of Jesus, upon him be peace, and his disciples continued to face war and aggression at the hands of their opponents, who had borne enmity towards them for three centuries. They used to propagate their religion and call people to it secretly and covertly, adhering to the pure doctrine of divine unity (*tawḥīd*) upon which Jesus, upon him be peace, had left them. During those three centuries, the hostile Jewish zealots entered their religion, led by Paul, who introduced into Christianity false doctrines such as the Trinity, redemption and the like. The struggle continued between the original Christians and the current led by Paul, until Paul's current prevailed and the followers of Jesus, upon him be peace, suffered a defeat that led to their extinction, or so few remained that they could barely suffice to resist the current. Thus Paul managed to triumph over true Christianity, distort its beliefs and corrupt [p. 38] its rulings, until the word "Christianity" came to be applied to this new religion that Paul brought into being, by which he opposed the call of Jesus, upon him be peace, and raided his teachings and his law.

In that period the Roman emperor Constantine (Constantine the Great, who founded the city of Constantinople named after him, and which became the capital of the Roman Empire) embraced the corrupted Christianity led by Paul — having previously been a pagan. His embrace of Christianity was the opening of Christianity's entry among the Romans. Pauline Christianity was led in his era by Athanasius, and it was he who conferred legitimacy on Constantine's government from the religious point of view — which was a beginning for religious monarchy among the Romans. This monarchy proceeded on the rule that purely religious affairs were delegated to the head of the Church — who was called the Pope — so that his word in them was regarded as final and decisive, while purely political affairs belonged to the king, so that his word in them was the final word. Nevertheless, the king was bound to follow the commands of the Church and was to some extent subject to its rulings, because the authority for determining the nature of affairs — whether religious or political — lay with the Pope. And since one scabbard cannot hold two swords, the question of the distribution of competences and powers between the Church and the state continued to be a pressing crisis and a great tragedy in the history of the Roman Empire, and so remained a burning, intractable problem in which the struggle raged, until democracy came and freed itself from the influence of the Church in state affairs by restricting its authority to the city of the Vatican only.

**The fourth type: Constitutional monarchy (Constitutional monarchy)**

"It is a political system in which the king rules on the basis of powers defined by a constitution." It is the opposite of the absolute monarchy mentioned earlier, since the king himself in it is not the source of legislation, completely independent in issuing rulings and taking measures; rather, his powers, the work of his government and the force of his law are defined within the framework of that constitution, from which he may not deviate even a hand-span.

It too has existed in various eras of humanity and various parts of the inhabited world. It existed in Greece for a period, and Rome too witnessed it in some periods of its history, and especially [p. 39] in the periods after the Middle Ages, when monarchy was in most cases constitutional.

Among this type is what prevailed in several states: the monarchy subordinate to the parliamentary system, as in Britain, for example, where its monarch — the Queen Elizabeth — and her kingship is ceremonial and symbolic only, while executive authority is exercised by the prime minister, the council of ministers or the house of representatives!

As you have noticed, "monarchy" is a polysemous general term (*kullī mushakkik*) that may be applied to every kind of monarchy, including absolute monarchy and ceremonial symbolic monarchy. Among kings there are those whose tongues are the source of law, and those who possess no authority at all except that they are crowned with the name of king. A brief introduction to its four types and their forms has passed before us, as they were embodied in various periods of humanity and their phases. On the other hand, the spread of democracy in the world has damaged the reputation of monarchy and distorted its image to the point that it has been regarded as an absolute evil in all its forms and shapes. Interacting with this false propaganda, we see some people negating its existence [in Islam] and rejecting it decisively from the Islamic point of view. In truth, a categorical ruling of rejection or acceptance on it absolutely is not correct; rather, it is a heading under which may fall both its sound and its corrupt kinds, and under whose concept is included what is beneficial and useful among its forms and what is harmful and injurious among its shapes.

As for the Noble Qur'an, we find it describing Ṭālūt (Saul) as king, mentioning his appointment as king in the context of bestowing favour and grace; as it also reminded the Children of Israel of His favour in making them kings: **"and made you kings"** [al-Māʾida, 5:20]. It likewise described David and Solomon as kings. Those who have turned their faces towards democracy — by their very nature of negating the existence of monarchy in Islam — have come with feeble interpretations and frigid, strained explanations in explaining this verse and interpreting the [word] monarchy in it, so as to make it agree with their purpose and suit their aim.

The truth is that the ruling on a system of government as right or wrong, or acceptance or rejection, does not turn only on its name and heading, but on its meaning and the powers of the king in it, and the foundations and principles he will follow in managing its affairs. If all this is free of anything that clouds it from [p. 40] the Sharia point of view, then the system is acceptable, and there is no justification for rejecting it in the Sharia, whatever its name and heading!

The upshot is that what prevails and has spread among us — that monarchy is an absolute evil — is nothing but the product of erroneous propaganda raised by modernist thinkers against it, out of partisanship for and preference for democracy; and we will come to its details in the coming pages, God willing. Islam does not make the ruling on a system depend on its name and heading only, but on its foundations and premises, which will be taken as basic principles in managing the helm of the system and running the wheel of government.

### (2) Aristocracy

A coined word taken from "the nobles" (*al-ashrāf*), meaning: a class of people of high status and prominent social position. In summary, this system [holds that] government and authority are not deserved by every Tom, Dick and Harry from among the rabble and riff-raff of the people; rather, they are the special preserve of a small number of citizens who are higher in status than others because of their family connections, their wealth and their particular abilities. Hence it is not the right of others to rule or seek rule.

There was also disagreement among the pioneers of this system in identifying the nobles who form the aristocratic class, and who therefore deserve rule over others by merit and worth. One said: they are those who possess lofty lineage distinguishing them from others of lowly lineage. Another said: they are those who occupy a prominent place in society from the religious point of view. And others defined them by their possession of abilities and noble human qualities, such as being the bravest and most intelligent of people. Thus their criteria for identifying the class of nobles among whom is confined the right to ascend the throne of authority and attain power and capability differed. However, all of them are united by this common measure: that authority is deserved only by a special group of people with a prominent social position distinguishing them from others; as for the rest, they have only to surrender to their right and thus submit to them and obey them.

It should be known that Aristotle and Plato were the originators of this idea and the inventors of this term, for they put forward the philosophy of intellectual aristocracy, holding that the right to rule should [p. 41] belong to those with prominent abilities and intellectual qualifications only. But this idea of theirs remained a wish and a fancy only, and was never actually realized in the external world, since no system has ever been established in which the rulers are chosen on the basis of their status and their mastery of knowledge.

It is worth noting here that aristocracy was originally applied to a form of government dominated by persons of prominent social standing. Today, however, it is also applied to the form in which this upper class does not directly exercise rule, but enjoys complete influence and full dominance over the rulers, such that it would be no exaggeration to regard them as the actual ruling party; in such a case too it is called aristocratic government.

What has become prevalent today in the use of [the word] "bureaucracy" in fact means this kind of system of government. Its gist is that administrative officials are able to dominate and take control of the system so that important government affairs are not concluded, and the state's important policies are not adopted, except in accordance with their wishes and the fulfilment of their demands; and they press the rulers so hard as to force them to follow them, to the point that they find no possibility of departing from their demands and wishes.

The word is compounded of bureau, meaning administrative officials, and (cracy) meaning rule; and the meaning of the compound is: that rule in reality belongs to the administrative officials and no one else.

With regard to identifying the upper class that deserves to rule people and lands, aristocracy has been divided into several types:

**(a) Intellectual aristocracy:** which is — as mentioned — an idea presented by Aristotle and Plato, but it never enjoyed actual application in external reality.

**(b) Racial aristocracy (Racial Aristocracy):**
It means: that the right to rule the country is confined to the members of one tribe or the members of a particular race, so that the king is only from among them.

It found currency and influence among a considerable number of nations and civilizations, and specifically in Hindu civilization, where the belief prevailed that rule was the special right of the members of [p. 42] the upper castes — Brahmans, Rajputs and Kshatriyas.

**(c) Religious aristocracy, or theocracy (Theocracy)**
A word of Greek origin, compounded of two Greek words: "theo", meaning god (from it comes the word "Theology" for divinity, i.e. the science of God), and "cracy", meaning rule. So the meaning of the word is: the rule of God.

The motive behind the launching of this system was a blessed, splendid idea: that true rule in this universe belongs to God, glorified and exalted, and therefore whoever assumes authority over people and lands should be subject to and follow the rulings of God, glorified and exalted. The question these people faced was: who has the authority to interpret the law of God, glorified and exalted, and to specify His rulings? Christianity delegated this authority to the Pope and charged him with interpreting the divine law and specifying its rulings, then informing the ruler of them so that he would be compelled to act on them and implement them. The outcome was that theocracy came in fact to be confined in its meaning to the rule of the men of religion, after it had linguistically denoted the rule of God. Hence we see that the word is usually translated today in this second sense (the rule of the men of religion), not the first (the rule of God).

As we mentioned a little earlier, the sultan's subjection to the rulings of the Pope continued to be an intractable problem in the history of the Christian governments among the Romans. It caused, on the one hand, the outbreak of partisanships and the intensification of rifts between state and Church; and on the other, since the Pope enjoyed unlimited and unrestricted authority in specifying rulings and legislating them, he was not slow to exploit it many times, on various occasions, in what was illegitimate and not beneficial — especially when this authority passed into the hands of worshippers of personal interest and servants of selfish whims, whose care for it and greed for obtaining it drove them to choose harsh plans and practise ferocious policies that made the Romans live suffocated in an atmosphere of hardship, injustice and aggression for many centuries. This ultimately resulted in the people's rebellion against religion, and consequently in the establishment of democracy on the ruins of the refutation of the Church's authority and the ending of its interference in state affairs, until [p. 43] the word "theocracy" today became an insult and a disgrace that does not strike the ears except to bring to the mind the image of those hardships and calamities that the people of Rome suffered and endured during the authority of the popes and priests.

And since the word "theocracy" has today come to be a disgrace, many Muslims have come to apply to it the ruling of categorical rejection from the Sharia point of view, ignoring and feigning ignorance of the essential difference between its original philosophy, on whose foundation it was built, and what its practical application among the Christians came to. From this we see today that whenever scholars speak of the Islamic system of government, or demand it in Pakistan, or contribute their share in politics aspiring to establish Islamic government, from the first moment the modernist secular class sets about aiming arrows at them and launching raids on them, shouting at the top of their voices with all condemnation and denunciation that these people claim to set the state alight in the fire of theocracy, and that we shall resist them and fight them, and so on and so forth... — without any of the opponents or supporters directing their attention to understanding the reality of the word and the idea at work behind it: that it in truth denotes "the rule of God", which is a great principle in Islamic politics that God has explained in an eloquent, elegant style in His words, glorified be He: **"Judgement belongs to God alone"** [al-Anʿām, 6:57; Yūsuf, 12:40, 67]. Consider, then, the gravity of the position of one who denounces it and proclaims — without awareness or insight — that it has no share in Islam! Does not his denouncing it and his judgement of its absolute prohibition entail negating the rule of God in Islam?!

Once more we clarify that theocracy in its original concept and meaning — namely that true rule in the universe belongs only to God, and that government should be subject to the divine law — is a sound, correct idea that agrees with what is established in Islam and what the Noble Qur'an has declared more than once. But when it was actually realized in the external world, it was accompanied by many evils and attended by numerous abuses that led to the distortion of its image and the ruining of its reputation. In what follows we shall turn to clarifying the distortion and disfigurement it faced at the hands of the men of the earlier religions: Judaism, Hinduism and Christianity.

#### Jewish and Hindu theocracy

[p. 44] The first theocracy to appear on the face of the earth belonged to the Jews. All the Children of Israel — before the sending of Jesus, upon him be peace — were charged with following the law of Moses, upon him be peace, revealed to him in the Torah, which they called "the Law". However, most of its rulings concerned worship, purity and the like, and few of them concerned the civil and civilizational aspect of human life. So when they resolved to establish the state and run its system on the basis of the law of the Torah — which, as mentioned, was almost devoid of civil rulings — they very often faced an issue whose ruling the Torah had not stated. So, to learn the divine rulings and enact law in their light in such cases, they delegated authority and power to the scholars and men of religion. They had a particular way of performing this task: their rabbi would sit in a tent devoted to this purpose called "the Tent of Worship" [Tr.: i.e. the Tabernacle], in which was placed an ark of a special kind, covered with a covering of gold. The rabbi would watch for suitable weather in which the flashing of lightning in the sky could be hoped for. He would sit near this ark in an attitude of watching, reciting some of its verses and invocations; when the lightning began to flash, this rabbi would rise saying that Yahweh (meaning: God) had revealed to him through this lightning the ruling of such-and-such on this issue.

Thus the belief prevailed and spread among them that Yahweh informed the rabbi of His law on new cases by speaking to him directly.

They would resort to it whenever they faced such an issue or such a situation befell them. The rabbi would take up his place in the Tent of Worship to wait for the lightning that would lead him to revelation according to their corrupt claims. If it flashed — fine; otherwise he would continue his watch to claim a ruling on the issue, alleging that it had been revealed to him or inspired to him.

It is clear that the claims of unveiling (*kashf*) and direct inspiration from God to their rabbi were nothing but fancies and illusions not based on firm evidence or sound reasoning; rather, it was greed for preserving their leadership and continuing their headship that led them to invent them.
