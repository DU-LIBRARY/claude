# PART TWO: The Islamic Principles of Politics

### Introduction

[p. 159] Having finished studying the various political ideas and systems, the time has come for us, in this part of the book, to acquaint ourselves with the guidance and teachings of Islam in the field of politics. It will include several discussions related to the subject, arranged in six chapters whose headings are as follows:

**Chapter One: Politics in Islam.** In it we shall first clarify the position of Islam on politics and its activities in the light of the Qur'an and Sunna, and follow it by defining the nature of the rulings of Islam in the field of politics and their manner — whether it contented itself with laying down general rules, or mentioned their partial details as well.

**Chapter Two:** Government and its functions in Islam.
**Chapter Three:** Clarifying the teachings of Islam concerning the formation of government.
**Chapter Four:** Explaining its teachings concerning the administration of government.
**Chapter Five:** Detailing the rulings of Islam in the two fields of international relations and defence.
**Chapter Six:** A study of the questions of overthrowing the government and removing the ruler.

## Chapter One: Politics in Islam

We shall speak in it of two important topics of extreme gravity for proceeding in the subject:

### 1 – Islam's position on politics

[p. 160] Two extreme ideas have spread in this regard, whose adherents have overstepped the path of justice towards the two extremes of excess and neglect. One of them: the separation between religion and politics — which is the secular idea that confines the role of Islam — like other religions — to the personal life of man and his individual affairs, and does not grant it any connection with political questions or the affairs of the state. What drove them to it was only to rid themselves of the interference of religion in political affairs, given what they had seen and suffered at the hands of Christian theocracy — the government founded on the basis of the Christian religion — of evils and afflictions, as you know from what we mentioned earlier.

Secular democracy, whose spread and dominance in the world have been granted, contributed to it side by side, since it arose from it — indeed it has become a part of it that does not separate from the conception of some of its concepts in any situation. It was also reinforced by the conduct of some religious circles, which actually narrowed their activities to the fields of creed and worship and gave most of their attention to refining and purifying morals only. Indeed, in addition to that, they undertook to direct criticism and blame at every one of the men of religion who delved into politics and contributed to its works and activities — as though they believed religion and politics to be two contradictories that can never meet in one thing.

This erroneous idea had no basis or source other than that they drew an analogy between Islam and other religions whose teachings were confined to a few fields of creed, worship and morals, not extending to the other branches of life. And it is a definitely false analogy; for Islam is not like other religions whose framework is limited to what we have mentioned of fields. Rather, God has distinguished it from among them with many features, among which is that it is marked by comprehensiveness and adorned with generality. It is a general and comprehensive religion that grants its followers valuable teachings and sound directives in all the fields of life and its branches, including economics and politics as well (and we shall mention some of its rulings in the field of politics in what follows, God willing). Hence denying its rulings in the field of politics is nothing but a denial of the belief that it is a religion God has endowed with perfection and comprehensiveness; for it is impossible that a religion be described as perfect [p. 161] while it has left its followers neglected in a great branch of human life such as politics.

**The second idea:** it is the idea of excess regarding the standing of politics in Islam. That is because some went deep into refuting the aforementioned secular idea, a going-deep that led them to regard politics as the ultimate goal sought from religion, and all other rulings as an instrument for achieving this goal and hence subject to it. This idea is embodied in the prevalent illusion that Islam did not come into the world except for the purpose of granting it a just political system and establishing one in it. From this starting point, the ideal successful Muslim according to the adherents of this idea is the one who exerts his efforts for the sake of raising high the word of religion through the path of politics only. As for those working in various religious fields and occupied with other religious services, they are in their eyes reactionaries and laggards who struggle in other than a struggle, inattentive to what their religion requires of them.

Both of these ideas deviate from the path of what is right towards the side of excess and neglect. Their source is error in understanding the correct, middle and balanced position of Islam on politics, which consists in believing that Islam is not like other religions in neglecting the side of politics and not attending to its rulings at all; rather it has granted its followers sound rulings and right directives concerning politics as well. But — despite that — regarding politics as an original aim and goal sought from Islam, and consequently subjecting all other rulings to it, is also an error.

An example of that: trade. Islam has attended to it with every attention, since it guided to its rulings and teachings with elaboration and detail, as a branch of life that the Muslim encounters in exercising his human needs — not as being the whole of religion. Likewise marriage: the Islamic Sharia has detailed all its questions and explained all its rulings; yet despite that it would not be correct to say that marriage is the whole of religion or its original aim. And politics is like these two: we grant that it — being a branch of life — Islam attended to it — as a complete, comprehensive religion — so it guided its followers to its general rules and basic principles. But it does not follow from this merely that politics is the whole of religion or its original aim.

[p. 162] And going deep into the details of the subject, we say: the noble verse **"And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me"** [al-Dhāriyāt, 51:56] tells explicitly of the purpose for which man was created, namely to perform the worship of God, glorified and exalted. And worship means submission and obedience; so it includes all the legitimate ways of showing submission — that is, the utmost self-abasement — on the one hand, and performing obedience to God and following His teachings in all the fields of life on the other.

To clarify that: human beings are all servants of God, glorified and exalted; but there is a difference between servitude to creation and servitude to the Creator. The owned slave of a human being has as his function to perform obedience to his master in all that he commands him and forbids him only, without its being required of him to worship him and abase himself before him. As for the servants of God, glorified and exalted, they are charged with both matters: showing the utmost self-abasement to Him and complying with His commands in all the fields of life. Hence the performance of this function of theirs is called "worship" (*ʿibāda*), whereas the obedience of an owned slave to his human master is not called "worship".

Then let it be known that worship is of two kinds:

**(1) Worship for its own sake and without intermediary:** it is that which has been laid down as pure worship for its own sake, such that there is no purpose in performing it other than devotion and self-abasement to God, glorified and exalted — like prayer, fasting, pilgrimage, zakat, the sacrifice and so on.

**(2) Worship for the sake of something else:** it is that which is not, in terms of its original intended purpose, of the side of devotion; rather its doer performs it to fulfil a worldly need or desire, but he observes in it the limits of the Sharia, follows in it the Sunna of the Messenger ﷺ and intends by it to please the Lord, glorified and exalted. It is then regarded, in his case, as a worship for which God will reward him. Such is trade, for example: man engages in it only to fulfil by it the needs of himself and his dependants; but if he complies in it with all the rulings of the Sharia related to it, seeking God's pleasure, trade turns in his case from a profession that brings him his provision into a worship for which he is rewarded and recompensed with God, glorified and exalted. However, it is a worship of the second kind, not the first; for it was not a worship in itself; rather it became a worship in the case of its doer by his performing it observing all the teachings of the Sharia and seeking by it the pleasure of God, glorified and exalted. And politics and government are likewise; [p. 163] they become a worship for which a person is rewarded if he is occupied with them and performs their activities according to the rulings of the Sharia and seeking the pleasure of God, glorified and exalted. Like trade, they are not worships in themselves; they were only counted among worships for the sake of observing the rulings of the Sharia in them and making the intention sincere in performing them.

So the purpose for which man was created is the worship of God, glorified and exalted, in both of the kinds mentioned above — worship for its own sake and worship for the sake of something else — and it is not correct to confine it to one of these two kinds nor to some of one of them or both of them. And self-evidently it is judged that the first kind is better and higher than the second, and that the second kind is not manifested in politics only; rather it has many individual instances and numerous forms. These two kinds, with their individual instances and forms, together constitute the meaning of worship which was the target of the creation of man. So from where, then, is it correct to narrow the meaning of worship and confine it to politics, which is one of these kinds?!

However, there is an important point to which we must draw your attention, namely that the kinds of worship of the second type differ among themselves in standing and importance from the point of view of the Sharia: whichever of them is more general in effect and wider in scope is regarded as among the most important in standing and greatest in gravity. And politics is likewise; for Islamic government — founded on straight lines through the application of a political system that agrees with the requirements of the Sharia — does not play its role only in enabling the Muslims to improve the performance of worships of both kinds and to perform them in a better way; rather it also provides them — alongside that — with opportunities to spread them and to widen the horizons of acting on them to the extent of the reach of that authority's influence. So its superiority over others among the individual instances of the second kind of worships is not denied. From this angle it is correct to emphasize it and draw attention to it. As for regarding it alone as an original aim of religion, that leads only to anarchy and the inversion of priorities and tasks in religion; for rooting this idea in someone's mind leads to entanglement in many Sharia evils, some of whose explanation we summarize for you below:

**The first:** that it causes disparagement of the works that are regarded as original worships in themselves; for raising the rank of politics to become an aim sought from religion entails lowering the ranks of [p. 164] these worships so that they become subject to it, and intended by religion as subordinate and in the second degree. And it is an error contradicted by what is known from the Noble Qur'an: that worships in themselves are what are intended originally, while empowerment (*tamkīn*) and politics are like means. God the Exalted said: **"[They are] those who, if We establish them in the land, establish prayer and give zakat and enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong"** [al-Ḥajj, 22:41]. For it clearly indicates that establishing prayer and giving zakat and other things mentioned in the verse in the context of the apodosis [of the conditional] are the aims and the intended matters, and that empowerment is nothing but a means to achieve this goal and attain this aim.

Some have argued for empowerment being the original intended aim from a verse of Sūrat al-Nūr in which God, glorified and exalted, said: **"God has promised those who have believed among you and done righteous deeds that He will surely grant them succession [to authority] upon the earth just as He granted it to those before them, and that He will surely establish for them their religion which He has preferred for them, and that He will surely substitute for them, after their fear, security; [for] they worship Me, not associating anything with Me"** [al-Nūr, 24:55].

The Sage of the Umma, His Eminence Shaykh Mawlānā Ashraf ʿAlī al-Thānawī (may God have mercy on him), has spared us plunging into answering this argument, since he came in this regard with a satisfying, convincing answer and clear, sincere words that cure the sick and quench the thirsty. We present to you below the translation of its Urdu text:

He (may God have mercy on him) said: "The noble verse: **'[They are] those who, if We establish them in the land, establish prayer and give zakat and enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong; and to God belongs the outcome of [all] matters'** [al-Ḥajj, 22:41] — it becomes clear from it that the original intent is the religious matters [i.e. the matters of worship which are performed as religion and devotion to God, glorified and exalted], not the political matters [i.e. the Sharia rulings related to politics], nor jihad; rather these two play the role of means for empowerment in performing these worships. From this starting point we see that the prophets, upon them be peace, all had the rulings of worships legislated for them, while jihad and politics were not legislated except when need called for them or interest required them [because they are two means]; and means are like that: they are not resorted to except when there is need.

[p. 165] And perhaps some are seized by a doubt that there is another verse that contradicts this position and indicates the opposite of this matter — that worships are a means and empowerment and politics an aim — namely His words: **'God has promised those who have believed among you and done righteous deeds that He will surely grant them succession upon the earth just as He granted it to those before them, and that He will surely establish for them their religion which He has preferred for them'** [al-Nūr, 24:55]; for it mentioned faith and righteous deeds as a condition upon which empowerment in the land follows, which indicates that empowerment is the original intent.

And its answer is that in it there is a promise of empowerment and power [as a reward] for faith and righteous deeds, and it mentions in it that power follows from religion as a characteristic [of it]. So politics and power are two matters that were promised on account of faith and righteous deeds; and a thing, if it is promised, it does not follow that it is intended also — otherwise it would follow that abundance of provision is an original aim of religion, since it was promised for the establishment of the Torah, the Gospel and the Qur'an, i.e. acting by: **'And if only they upheld [the law of] the Torah, the Gospel, and what has been revealed to them from their Lord, they would have consumed [provision] from above them and from beneath their feet'** [al-Māʾida, 5:66]. Does anyone say this?! No — rather it is among His promise, God promised it to the religious among them that they would not remain hungry and naked. [So know that] what is promised need not be intended. And what we are concerned with is likewise: politics and power are two matters that were promised in order to follow as a characteristic upon faith and righteous deeds, not because they are an original aim of it.

At any rate, know that the original intent of religion is worships, and politics and empowerment are a means to them. But let it be noted that the purpose of our speech is nothing but to explain the ranks of these two matters in Islam: that religious matters are what are intended originally, while politics is nothing but a means to them. We do not mean by that that politics is not sought in religion at all, nor in any degree."¹

> ¹ *Ashraf al-Sawāniḥ*, section "Khātimat al-Sawāniḥ", vol. 4, pp. 28–29, Multan printing.

The upshot is that whoever is dominated by this idea will hardly fail to take on a political character, and so he looks at worships with a political view, trying to dye them with a political colour and to interpret the purpose of [p. 166] their legislation so that it becomes a means of attaining "the highest goal" embodied in power and empowerment. And that actually happened to them, when they said: the original purpose of the legislation of prayer lies in creating collective thought concerning the attainment of political aims, accustoming the Muslims to observing order and coordination, and strengthening ties and paving ways of cooperation and mutual support among them, so that their efforts unite and their word agrees to work towards achieving this sought aim. And that zakat aims by its legislation at reviving and stirring up the sentiment of spending; and fasting aims by its legislation at training to bear want and suffer poverty, so that the Muslim is in full readiness from it if it befalls him on the way to attaining power: the aim sought from religion. As for the pilgrimage, behind its obligation it aspires to play the role of a world Islamic conference, and hence to bring about a coalition and agreement among the Muslims who represent their countries from various parts of the world.

This is what they said concerning the main worships in Islam, focusing their ambitions on their worldly interests which we do not doubt are attained and obtained from them. But we say: they are benefits that follow from them as a subordinate matter; they are not the purposes behind their legislation originally. As for the spirit of these worships and the original intent of them, it is the strengthening of the bond of the creature with the Creator by way of turning in repentance and humbling oneself before Him, and hence giving precedence to obeying Him and choosing it in every matter, even at the cost of harming one's material interests. And regarding them as means to attain political aims and hence subjecting them to them leads only to extinguishing this spirit and killing these sentiments. That suffices as ugliness and disgrace!

**Second:** If these worships were regarded as an instrument and means to attain that sought aim, it would be natural that it be permitted to sacrifice these if need called for them in achieving those aims, and to give precedence to the interests of those over the interests of these whenever interests conflict between the two parties. From this there would be no harm in abandoning congregational prayer and attendance at the mosque if it were for the sake of political movements and meetings. Indeed, they might even permit missing the prayer for this purpose; and likewise they permitted committing some Sharia disapproved acts (*makrūhāt*) if a person found no alternative to them for the sake of attaining this aim!

[p. 167] **Third:** Disparagement of these worships has led them to believe that those who perform them abundantly and call others to them out of desire for their merits are inattentive to the original intent of the Sharia; indeed they may deal with them with contempt and mockery merely because they have abandoned the field of politics and occupied themselves with these worships. From this they do not reckon with the books of the merits of deeds nor give them weight, nor do they see any need for reading them for the sake of acting on them. Perhaps what trickles from their style concerning these books is that they count them among the obstacles that turn the Muslims away from "their original aim", and hence they see no need for their existence among the Muslims. Add to that that they expressed the attempts at purifying the soul [*tazkiya*] as "opium". The matter has even gone so far with them that they have not left the practising scholars devoted to the Sharia sciences, and their students who acquire them, either; rather they have accused them of reaction and backwardness and judged them to be trying to plough in thorns and briars and to be struggling in other than a struggle, and that they are still deprived of the correct conception of religion and its principles!

**Fourth:** This idea entails believing that most of the prophets sent, upon them be peace, were unsuccessful in their mission that was entrusted to them from God, glorified and exalted. For none of them was able to found a government and acquire authority except a few — among them, in addition to our master Muhammad ﷺ: our masters Joseph, Moses, Samuel, David and Solomon, God's blessings and peace upon them all. It is not established of others besides them that they founded a state or attained a government. And uttering the disappointment of these in performing their duty or their failure in completing their task is daring of any kind — God forbid that! As for those who have turned their faces towards politics as the only goal sought from religion, they do not shy away from saying so!

In sum, the foregoing detail [shows] that politics has its standing in religion, but regarding it as an original aim of religion leads to disorder in the ordering of its priorities and its tasks of every kind. On the other hand, confining religion to prayer and fasting and neglecting its teachings in the other branches of life is also a gross error.

The truth of the matter is what we have stated more than once: that religion has branches and fields among which [p. 168] is politics too; so removing it from religion and rejecting its connection with it absolutely is error and misguidance.

Then, it is obligatory to take religion and its teachings with regard and compliance in all fields of life, and it does not allow confining it to some of them to the exclusion of others. As for confining efforts and activities to one field of it, that is of the kind of the principle of distributing works and sharing them among the members of the Umma, such that some occupy themselves with one branch to which they give their attention and expend in its way their efforts and struggle, and do not withhold in its service their dear and cheap, and others with another likewise. And choosing a branch of religion to this extent only is no harm in the Sharia; rather coordination among all sides will help the affairs of religion to run on its wheels with all precision and mastery. The problem occurs when each group begins to tread the path of exaggeration in what it has chosen of the branches of religion and raising its standing to the point that it believes it is the whole of religion, and lowering the value of the other branches to the point that it strips them and excludes them from religion.

So there is no harm, then, in someone choosing — in view of his circumstances and qualifications — to undertake the service of religion in the field of politics and by way of it, then practising its activities according to the rulings of the Sharia. There is no doubt that he contributes his share in serving a branch of religion; but he has no right to say that politics alone is religion and that he alone is the one undertaking the service of religion. Rather, he must acknowledge with broad-mindedness the services of others besides him who are occupied in other fields and religious domains.

### 2 – The nature of the rulings of Islam concerning politics

The second matter we would like to clarify in this regard is that the teachings and rulings of Islam concerning politics are limited to principles and general rules only. As for the implementation of those rules and their application in practice in external reality, Islam has not defined for it a special form; rather it has entrusted it to the scholars of the Sharia and those expert in it in every age, to choose for it a form that agrees with the rulings of the Sharia on one hand and harmonizes with the requirements of that age on the other. And for further clarification we say: the principles and general rules that God has legislated concerning politics are fixed, not changing with the passage of ages and the succession of eras; but if the people of insight among the Muslims and those of [p. 169] complete knowledge among them of the Sharia were to undertake — by consultation among themselves — the formation of a formula and system to act on in the light of those principles and observing them fully, it would be acceptable in the Sharia.

And as an example of that let us take the noble verse: **"And prepare against them whatever you are able of power"** [al-Anfāl, 8:60]. God commanded the Muslims in it to prepare what was within their capacity of equipment to resist the unbelievers and fight them. And although He mentioned in the same verse something or two things of the kinds of power, that was not by way of confining power and defining its kinds; rather He entrusted that to those of understanding among the Muslims to undertake — in the light of their experiences — preparing what they see as suitable in view of their circumstances and needs, the requirements of their age and the capabilities of their enemy.

And politics is likewise: Islam has contented itself with legislating its general rules. As for its particulars — the number of government departments and ministries, the distribution of administrative authorities, the specification of its type between unitary and federal, and the definition of the legislative chamber whether one or two, and the system of consultation in it — Islam has not defined anything of that; rather these are permissible matters (*mubāḥāt*) which the Sharia has entrusted to people of insight among the Muslims to choose from them what is most suitable to their circumstances and most fitting for their age. It is then futile to look, behind the discussion of the principles of Islam in politics, for explicit statement of the details of these particulars in the words of the jurists and mujtahids, since they did not undertake to explain them, because the Sharia did not define particular forms for them — as with the covenant in other permissible matters, since it — out of its conviction of the sufficiency of human reason and its qualifications in them — usually does not legislate particular rulings concerning them; rather it entrusts them to human reason to choose among them what is most beneficial and most fitting for it. The Sharia only comes with decisive legislation obliging to take it, without there being any alternative to abandoning or changing it, in the matters that are likely sites for the slip and error of human reason, which falls short of perceiving its dimensions if it were left free to judge on them. So the Sharia guides it in them to what it is most likely in its good, lest it stumble in its wilderness wandering and stumbling.

From this we can say that the rulings of the Sharia related to politics are distinguished by both of these two features: firmness in its basic principles and its general rules which God guaranteed to lay down [p. 170] and legislate on the one hand, and flexibility in applying them through choosing the formula for acting on them, such that it can choose for every age and city — within the framework of those principles — what is most in harmony with its requirements and most useful for its interests on the other.

And the import of the words is that when we speak of Islamic politics we do not mean by it a particular formula for the administration of government whose every detail has been specified with all precision and firmness, without there being in it any room for change. Rather we only mean by it its basic principles and its general rules that were legislated in the Book of God and the Sunna of His Messenger ﷺ — which we shall try to clarify in the coming pages, God the Exalted willing.

## Chapter Two: Government and Its Functions in Islam

### Preface

We cannot have a correct, serious understanding of the rulings and principles of Islam concerning politics as long as Islam's vision of government and its correct conception of it are not clear to us, and as long as we remain ignorant of its functions and the aims it aims at behind its establishment. We undertook in the first part of this book to review the many points of view that a group of philosophers presented about the beginning of government and the aims sought from it, conjecturing in them various kinds of conjectures and wandering in them in the valleys of empty analogies and flimsy guesses. As for the Islamic point of view in this regard, it differs from them entirely, as we shall clarify in what follows, God willing. And there is no escape for us from knowing it with precision and mastery so that it becomes easy for us, through it, to root the political principles of Islam.

### Belief in "the Supreme Sovereignty of God": the basis of the Islamic conception of government

Among the most important principles of Islamic thought concerning government, and which we can regard as the foundation of foundations in the field of Islamic politics, is the belief and acceptance of supreme sovereignty and [p. 171] absolute sovereignty (*al-siyāda*) for God, glorified and exalted, in the sense that He, mighty and exalted, is the true Ruler, and human beings only rule over their fellow human beings in subordination to that sovereignty.

It is the basic foundation in which there is no scope for disagreement and no room for ijtihad; it cannot be ignored, nor can there be mutual understanding or agreement to the contrary at any stage. It is the first clause with which the constitution of Islamic politics opens. The Qur'an has explained it with all clarity and explicitness in various styles and different words; so it said once: **"Judgement belongs to God alone"** [al-Anʿām, 6:57].

And it said another time: **"Unquestionably, His is the judgement"** [al-Anʿām, 6:62].
And it expressed it in one place with His words: **"Unquestionably, His is the creation and the command"** [al-Aʿrāf, 7:54].
And in another with His words: **"And to God belongs the dominion of the heavens and the earth"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:189].
And with: **"Say: O God, Owner of dominion, You give dominion to whom You will"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:26].

These verses all indicate explicitly that sovereignty (*al-ḥākimiyya*) and dominion (*al-siyāda*) in this universe belong to God, glorified and exalted, alone. And we mean by dominion and sovereignty: "that absolute authority uniquely entitled to determine right and to make it binding, and which alone has the supreme word, without partner, in the affairs of society and the state; such that there exists no other authority that equals it or approaches it, let alone rises above it."

This is the original principle on which Islamic politics stands, while democracy was established on the basis of contradicting this principle, since it handed over absolute authority to the people. And Islam regards accepting it in the aforementioned sense for other than God, glorified and exalted, as associating partners (*ishrāk*) with Him, glorified and exalted, in this attribute of His — may God protect us from it.

And the word "theocracy" (Theocracy) means in its linguistic origin that rule belongs to God; but in the earlier religions — Christianity, Judaism and paganism — only its name remained; as for its reality, the followers of these religions inflicted on it, by way of corruption and destruction, what reversed its concept for which it was coined entirely. So it became an expression for the rule of the spiritual leaders — the priests, rabbis, monks and the priesthood — instead of God, glorified and exalted. From this starting point we see the word [p. 172] even in our age: no mind ever rushes from hearing it to the idea that rule belongs to God; rather the mind rushes from it to the rule of those leaders, as has been explained in the political sources and books of Europe.

As for Islam, it regarded that distorted picture of it as among the ugliest kinds of polytheism and the most hideous of them, since God, glorified and exalted, said: **"They have taken their scholars and monks as lords besides God"** [al-Tawba, 9:31]. So when we speak of the principle "rule belongs to God" as the foundation of foundations in Islamic politics, we do not acknowledge any connection with the leadership of the men of religion that took shape in the earlier religions under the curtain of the word "theocracy", and which ultimately resulted in distorting its concept in one respect, and wandering in the task of associating partners with God in another respect.

In contrast to the earlier religions, Islam took the principle of rule belonging to God in its true concept and its correct meaning, embodied in the fact that what God has revealed of rulings — by recited revelation (*al-waḥy al-matluww*) or otherwise — is the basic constitution and the primary sources of Islamic government, which cannot deviate from it — even a hand-span — to lay down legislation or take a measure that contradicts these rulings or opposes them in anything.

And the acceptance of this principle is the essential difference that distinguishes Islamic politics from democracy. For the legislative chamber in secular democracy enjoys the absolute authority that grants it the power to legislate and enact as it wishes, without finding in its way any obstacle that limits its power or narrows its influence — even in the case where there is in the constitution of the country some restriction on its authority and a prohibition of its legislation in some matters, it can lift it by amending the constitution! As for Islamic government, the situation differs entirely in it, since its fixed, unchanging constitution is the Book of God and the Sunna of His Messenger ﷺ, such that whoever assumes the authority of legislation in it may not deviate from them a hand-span, or take a decision that clashes with a ruling of these two basic sources.

It is the habit of Western scholars that, in researching the history of various theories and schools on some subject, they ignore the thoughts and services of the Muslims in it, by virtue of the blind religious partisanship [p. 173] which they can hardly rid themselves of even in their scholarly research. As has actually happened in their research on politics: they mention its schools and doctrines beginning with the Greek philosophers such as Plato and Aristotle, passing through the Christians in the era of the flourishing of Christianity, and ending with Voltaire, Montesquieu and Rousseau — skipping over by that a long stage of history in which the Muslims were the leaders of the world and the guides of humanity, who took up the reins of government and became the captains of the human ship. They renewed it with a politics entirely different from what the world had known before: a just, balanced politics built on the basis of its original principle — acceptance of rule and sovereignty for God — not to the limit of the abstract idea and pure theory only, but with its practical application which was manifested in its truest forms and most splendid scenes, like the Rightly-Guided Caliphate and the Islamic governments after it. And it is a contradiction of the principles of history and a violation of scholarly trustworthiness that they did not mention it even as a pure theory among past political theories — to the point that in speaking of the principle that rule belongs to God, you see them speaking of the superstitions that the Jewish, Christian and pagan theocracies brought about, ignoring all the Islamic vision of this principle and the Islamic governments that were established on its basis.

At any rate, belief in rule belonging to God is the basis of Islamic government. And mere faith in this principle removes the veil from many political problems in whose solution the scholars of politics have stumbled, and uproots the foundations of many political theories whose birth had no factor except flimsy conjecture which the political mind, bare of faith in this principle, laboured over — like the social contract theory, for example. For it is regarded as the best point of view concerning the origin of government, although it has no basis other than conjecture and following supposition, and no one knows with precision and accuracy when this took place and who witnessed it and concluded it. Rather they are bewildered questions in which the Western scholars of politics wander aimlessly, and so their condition became the best illustration of the words of God, glorified and exalted: **"They have no knowledge of that; they are only guessing"** [al-Zukhruf, 43:20].

Whereas the believer in the principle of rule belonging to God is protected by his faith from wandering in the valleys of these illusions and [p. 174] false analogies. Rather he knows that God, when He created the first human being, announced with him clearly his appointment as vicegerent (*istikhlāf*) by His words: **"Indeed, I will make upon the earth a vicegerent (khalīfa)"** [al-Baqara, 2:30] — which indicates that Adam, being the first human being to set foot on the earth, did not descend to the earth except as the vicegerent of God in it, in the sense that God, glorified and exalted, appointed him as vicegerent to undertake the implementation of His rulings and His commands on His behalf and in subordination to His true rule. Thus the first government on earth arose with the descent of the first human being in it: Adam was a ruler and the rest of humanity ruled by him. And the basis of his government also was the same principle that rule belongs to God, and that no government is ever administered except by what pleases God, and that the government of human beings, whatever it may be, is subordinate to the absolute sovereignty of God and a deputy of His supreme sovereignty. From this starting point came the application of "the Caliphate" (*al-khilāfa*) to this government, and "the Caliph" (*al-khalīfa*) to the Commander of the Faithful who administers it.

### The meaning of khilāfa in Islam

The word "*al-khilāfa*" or "*al-khalīfa*" occurs in many places in the Noble Qur'an, and the commentators have mentioned two meanings for it:

**The first of them:** following the rulings of God, mighty and exalted, committing to obeying Him, and taking on His character traits in the individual life of human beings; in the sense that everyone who believes in God is commanded to obey Him and to take on His character traits in all the moments of his life. In view of this meaning of it, it makes every believer in God His vicegerent on the earth, charged with obeying Him and taking on His character traits. It is the meaning that most commentators chose in explaining the words of God, glorified and exalted: **"Indeed, I will make upon the earth a vicegerent"** [al-Baqara, 2:30].

**The second:** vicegerency from God in His attribute of sovereignty, in the sense that a human being obtains government over others, so he undertakes the implementation of his authority on behalf of the government of God and as a deputyship from Him. It is the meaning that is necessarily taken in what God mentioned of David, upon him be peace, saying: **"O David, indeed We have made you a vicegerent upon the earth"** [Ṣād, 38:26]. It is what we mean in speaking of the political principles of Islam, and it is what grants us a basic conception of government in Islam: that it is not obtained by human beings independently and in their own right, but rather by deputyship from God and vicegerency from Him. And if it is a deputyship from the government of God, it must be subordinate to His rulings and complying with His commands, in fulfilment of [p. 175] the requirements of deputyship and in implementation of vicegerency in its true spirit and meaning. It is the dividing line that distinguishes Islamic government from others; for the irreligious secular governments do not see above their authority any authority, whatever it may be, such that they would submit to its rulings and commit to obeying it.

It is worth mentioning here that the Prophet ﷺ was a vicegerent of God directly and without intermediary; as for the Rightly-Guided Caliphs, their vicegerency was not direct, but through the intermediary of the Prophet ﷺ. From this starting point they chose for themselves the title "Successor of the Messenger of God" (*khalīfat rasūl Allāh*) rather than "Vicegerent of God" (*khalīfat Allāh*), as it is reported that a person said to Abū Bakr, may God be pleased with him: "O Vicegerent of God!" He said: "I am not the vicegerent of God; but I am the successor of the Messenger of God [God bless him and give him peace]."¹

How excellent is ʿAllāma Ibn Khaldūn, who deposited in his *Muqaddima* discussions at the summit of splendour and lucidity; and although it does not exceed one volume, he did not leave a branch of life without taking it up in research, being creative in it, doing well and being original and beneficial. It is natural that he took up the subject of the imamate and caliphate in research too; he mentioned that government has three kinds:

1. Natural kingship (*al-mulk al-ṭabīʿī*): it is bringing everyone [to act] according to the requirement of purpose and desire. [Dictatorial authorities enter into it.]
2. Political kingship (*al-mulk al-siyāsī*): it is bringing everyone [to act] according to the requirement of rational consideration in obtaining worldly interests and repelling harms. [And democracy is of this kind, since it has taken reason as a criterion for estimating interests.]
3. The Caliphate: it is bringing everyone [to act] according to the requirement of Sharia consideration in their otherworldly interests and the worldly ones that revert to them.²

How excellent are his words — how comprehensive they are of the kinds of government! You will not find a government over the course of history but it falls within one of these forms. And it is reported that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb once said: By God, I do not know

### The functions and duties of government and its intended aims in Islam

The prevailing governments today have focused their views and devoted their efforts to attaining the goal of pleasing the people, spreading joy and comfort in it, protecting its rights, and other matters that nourish and satisfy its materialism only. You will not find in the constitution of a government among governments that it aims at the spiritual education of the people, spreading good in it and deterring it from evil by commanding what is right and forbidding what is wrong. That is because they do not possess a balanced criterion with which to estimate good and evil and distinguish between them; rather the thought of modern philosophers in this field has developed to a degree that led them to regard good and evil as two relative matters (relative term), by entrusting the authority for specifying them to society and the people. From this, if one of the most hideous evils prevailed in a society such that people approved it, it would soon leave the category of evils and enter the category of good things. Just as it is not necessary among them that a ruling on a thing being evil be general and comprehensive for all ages and cities; rather it is possible that something regarded as among the good things, if it found currency and acceptance in the people of another state, [be different there].! If they have no complete, absolute conception of good and evil, it is natural that they should reject that spreading this and deterring from that be among the aims that governments and authorities target.

And Islam, since it has a balanced criterion that distinguishes good from evil, embodied in entrusting the ruling on things as good and evil to their Creator — so whatever He regarded as good is good absolutely in all ages, cities and creatures, and whatever He decreed as evil is evil absolutely and permanently — therefore among the most important functions and duties of Islamic government was that it should devote in it [p. 177] its efforts through attending to commanding what is right and forbidding what is wrong.

The Noble Qur'an has sufficed us, since it explained the functions of government and the aims sought from it, saying: **"[They are] those who, if We establish them in the land, establish prayer and give zakat and enjoin what is right and forbid what is wrong. And to God belongs the outcome of [all] matters"** [al-Ḥajj, 22:41].

So the Qur'an did not make spreading joy and happiness among the people one of the functions of government; for happiness is a general term whose instances differ with the difference of people, time and place, to the point that the criminal is gladdened by his crime and the thief is gladdened by his theft. So it is an ambiguous matter, and it is feared that it would be taken as a cover behind which every evil is committed. And God forbid that the Qur'an should make such an ambiguous matter one of the functions of government.

And if we let our gaze roam over what the Qur'an mentioned in enumerating the functions and duties of righteous government, we would find that they are the matters that achieve the goal sought from government. And below we undertake their quick review from this angle.

#### 1 – Establishing the prayer

It is the first thing the Qur'an mentioned among the functions and aims of Islamic government. Perhaps the secular mentality, with its pure rationalism, does not arrive at the connection between prayer and government; but the truth — even from the perspective of secular principles — is what the Noble Qur'an mentioned. For the goal of spreading joy and happiness among people will never be achieved except if there is over them a ruling body that steers them to coordination and order; otherwise, if each of them were given free rein to attain his joy and comfort however he could, that would result in the spread of anarchy and the disruption of order.

Then it is self-evident that mere regulation by the stick of law does not suffice to attain that goal; rather there must for that be a conscious religious education which itself plays the role of motivator, carrying people to observe order even if there is no stick of administrative authorities over them. And the meaning of that education is that a person should come to feel that he will stand one day before the Lord, glorified and exalted, and He will hold him to account for his deeds. This feeling, if it were rooted in his mind and became always before his eyes, would be the great barrier for him [p. 178] against committing any crime.

And there is nothing more important in role and greater in effect for rooting this feeling and reviving it at every moment of life than the prayer. From this God the Exalted gave it precedence in His mention of it among the explanation of the functions of righteous government and its aims; and the Prophet ﷺ pointed out about it, to the point that he expressed it as the pillar of the religion; and he gave it more attention and concentration through his undertaking the education and cultivation of the Companions, and commanded people always with it as far as he could; and he showed the utmost joy when he saw — while in his illness in which he died — his Companions praying in congregation behind Abū Bakr al-Ṣiddīq, may God be pleased with him. And he brought up his Companions to attend to it according to its worth; so it was among the most important of matters in standing and most preoccupying of mind, and the highest in standing and loftiest in rank in the era of the Rightly-Guided Caliphate, which is the splendid model of Islamic government, to the point that the caliphs in it were rulers and imams of the prayers at one and the same time.

And because of the strong connection among them between rulership and leading the prayer, the word "imam" became an expression for their commander, and "the lesser imamate" for leading them in their prayers. And among their emphasis to the governors of government and its employees on establishing it was that they regarded it as among the most important of their duties and greatest of their tasks. That becomes clear from what Mālik narrated in his *Muwaṭṭaʾ*, saying: "From Nāfiʿ, the client of ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar, that ʿUmar ibn al-Khaṭṭāb wrote to his governors: The most important of your affairs to me is the prayer; whoever preserves it and guards it has preserved his religion, and whoever neglects it is more neglectful of what is besides it."¹

And he followed this statement of his by explaining the prayer times in detail and elaboration, which also indicates the utmost of his attention to it. And this letter of his was not a letter he sent to a relative or friend; rather it was a royal decree which he issued in his capacity as Commander of the Faithful and then sent to all the governors of his kingdom.

And likewise were our master ʿUthmān and our master ʿAlī, may God be pleased with them both, as they used to turn their attention towards teaching people the manner of ablution while they were caliphs, as Imam Abū Dāwūd narrated in his *Sunan* many stories of this kind.²
