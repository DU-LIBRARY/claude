### Democracy weighed in the balance

We have presented earlier a brief introduction to the various political schools and many of the political systems prevailing in this age of ours. You noticed, at the end of the discussion of democracy, its basic ideas, its philosophy, the institutions arising from it, and the kinds of governments that appeared and took shape as a result of activating it and putting it into effect.

Here we single out — from among those systems — secular democracy for commentary only, since it has become — after the other political systems failed in practical reality and no longer remain in force — the ideal modern political point of view and the best system of government and authority, to the point that it is said and held in the world today, to the extent of it being said: "It is impossible for there to occur any system of government better than it." A book has recently been issued by a senior official of the American State Department entitled "The End of the History and the Last Man" [Tr.: Francis Fukuyama, *The End of History and the Last Man*], whose summary is that history — having passed through the stages of development and progress — has reached its end; and that after the discovery of secular democracy first, and the production of liberal democracy second, no better system than it can exist or be born.

This is nothing but like Karl Marx's prophecy of the eternity of socialism and that it is the final developed system for the world, which would not be followed by a better or more ideal system.

The same expression has come to be used for democracy and capitalism today, as they — especially after the fall of the Soviet Union in Russia — have begun to claim that democracy and capitalism in the fields of politics and economics have actually achieved a brilliant victory, such that it has become impossible for [p. 144] any other system to rival them.

So let us study this system with precision and close attention, to discover whether there is anything of solidity and reasonableness in it, or whether it is merely a false illusion and a counterfeit reality?

There is no doubt that democracy has contributed considerably to spreading and activating many principles that strengthened the freedom of the individual and his independence to some extent; it has also contributed not a little to rescuing humanity from the persecutions and afflictions it suffered at the hands of individual governments and despotic authorities that prevailed before it in the world. We also grant here that it gave the individual human being freedom of opinion and thought, after dictators had stripped him of it, and brought him out of the state of stagnation and constriction he had lived in under those tyrannical, despotic authorities.

But despite all that — if we examine closely its intellectual structure and its theoretical foundations, we find that they are not built on serious, sober theories; rather, they are nothing but the product of a reaction to the man-made systems that ruled humanity unjustly, relying on what they had invented of erroneous ideas and values.

It is known that the West was ruled by dictatorial governments in most of its past ages, as was explained in detail. And whatever of them was formed in the name of religion or on religious bases was subject to theocracy (Theocracy), with what it deserves of the evils whose explanation I gave earlier. As an example of that, take Roman theocracy: it was bare of any spiritual and religious foundation; rather, nothing was implemented in it except the personal opinions of the Pope as religious rulings, after they believed him sound from error and free from mistake — which naturally caused the destruction of the rights of the people and unfairness to them. So the reaction to that was that the advocates of democracy threw the yoke of religion off their necks entirely, and founded it on the principle of delegating absolute sovereignty and supreme authority to the people. This becomes clear from the fact that democracy is a translation of the English word (Democracy), meaning "rule of the people". From here the principle of the authority of the people emerged. Then, when they granted sovereignty to the people and believed that there was no [p. 145] authority above its authority, it was natural that they should regard it as free in its affairs and matters, not subject to any heavenly law. Hence they resorted to tying and linking this principle to secularism, so democracy and secularism became inseparable, such that liberal democracy (Liberal Democracy) cannot be applied without secularism. In sum, the meaning of democracy is that religion has no connection with the affairs of the state; rather it is a personal matter of the individual relating to his individual life, while the affairs of authority will be concluded without submission to religion and its teachings.

The first thing taken against this idea is that rule, for it to be conceived, needs both its ends: the ruler and the ruled together. When it regarded the whole people as ruler, and it is granted that the ruler requires the existence of a ruled under him, here a question arises in the mind of every rational person: what then remains to play the role of the ruled and occupy its function? The land of the state and its buildings, its inanimate things and the plants that grow in it?! Delegating rule to the whole people results in nothing but the pervasive anarchy and absence of government with which running the state's system becomes impossible; for the existence of two rulers in one region leads to corruption and ruin — so how if the whole people were rulers?!

The saying has become famous in defining democracy: "Government of the people by the people for the people", i.e. it is the rule of the people by the people and for the people. This indicates that the people itself is ruler and ruled at the same time — which is nothing but a sophistry that cannot be valid on any standard; for it is self-evidently impossible for the one person to be ruler and ruled and an instrument of delegating rule at one and the same time. So with this detail the whole foundation of democracy — I mean the principle of rule for the people — has been undermined.

The democrats have answered it that the people is ruler in the sense that at the time of voting it voluntarily elects for itself a representative from among the candidates; the representatives collectively become rulers, while the rest of the people remains ruled by them.

But we say: first, it is not a true representation, as we shall clarify shortly, God willing. Second, if we look at it precisely we find it to be the rule of a small group of the people, not its whole nor its majority; for the choice of the people and its sovereignty are confined to the period [p. 146] of elections only; then sovereignty and authority all pass to the representatives and members of parliament, and the majority of the people becomes in their hands entirely stripped of choice. So it is not correct, then, to regard it as the sovereignty of the majority of the people.

Further, sovereignty according to the scholars of politics is that a person should have absolute authority and power, disposing by it in the affairs of the state and in determining right and obligation as he wishes, such that he is not subject to anyone besides him and there is no other authority above it. In view of their definition of it, then, how can the sovereignty of the people be straight, while the authority of the representatives and the members of the legislature is above it?!

Second, after delegating complete authority to the people, another question arises in the mind of the researcher himself: what is democratic government aiming at, or what is the hoped-for purpose behind establishing democratic government? The scholars of politics are bewildered in answering this question, and more than one of them has tried to cast his bucket in defining and specifying the aim of democracy; but all of them were unable to find a satisfying, convincing answer to this question. Rather, they came with what neither cures nor quenches. One said: "Government itself is the aim sought from democracy." This is merely an opinion and pure theory, which we do not see as worthy of comment. Another said: "The ambition of democracy is to satisfy the majority of the people, and to provide comfort and spread ease in it; for the people does not issue a ruling except if it sees it as harmonious and consistent with what it desires."

But we say: satisfaction and comfort are entirely relative matters. What satisfies some people may be considered, in relation to others, disliked and not preferred; and among things are those that ease some people and gladden them while at the same time causing the disturbance and anxiety of others. So what rule, then, is applied in preferring the satisfaction of some and giving precedence to their comfort over the satisfaction and comfort of others?

The democrats have no convincing answer to this question except to resort to making the rule of the majority the arbiter, saying that its satisfaction is given precedence over the satisfaction of others.

But we say: it contradicts the requirement of democracy itself, since the whole people has not obtained what it desires; and second, it entails that a vice be included among the aims [p. 147] of democracy if the majority of the people is pleased with it. And it suffices as a mark of blindness and an ugliness of misguidance.

And the last idea in this regard — and it is the most accepted in modern democratic circles — is that it aims at protecting the rights of the people.

This idea is refuted and falsified by the fact that there is no regulating criterion defining the rights of the people that they aim to protect under democracy. Nor do they have any refuge in that either except the principle of regarding the rule of the majority of the people — to which they have often resorted, yet it did not avail them and benefited them in nothing, little or much. So they said: "What that majority sees as among the rights of the people, democracy stops to protect it; and what not, not."

And we say in this too what we said in its sisters: that delegating the definition of rights to the majority of the people results only in their becoming a target with which the whims of that majority play, without there being a complete definition and full conception of the rights that democracy stops to protect. Often the people today sees something as a right, so it strives to obtain it or defend it; then when tomorrow comes, that same majority soon rejects it and refuses that it be among its rights that deserve attempts to reach.

At any rate, you will not find in its alleged aims and its sought goals its attempts to spread good and deter evil, and spread virtues and remove vices from human circles; likewise you will not find among them a sound criterion nor a balanced measure for estimating good and evil. Rather, they have acknowledged at present that it has gone beyond all limits: that good and evil are relative matters, so it is possible — in view of this theory — for the goodness and evil of things to differ with the difference of time, place and environment. So a matter that was once all evil has become, by virtue of this arbitrary philosophy, possible to regard today as all good, and likewise conversely.

## Nationalism (Nationalism)

Among the various political theories that have found currency in the present age is nationalism (*al-qawmiyya*), which means: that a union and solidarity arise on the basis of sharing in race, nationality and language. As for the solidarity that the religious bond works to create, they do not regard it as nationalism in anything; rather they call it reaction and backwardness, and do not grant it any connection with the theories of the new age and the requirements of the present era.

The fact is that nationalism and patriotism (*al-qawmiyya wa-l-waṭaniyya*) will never be fit to become a criterion for solidarity and unity. For if we regarded them as the two criteria of union and division, that would lead to the tearing apart of humanity and its splintering along the lines of every city — indeed of every neighbourhood as well. Let us take as an example of that the state of Pakistan. If we said that belonging to Pakistan is the point of union and solidarity that makes one nation (*qawman wāḥidan*), which has the right to establish an independent state on the basis of this unity, then the question that poses itself in the mind of the researcher is: the Sindhis are united by the bond of belonging to Sindh — and it is a province of Pakistan — and likewise the people of the Punjab — the largest of the provinces of Pakistan in terms of population density — and the Pashtuns are united and joined together by the bond of belonging to their provinces. So do they have the right to form a nationalism and patriotism on the basis of this belonging, and then to demand the establishment of [p. 154] independent states along the lines of these units?! And perhaps two cities of one province — like Karachi and Hyderabad in Sindh, for example — differ in culture and social life; so what limit in the definition of nationalism prevents regarding the people of Karachi as one nation united by the bond of belonging to this city and joined together by the agreement of culture and traditions?! And then, what is it that prevents them from demanding the establishment of an independent state on the basis of this patriotism and along the lines of that nationalism?! And likewise the people of Hyderabad. Indeed, cultures, languages, traditions and the principles of social life may differ between neighbourhoods and the lanes of one city. So why then are the inhabitants of this neighbourhood not regarded as an independent nation, and the dwellers of that one as another nation, on the basis of belonging to neighbourhoods and the difference that exists in their dialects, their cultures and the principles of their social life — and why is the right of independence and of establishing an independent state not reserved for both parties?!

From this we do not see ourselves departing from the truth if we said that the calls to nationalism and patriotism are what have played a prominent role in tearing apart the unity of humanity, dividing the word of its children, and sowing the seeds of rancour and enmity in their midst along the lines of these false foundations.

And it is a wonder that the advocates of nationalism — before the independence of Pakistan — used to raise their slogans that all the people of India — with their different languages and their various colours — form one nationalism (*qawmiyya wāḥida*) united by the bond of belonging to the homeland (*al-waṭan*). And when Pakistan was built as an independent Islamic state, they refused to regard its people as one nationalism; rather they made them four nationalisms on the basis of the difference of their languages, cultures and colours — a matter that has developed today so that they have become five, by regarding the Muhājirūn [the Urdu-speaking migrants from India] as an independent nationalism united by the bond of migration from India after independence. So we do not know whether to call it blindness or partisanship: that the people of a large state with high population density like India, inhabited by tens of nationalities, were one nationalism, while the state smaller than it by far is divided into five units and each of them is regarded as an independent nationalism in itself?! This followed only because, when the unity of colour, language and people was accepted as a measure for regional and political union, the chain extended with them and did not stop at the limit we have just explained.

It should be known that the word "*al-qawm*" (the people, the nation) may properly be applied linguistically to a group of people united by [p. 155] the bond of sharing in homeland, race or language. Evidence for this is what the Qur'an relates of the prophets — upon them and upon our Prophet be blessings and peace — that they called their nations, which were unbelieving, with their words: "O my people (*yā qawmī*)". So it applied in it "*al-qawm*" to the nationalism that was formed on the basis of union and solidarity in language and lineage regardless of creed and religion; and so it included everyone who entered that nationalism, Muslim or unbeliever, monotheist or polytheist.

This is according to the meaning of the term in language. As for, in view of the principles of Islam, whether it is valid to regard that nationalism as a measure and criterion for political unity or not — we arrive at the answer to this question in the light of the following verse: **"It is He who created you; and among you is the unbeliever, and among you is the believer"** [al-Taghābun, 64:2]. For it divided all mankind on the basis of faith in God and unbelief in Him into two units: a believer in Him, glorified and exalted, and an unbeliever in Him.

And likewise with the verse in which God commanded the Muslims to take as an example our master Abraham — upon him and upon our Prophet be blessings and peace — when he disowned his people, where He, mighty is the One who said, says: **"There has already been for you an excellent example in Abraham and those with him, when they said to their people: 'Indeed, we are disassociated from you and from whatever you worship other than God. We have denied you, and there has appeared between us and you animosity and hatred forever until you believe in God alone'"** [al-Mumtaḥana, 60:4].

The noble verse has shed light, in an eloquent style, on both matters together: its expressing the community (*umma*) of Abraham as "his people" (*qawmihi*) is what indicates the applying of the word "*al-qawm*" to those whom the bond of lineage and language unites even if the differences of creed and religion separate them; and Abraham's disowning of his people is what indicates that the Muslims cannot be bound with the unbelievers by a political unity, even if they are of one people (*qawm wāḥid*); and that is because of the vast distance between the two parties and the clear difference in the aims and purposes of life.

What should be noted in this regard is that a person's feeling of love for those who share with him in homeland, lineage or language, and his heartfelt bond with them or his affinity to them, are among natural matters. Hence Islam has not forbidden taking account of them, nor prevented fulfilling their requirements, provided that they be within their correct frameworks and within [p. 156] their limits established for them by the Sharia.

Likewise, the meaning of "hatred and animosity" towards the unbelievers is not that establishing relations with them is regarded as categorically forbidden and not permitted in any situation. Just as the verse does not indicate the prohibition of goodwill (*muwāsāt*) and kindness towards non-Muslims on the basis of humanity. For God, after mentioning Abraham's disowning of his unbelieving people in the context of praise, brought shortly after that — and in the same sura — the other verse in which He made clear to the Muslims the method of dealing with non-Muslims, saying, glorified and exalted: **"God does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes — from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them. Indeed, God loves those who act justly"** [al-Mumtaḥana, 60:8].

Just as it is permissible to conclude with them, when there is need, agreements of peaceful coexistence (*ittifāqiyyāt al-taʿāyush al-silmī*), on condition that they do not conflict with the interest of the Muslims, in view of what He, glorified and exalted, said: **"And if they incline to peace, then incline to it [also] and rely upon God"** [al-Anfāl, 8:61]. Of this kind was the Prophet ﷺ's concluding the truce and peace treaty with the polytheists of Mecca at al-Ḥudaybiya; and when the Banū Bakr, allies of theirs, broke it against the Khuzāʿa, the conquest of Mecca — may God ennoble it — took place in its wake. Likewise he made a treaty with the Jews of Medina concerning internal security, what we know today as "the Charter of Medina" (*Mīthāq al-Madīna*). He remained on that until the Jews broke it openly, and in its wake the Prophet ﷺ raided them.

The upshot is that establishing security relations and concluding peace charters with non-Muslims, and bringing about goodwill and kindness towards the non-hostile among them, is permissible in the Sharia; and Islamic history is full of the like of these relations and agreements, and we refrain from exhausting them here.

But since there is a clear difference between the two parties in the aims of life, whenever the Muslims wish to establish a state by their choice and capability, they found it on the religion of Islam and the creed of divine unity (*tawḥīd*), not on nationalism and patriotism. And the meaning of that is that if the Muslims had before them two choices concerning the founding of the state — the choice of religion and creed, and the choice of nationalism and patriotism — it would be obligatory on them in the Sharia to give precedence to the first choice, and so found their state on the basis of the religion of Islam and the creed of divine unity.

[p. 157] And the summary of what has preceded is that "*al-qawm*", even if it is applied linguistically to those sharing in homeland, lineage and descent, Islam has categorically denied the regarding of this nationalism as a basis for political unity, and the Prophet ﷺ forbade it with the most severe prohibition — as appears in the hadith which the two Shaykhs [al-Bukhārī and Muslim] narrated from Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh, may God be pleased with him, whose text is: "From Jābir ibn ʿAbd Allāh, may God be pleased with them both, he said: We were on a military expedition — Sufyān said: once in an army — and a man of the Muhājirūn struck a man of the Anṣār on the backside. The Anṣārī said: O Anṣār! And the Muhājirī said: O Muhājirūn! The Messenger of God ﷺ heard that and said: 'What is this call of the Jāhiliyya?' They said: O Messenger of God, a man of the Muhājirūn struck a man of the Anṣār on the backside. He said: 'Leave it, for it is foul (*muntina*).'"¹

So the Prophet ﷺ regarded mutual support and backing on the basis of emigration or helping (*al-hijra aw al-nuṣra*) — that a Muhājir should support a Muhājir merely because he is of the Muhājirūn, regardless of whether he is in the right or in the wrong; and likewise the Anṣārī concerning the other Anṣār — as a call of the Jāhiliyya, and described it as foul; and he taught that a person's support should go with the truth wherever it goes, even if it is against a person of his own people (*qawmihi*).

And the idea of "nationalism" is nothing but the offspring of secular democracy, which threw off its neck the yoke of religion; so it became empty-handed of what it could regard as a basis for political unity. So it resorted to nationalism and patriotism and took them as a criterion for political union — and they are far from that by a distance that is not close.

Nationalism has brought upon the Islamic Umma evils and woes, and it is an idol that was found among the Muslims who took it as a god. So it was used to tear apart their unity, scatter their gathering and divide their word.
