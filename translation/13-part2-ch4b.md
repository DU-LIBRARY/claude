
[p. 237]

> *Jamhūriyyat ik ṭarz-e ḥukūmat hai ke jis men*
> *Bandon ko gina karte hain, tolā nahīn karte*

Democracy is a kind of government in which people are merely counted and not weighed. (What counts in it is mere number, without distinguishing among the voters between dullness and toughness, foolishness of mind and sharpness of opinion, etc.)

While a passing look at the rulings of the Qur'an and Sunna clearly shows us that Islam has never regarded the majority as a criterion that judges things to be lawful or unlawful, defines truth and falsehood, and distinguishes good from evil. For the Qur'an has rejected that categorically, saying: **"And if you obey most of those upon the earth, they will mislead you from the way of God. They follow nothing but conjecture, and they are only guessing"** [al-Anʿām, 6:116].

So it has no weight at all, then, in the matters on which the Islamic Sharia has decided with a ruling. And of such, the poet Muhammad Iqbal also said:

> *Ke az maghz-e do ṣad khar fikr-e insānī namī āyad*

The thought of a single human being does not come out of the brains of two hundred donkeys.

As for what is besides that, Islam has not rejected it outright; rather it can be considered as a Sharia criterion in two matters of religion:

**The first:** in the permissible matters in which there are before the imam many options, and the people of the shūrā differ over them; so the majority can be considered in them as a factor giving preponderance to one of those forms and options, all of which are permissible. And the evidence for that from the side of transmission is the action of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him: shortly before his death he formed, for choosing the caliph after him, a committee consisting of six of the senior Companions — ʿUthmān, ʿAlī, ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, Ṭalḥa, al-Zubayr ibn al-ʿAwwām and Saʿd ibn Abī Waqqāṣ, may God be pleased with them all — and he directed them to the plan of action which they would take in their affair, saying: "Consult [one another] in your affair; and if it is two and two, then go back in

[p. 238] consultation; and if it is four and two, then take the side of the majority."¹

So it became clear from him that al-Fārūq, may God be pleased with him, considered the majority of opinion in this issue — which can be used as evidence for considering it in others among the permissible matters.

**The second:** in the issues open to ijtihad (*al-masāʾil al-mujtahad fīhā*), which are the matters concerning which no decisive ruling has come from the Lawgiver, and the opinions of the mujtahids have differed in their ruling on the basis of the difference of their grounds in explaining what is general in the Qur'an and the Sunna and clarifying what is ambiguous of them, and so on. So the majority of opinion can be considered in them as a factor giving preponderance to one of those probabilities and concepts, in the sense that the saying held by most of the imams be chosen. And some jurists have expressed this position by saying: the majority of opinion is not in itself evidence; rather we can take it as an indication, in the sense that probability prevails that the evidences of the position which most of the imams chose are preponderant; for if it were not preponderant, those eminent imams and great scholars would not have held it.

And this does not mean that whenever the majority of the imams is on one side, that is the preponderant one necessarily and definitively; for there are many issues in which Imam Abū Ḥanīfa differed from the rest of the imams, so he became alone on his side and on another side all the imams and the mujtahid jurists. But I heard my father, His Eminence Shaykh Mufti Muhammad Shafiʿ (may God have mercy on him), relate from Shaykh Maḥmūd al-Ḥasan al-Deobandī (may God have mercy on him) that he said: it is my strong probability in such issues that the position of the Imam and his argument are of the utmost strength; for if it were not so, he would not have dared to oppose all the imams definitively.

So the upshot is that the majority of opinion, even if it is not in itself a standing argument nor a decisive proof, there is no harm in taking it as a factor of preponderance for one of the positions when Sharia evidences conflict concerning it. And from this it is correct that the constitution of the shūrā council provide that the decision, in case of disagreement in permissible matters, is according to the Sharia by the majority of opinions.

And what deserves attention in this field is that many councils and bodies have provided

> ¹ *Al-Ṭabaqāt al-Kubrā*, al-ʿIlmiyya ed. (3/45).

[p. 239] in their constitution that if the opinions of the two positions are equal, preponderance belongs to the side in which the president is; and sometimes this is expressed by saying that the president gives a casting vote (Casting vote). And it too has a basis in the action of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, since he said in the plan of action which he instructed the people of the shūrā to proceed by in the field of choosing the caliph after him: "And if the opinion of three and three agreed, then follow the side of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf."¹

And likewise it has become prevalent in the world today that they sometimes allow persons to attend the shūrā council and give opinion in it without their having a vote in voting; and this is what is called in contemporary terminology the non-voting member (Non-Voting members). And it is also permissible in the Sharia, relying on what ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, did: that he allowed his son ʿAbd Allāh to witness the council of the people of the shūrā as a mere witness, without his opinion having weight in disagreement among them; and among what came in the plan of action which we presented introducing briefly is that he said: "ʿAbd Allāh ibn ʿUmar will witness you, and he has nothing of the matter."²

### 5 – Qualities of the members of the shūrā

The third discussion in the matter of the shūrā is: are there conditions that must be present in the members of the shūrā who advise the imam in the affairs of the kingdom, or not? We say in that: the shūrā council consists of the people who loosen and bind among the Muslims, a brief introduction to whom and to their important functions has passed before us earlier, among them the choosing of the imam and the appointment of the caliph for the Muslims. From this it becomes clear that the shūrā council does not consist of every Tom, Dick and Harry of the riff-raff of people and their rabble; rather they must have qualities, some of which are self-evident, like truthfulness and trustworthiness, alongside knowledge and insight in the affairs of the state and the tasks of government. While the Sharia has not specified any of their other qualities; rather it left that to the Muslims to choose in every age what agrees

> ¹ *Al-Ṭabaqāt al-Kubrā*, al-ʿIlmiyya ed. (3/45).
> ² *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* (5/17), Book of Merits, chapter on the story of the pledge and the agreement on ʿUthmān, hadith no. 3699.

[p. 240] with its requirements and meets its needs.

But the amount we can define, even from the word itself, is that the most important thing is that they be people whom the Umma trusts as to their truthfulness, their trustworthiness and their insight into the interests of the Umma and its political issues.

And in tribal life there was no need for electing them; rather the leaders of the tribes and the notables of the people were almost designated for this purpose. As for today, the situation has not remained as it was; so a coordinated election of them has become necessary. And the Sharia has also not specified for it a special method among the methods of election prevailing today, whether by way of the electoral body (Electoral college), or on the basis of the right to vote for every adult citizen. And what is preponderant with me is that if the rate of learning and political insight were high in the state, they should be elected by way of voting from every adult citizen; just as there does not appear any harm from the point of view of Sharia consideration in the elections being in stages and according to ranks, if that is what is appropriate to the condition of the people and the interest of the Umma.

### 6 – Membership of women in the consultative council

The opinions of the scholars of Islamic political jurisprudence have conflicted on whether a woman may according to the Sharia be a member of the shūrā council or not. So [there is one] who holds it permissible, arguing by what came from the Prophet ﷺ: that he often consulted the women even in the general matters of the Muslims, as was so in the Treaty of al-Ḥudaybiya when the Prophet ﷺ ordered his companions to slaughter [their sacrificial animals] and shave [their heads]; and although he repeated this command three times, none of them did it. So astonishment took him when he saw this slowness in complying with his command, from those who had been eager to comply with his commands, and he had never known that from them before. So he entered upon Umm Salama and mentioned to her what he had met from the people. Umm Salama said: "O Prophet of God, would you like that? Go out, then do not speak to any of them a word until you slaughter your sacrificial animals and call your shaver and he shaves you." So he went out and did not speak to any of them until he did that: he slaughtered his sacrificial animals and called his shaver and he shaved him. When they saw that, they stood up and slaughtered, and some of them began to shave

[p. 241] others."¹

And commenting on this argument we say: the hadith, even if it indicated his consulting the woman, at the same time is not fit by itself — and merely by his consulting a woman on some issue — to be used as evidence for permitting the woman to be a member of the shūrā council, as is not hidden.

And the second evidence for this position is what was of the action of ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, may God be pleased with him, as a member of that committee which ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, formed for choosing the caliph after his death: when he withdrew and left his right from that, he undertook to strive for the Muslims as to the best of them, to appoint him. So it is mentioned that he asked whoever it was possible for him to ask of the people of the shūrā and others, and that continued three [days]; and among what history narrates to us about him is that: "Then ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf, may God be pleased with him, rose up consulting people about them [the candidates], gathering the opinion of the Muslims with the opinion of the heads of the people and their leaders, all together and scattered, in twos and singly, and gathered, secretly and openly, until he reached the secluded women in their seclusion."²

So these held fast to his reaching the secluded women, consulting them about the matter of the caliph, [as evidence] that there is no harm in the woman becoming a member of the shūrā council of the Muslims.

But I say: "This argument is weak; for in the same story it is reported after that that he even asked the children in the schools"; so just as it is not correct to argue by this sentence to make the children of the schools members of the shūrā — as is judged self-evident — likewise it is not correct to argue by it for the right of women to membership of the shūrā council either.

As for the people of the first position — the prohibition of granting them the right to membership in the shūrā council — they argued for it by what al-Tirmidhī narrated of the saying of the Prophet ﷺ: "And your affairs are [handed] to your women, then the belly of the earth is better for you than its surface."³

And finally, I have presented before you gentlemen these two positions and their proofs in detail with brief following-up and commentary on them. ### 7 – Membership of a non-Muslim in the consultative council

The fourth discussion in this regard is granting the unbeliever membership in the shūrā council of the Islamic state. Some held that it is not permissible, arguing by the following verse: **"O you who have believed, do not take as intimates those other than yourselves, for they will not spare you [any] ruin. They wish you would have hardship. Hatred has already appeared from their mouths, and what their breasts conceal is greater"** [Āl ʿImrān, 3:118].

And commenting on it we say: "This argument is not clear; for the verse aims at explaining the ruling of the unbelievers hostile to Islam, by forbidding the Muslims from taking them as intimates in their special affairs; and it indicates its restriction to the hostile among them — events which ʿAllāma al-Ālūsī (may God have mercy on him) mentioned as a cause of the revelation of this verse, saying: From Ibn ʿAbbās, he said: Men of the Muslims used to keep connections with men of the Jews because of what was between them of neighbourliness and alliance in the Jāhiliyya; so God the Exalted revealed concerning them, forbidding them from taking them as intimates for fear of strife against them, this verse. And ʿAbd ibn Ḥumayd included that it was revealed concerning the hypocrites of the people of Medina: the believers were forbidden to take them as friends."¹

> ¹ *Tafsīr al-Ālūsī* = *Rūḥ al-Maʿānī* (2/253).

[p. 243] And it is a clear matter in which there is no doubt: that the Muslims are forbidden from making the hostile among the unbelievers their intimates and the keepers of their secret, and from granting them membership in their shūrā council or bringing them into it. As for the non-hostile among them, who inhabit the Islamic state in non-hostility to it and live in it a safe, peaceful life, the jurists allowed them to attend the shūrā council, relying on the action of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him: that many of the dhimmīs once attended a shūrā council of his, as ʿAllāma al-Sarakhsī mentioned in *al-Mabsūṭ*; and he said in one place in the wake of the story of a shūrā of ʿUmar, may God be pleased with him, which a Christian attended: "And in it is evidence that there is no harm in bringing some of the People of the Book into the shūrā council; for the Christian who said what he said had attended the council of ʿUmar — may God be pleased with him — for the shūrā, and he did not disapprove of it."¹

And perhaps that was only in relation to some matters that related to the dhimmīs specifically, so he consulted some of them in it; which indicates that there is no weight for their opinion and their counsel in the public affairs of the Muslims and their social issues. As for in matters related to the dhimmīs specifically, or what is of the kind of permissible matters, there is no harm in bringing them into the shūrā for it and seeking opinion from them about it.

### 8 – Legislation and law-making

Parliament or the house of representatives is what performs the task of legislation in contemporary political atmospheres and schools; and from this the term "legislative council" or "legislative body" is applied to it also. And it enjoys in the democratic system absolute authority and complete power concerning legislation, without being committed in it to a matter or restricted by anything or confined to a limit — except for the restrictions which a constitution may impose on it in some state; but they too do not remain restrictions in the end; for parliament itself is able to lift them by amending the constitution by the majority of the opinions of the representatives and members. So it is correct, then, to say that democracy grants it complete freedom and absolute independence to legislate whatever it wishes and make law as it wants.

> ¹ *Al-Mabsūṭ* of al-Sarakhsī (24/7).

[p. 244] While Islam looks at the Qur'an and the Prophetic Sunna as a basic constitution and a fixed principal source — not capable of replacement and change — for the Islamic state; and from this it subjects to them both all that it undertakes of legislation and law-making. So after codifying this rule and establishing this principle, we can divide legislation in Islamic government into three kinds:

**The first:** that Islamic government undertake the implementation of the clear, decisive Islamic rulings from the Book and the Sunna, on which there is no room for disagreement and no place for ijtihad in explaining them, as a law for it. And it can choose for it one of these two forms: that those rulings be in the form of written law (codified law) and the courts be obligated to judge by what is in it. And the second: that the courts be obligated to judge in all disputes and cases by the rulings of the Qur'an and Sunna without those rulings being handed to them in the form of a written law or a codified constitutional document. So it would be among the functions of the courts then, and the duties of their judges, to refer to them in every case to learn its Sharia ruling. And so that they can arrive at that, it is necessary in this form that the judges of the courts be with knowledge and familiarity with this kind of the rulings of the Qur'an and the Prophetic Sunna, so that it is possible for them and naturally to judge by them.

**The second part:** the cases whose rulings these two sources have not stated in anything; so they need, in arriving at their ruling, the faculty of ijtihad and the qualification for derivation from them; or what is general and ambiguous among them, in explaining and clarifying which two may differ. So the Islamic state undertakes their implementation in the form of law; and since they are a place for ijtihad and a scope for derivation, they therefore need a rule for preferring one of the positions and opinions when disagreement occurs in them. And it is possible to solve them also by one of these two forms: that those rulings be codified, so they are implemented as a written law of the country (statute law); or the government issue a brief law meaning obligating the courts in preferring among these positions either by themselves, or restricting them with following a particular school among the schools of Islamic jurisprudence — as if those courts were restricted to judging in all disputes and pleadings according to Ḥanafī jurisprudence. And to learn the position of Ḥanafī jurisprudence in a case in which the court will need

[p. 245] to refer to its sources. And it is clear that it will not actually be applied and will not be implemented in practice except if the judges of the courts are specialists versed in the Islamic sciences; otherwise that would lead to gross errors and heavy losses, as is not hidden!

As for in the early period of Islam, the first method was prevalent in it, by virtue of the deep learning of the judges in the Islamic sciences and their qualification for ijtihad from the Qur'an and the Prophetic Sunna directly. As for after that — and the state of the judges had declined in mastering the Islamic sciences — the government would appoint for them a number of muftis, such that it was necessary for them to consult them before deciding anything.

And the most appropriate, in view of our circumstances and our contemporary situations, is that the law be implemented after codifying its texts and its articles in writing; and not leave the rulings for them as an unwritten law, in which the fears of fraud, cheating and forgery increase more — and especially in our time, in which these blameworthy traits have spread and prevailed even in judicial circles! But it is known that this enormous work needs a group of eminent jurists of the Sharia and experts, who undertake by consultation among them codifying those rulings and writing them in the form of law, as was so in the last eras of the Ottoman Caliphate, where "the Majalla of Judicial Rulings" was arranged and codified, which became a law for it and was considered a source for judicial rulings in Islamic lands for a not short period. As for today, until the codification of rulings — which needs time and the efforts of the greatest group of the Umma — it is possible temporarily and for a transitional period to act by the first form of the two forms mentioned just now.

And there has continued to be a proposal put forward in political circles with intensity and force, namely that legislation concerning both these kinds of rulings be entrusted to parliament alone, so that it performs, by all of its members, the role of ijtihad and derivation in clarifying these rulings from the Qur'an and Sunna and defining their concepts and so on.

We say to them in that: it is possible that this proposal be accepted if the election of members of parliament among you took place on the basis of their knowledge and familiarity and their connection with the sciences of the Qur'an

[p. 246] and the Prophetic Sunna — which is impossible today; for they have become such that most of them have no connection with them, let alone having qualifications for understanding them and insight into them and ijtihad and derivation from them. So if the situation is as bad as this, then how are they to be trusted in understanding their meanings and clarifying what is general in them and specifying what is ambiguous in them?!

**The third part:** legislation concerning the permissible matters on which the Sharia has not decided with any matter. And legislation in parliament today is usually what is about these permissible matters: so a law about the employees of the government and their privileges, and another about the municipal council and its departments, or the organization of armies and their arrangement. And to guarantee its freedom from what contradicts the Sharia, it should be presented first — and before it takes on the character of law and is put into implementation — to experts of the Sharia and the scholars, so that they review it with a precise scholarly review, so that there is not found in it anything — even a clause — that contradicts its rulings.

And the prevailing method today is that legislation and law-making about something usually take place in parliament and the house of representatives; then after its adoption and its agreement on it, it is presented to the head of state or its king to approve it by signing it if it seems right to him to do so. But if he found his opinion contrary to it, the most he can do is send it to parliament to undertake sifting it another time; then if parliament held firm on its adoption after reviewing it at the direction of the president, it is presented to him another time so that he signs it, approving it whether he wills or refuses!

And as passed before us just now, the first two parts of legislation in the Islamic state are only completed by mastering the Islamic sciences and specializing in them, which the members of parliament today lack, as is tangibly witnessed. So they are not to be trusted in this field; rather there must be a body formed of the scholars of the Sharia who have mastered its sciences and are specialists in it, to undertake clarifying the rulings of the Sharia and interpreting them in the light of its fixed principles and its established rules. And there is no harm in there being with them experts from various fields of life and specialists from its various branches, to supply them and help them in knowing the jurisprudence of external reality, so that a correct picture of it becomes rooted in them, which makes easy for them undertaking the task of ruling on it. And their role will be confined to making the scholars insightful about external reality

[p. 247] only, without their having any hand in specifying the rulings of the Sharia and interpreting them; rather the reference in it, first and last, is to those scholars and jurists only, who possess complete skill and full mastery and precise view of the principles of derivation from the four sources: the Qur'an, the Prophetic Sunna, consensus and analogy.

So this body itself undertakes the task of legislation and law-making concerning the first two matters, either directly and without need of anyone, or it performs the function of specifying rulings only, and so presents it as a proposal to the shūrā council or parliament to undertake implementing it officially if it agreed with it. But if it disagreed with it in it, it sends it to it with a clarification of what was problematic to it in it and an explanation of the justifications for its rejection, so that it reconsiders it and reviews it with more precision and mastery, taking note of parliament's objections to it and its justifications for rejecting it. And after all that, either it undertakes amendment in the draft law according to parliament's direction, or it answers its observations with a satisfying, convincing answer, so that parliament then undertakes implementing it officially and finally.

And a doubt has been raised today around this proposal: that it means nothing but subjecting parliament to this body of scholars — which is a form of theocracy and papacy. And I answered it earlier, specifically in the discussion of theocracy, in detail sufficient to remove this doubt and uproot it from its roots, just as I spoke at length on it in my Urdu book *Nifāẕ-e Sharīʿat aur us kā ṭarīqa-e kār* (Implementing the Sharia and its method), in which I clarified prominent essential differences that distinguish between what we are concerned with — the body of scholars — and what theocracy was upon. And the summary of the statement in that is that it is self-evidently established that what counts in every art is its men who are specialists in it; so if our discussion is about insight into the sciences of the Qur'an and the Sunna and explaining and interpreting it and deriving rulings from it, why then not rely on the scholars in it, when they have spent their lives in the way of acquiring its sciences, not sparing their dear and cheap in it, until they mastered them and grasped of them the amount with which sufficiency is achieved in leading the Umma and steering its wheel according to the rulings of the pure, tolerant Sharia, without distortion, forgery, flattery or fawning? And for more detail on the difference between the role of the scholars of Islam

[p. 248] in government — and especially in the field of legislation — and what Christian theocracy was upon in the field of authority and rule, let him refer to what we presented in the discussion of theocracy in the first part of this book.

And what has prevailed today in the patterns of contemporary government — that the head of state or the king is entirely subject to the house of representatives such that they are compelled to sign the decision which parliament issued even against their will and their non-agreement to it — contradicts the principle of respect for the freedom of conscience altogether, and makes the president merely a symbolic head. So he ought to be allowed to reject it and not sign it if he did not agree with it in principle; and in this form, let the decision be taken and the legislation completed by the signature of the speaker of parliament and the legislative council — avoiding the occurrence of the situation which Queen Victoria faced when a law was presented to her with which she did not agree, yet she approved it, saying: "I sign it while I believe it to be wrong."

And on another side, the law is not completed in its final form except after its passing through many stages in which some deficiencies may creep into it from the Sharia side. So it appears necessary that every citizen be allowed to challenge a law on the basis of its contradicting the Sharia and its clashing with it, in the supreme constitutional court which includes the specialists in the Sharia sciences with experience and insight in the rulings of the Qur'an and Sunna, so that they undertake to hear the position of both parties: the complainant and the representative of the government bodies, and to rule on the soundness of the complaint or not in the light of its proofs. So if the complaint was sound and it was established to the court that the law or a clause of it contradicts the Sharia and clashes with its principles, let it order the government to cancel it or amend it so that it agrees with the requirements of the Sharia. And the court's competence to rule in such a case will resemble what is established in America: that the court is distinguished by the authority which permits it to cancel a law which parliament adopted and approved.

And Pakistan has established the two constitutional institutions "the Federal Shariat Court" and "the Shariat Appellate Bench of the Supreme Court" for this purpose; and we would

[p. 249] see in them sufficiency in this field were it not for many evils and deficiencies that exist in their administrative structure — among them the shortage of the number of men of knowledge and experts in the Sharia in them; and second, that the reference in employing scholars in them and removing them from their membership belongs to the head of state absolutely. So the administrative authorities then enjoy obtaining from them the ruling according to their whim and pleasure, through removing some of their members if they did not agree with them on the law of the government and its opinion as clashing with the Sharia — as actually happened in the past. So if these two defects and deficiencies latent in them were removed, these two institutions would play their prominent role greatly in drafting laws with a religious drafting and shaping them in an Islamic mould.

### 9 – The scope and limits of obedience to the ruler

The Islamic Sharia makes it obligatory on the subjects to obey the imam who was chosen and designated as imam of the Muslims and their caliph on the basis of the details which we mentioned earlier with elaboration and clear explanation — and that is if it was within the framework of the rulings of the Qur'an and the Prophetic Sunna, not contrary to them in anything. And the evidence for that is what God, glorified and exalted, said: **"O you who have believed, obey God and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you"** [al-Nisāʾ, 4:59]. So God commanded in this verse obedience to those in authority (the caliphs and their officials) alongside obedience to God and obedience to His Messenger ﷺ. So it became clear from it that whatever was of the commands of God, glorified and exalted, or the commands of His Messenger ﷺ is obligatory on all the Muslims, including the imam and the holders of authority and others. As for the ruling of obedience to the commander, it is directed to the Muslims in two cases:

**The first:** that the imam, if he issued a specific ruling to undertake a matter which is permissible in the Sharia, then it is obligatory on the Muslims to undertake it and perform it on the basis of the imam's command with it. And that is as if the imam commanded his subjects to fast a day, for example: it is obligatory on them to fast that day in performance of the obligation of obeying the imam — as ʿAllāma Ibn ʿĀbidīn al-Shāmī (may God have mercy on him) said, attributing it to more than one of the jurists: "That obedience to the imam in what is not disobedience is obligatory; so if he ordered fasting, it became obligatory."¹

> ¹ *Al-Durr al-Mukhtār* and the gloss of Ibn ʿĀbidīn (*Radd al-Muḥtār*) (5/422).

[p. 250] And likewise if the imam forbade a permissible matter: it is obligatory on the subjects to desist from it merely by the imam's prohibition concerning it.

And the meaning of that is not that the state of that Sharia-permissible thing has changed; rather it still remains, from the Sharia side, in its character, I mean permissibility; but its ruling changed after the imam's command with it or prohibition of it, by the motive of the obligation of obeying the imam only.

And proceeding from this it is obligatory on the subjects to comply with the traffic rules issued by the governmental bodies and administrative authorities, as being commands of the imam or his officials in permissible matters.

But alongside that, the jurists have also established this principle with all explicitness and clarity: that "the disposal of the imam over the subjects is tied to the interest (*maṣlaḥa*)"¹ — in the sense that obedience to him is only obligatory in the commands which he issued by the motive of the interest of the subjects and the motives of gentleness with them. As for the rulings which he commanded with injustice to the subjects and tyranny over them, or for the purpose of exhausting them and constraining them, obedience to them is not obligatory in anything. And its basis is what God commanded David, upon him be peace, with, saying: **"O David, indeed We have made you a vicegerent upon the earth, so judge between the people in truth"** [Ṣād, 38:26]; and what is narrated that the Prophet ﷺ sent an expedition and put over them a man of the Anṣār, and ordered them to obey him. He became angry with them and said: Did not the Prophet ﷺ order you to obey me? They said: Yes. He said: I resolve upon you that you gather firewood and kindle a fire, then enter it. So they gathered firewood and kindled a fire; and when they were about to enter, they stood looking at one another. Some of them said: We only followed the Prophet ﷺ fleeing from the fire; shall we enter it? ### 10 – Freedom of criticism in Islam

The ruling of the obligation of obeying the commander does not mean that criticism of a step the imam took or a work he undertook is categorically forbidden. Rather Islamic government founded on straight foundations grants the people complete freedom in criticizing the imam — and especially if they saw from him what contradicts the rulings of the Sharia and opposes its fixed principles; and in this form it is also allowed for them by way of the court door to sue him so that he answers to it about it, and to cancel what contradicts of his commands the Book and the Prophetic Sunna. And for that, the imamate is not a rank that makes its holder exceed the limits of human nature and become infallible from error in undertaking and free from mistake in the matter. And from this starting point came the command to consult the believers, so that each gives his opinion before the imam with all freedom and firmness without fear, blame or regret.

And the Prophet ﷺ forbade the generality of the Muslims from following the imam in what contradicts the Sharia; rather he commanded them to strive to set him right as far as they could, as came in the hadith from Kaʿb ibn ʿUjra; he said: The Messenger of God ﷺ came out to us and we were nine; he said: "There will be after me commanders; whoever believes them in their lying and helps them in their injustice, he is not of me and I am not of him, and he will not come to me at the Pool. ### 11 – Between criticism and insult

But despite that, the Islamic Sharia has drawn a clear dividing line that distinguishes between what is criticism of the sultan and what is regarded as an insult to him. So it permitted that, urging it, while it forbade this with the most severe prohibition. And that is as al-Tirmidhī included with his chain from Ziyād ibn Kusayb al-ʿAdawī; he said: I was with Abū Bakra under the pulpit of Ibn ʿĀmir while he was giving a sermon, and he was wearing thin clothes.

> ¹ *Ṭabaqāt al-Shāfiʿiyya al-Kubrā* of al-Subkī (8/216).

[p. 258] Abū Bilāl said: Look at our commander wearing the clothes of the transgressors. Abū Bakra said: Be silent; I heard the Messenger of God ﷺ say: "Whoever insults the sultan of God on the earth, God insults him."¹

So it became clear from it that criticism of the sultan must be within its special framework and its limits established for it in the Sharia, without exceeding them and going beyond them so as to turn from it into an insult that is categorically forbidden in the Sharia.

And Aḥmad narrated it in his *Musnad* in a fuller wording than this, saying: From Abū Bakra; he said: I heard the Messenger of God ﷺ say: "Whoever honours the sultan of God in this world, God honours him on the Day of Resurrection; and whoever insults the sultan of God in this world, God insults him on the Day of Resurrection."²

And the hadith, even if there is talk about its chain, many of the hadith scholars have nevertheless narrated it with different chains and many routes, which makes it fit to be used as evidence that insulting the sultan is not permissible in the Sharia. And the essential difference between them lies in the aim which its doer wants behind undertaking it: if it was counsel and reform and seeking the pleasure of God — not for humiliation nor for display — then it is constructive; otherwise it is insult or showing off. As becomes clear from that which Aḥmad narrated with his chain from ʿIyāḍ ibn Ghanm, may God be pleased with him, that he heard the Messenger of God ﷺ say: "Whoever wants to counsel a holder of authority on a matter, let him not show it openly to him; but let him take him by his hand and be alone with him; if he accepts it from him, so be it; otherwise he has fulfilled what was upon him."³
