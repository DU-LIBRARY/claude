## Translator's Foreword

[p. 13] Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds, and blessings and peace upon the best of His creation, Muhammad, and upon all his family and companions. To proceed:

This book originated as a series of lectures that our teacher, His Eminence Shaykh ʿAllāma Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani — may God the Exalted preserve and protect him — kindly delivered during a training course held by Jāmiʿa Dār al-ʿUlūm Karachi for scholars of the Sharia, teachers at religious institutes and seminaries, and those working in the field of fatwa in Pakistan. Its aim was to acquaint them with the most important topics discussed in political science, beginning with an explanation of its first principles, passing through an introduction to the various political theories and systems of government, and ending with a discussion of the principles and foundations of Islam that it has legislated in the political sphere, together with the forms of their practical application in the present age.

The lectures had been recorded on tape. Our two teachers, Shaykh ʿAbdullāh Memon and Shaykh Muhammad Muzammil Kāpāriyā — may God preserve and protect them both — undertook to transcribe and arrange them, and they had the good fortune of seeing them printed in book form after His Eminence Shaykh Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani — may God preserve and protect him — had revised them. He made changes to them, on the one hand by deleting some matters he considered unhelpful, and on the other by adding a number of important topics and issues, as well as correcting their wording and arranging their material. Thus the book has been presented in its final form to Urdu readers.

I considered myself very fortunate when His Eminence the Shaykh, may God preserve him, charged me with rendering it into Arabic, despite my weakness and shortcomings in this blessed language, its literature and its styles. That is on the one hand. On the other hand, a shiver would seize me at the magnitude of the responsibility that weighed on my shoulders whenever I pictured myself acting as interpreter between the Shaykh, may God preserve him, and Arab readers on so important a subject as politics. Nevertheless — through God's grace and the prayers of His Eminence the Shaykh, may God preserve him — I rolled up my sleeves and applied myself in earnest until the work of translation and revision was completed [p. 14] in about three months. What we present today to Arab readers comes with the hope that they will attribute to the translator — not to the Shaykh, may God preserve him — any error they find in it.

It should be noted that the book is divided into two parts. The first of them was devoted by His Eminence the Shaykh to introducing political science, its various schools, and the many systems of government arising from and based upon it. (He did not, however, withhold his critique and comments on them from an Islamic point of view wherever that was needed.) Naturally, he drew on a great many Arabic and foreign sources in preparing it, but he did not see much use in giving references to them or listing their titles: first, because of the difficulty of doing so, and second, because in that part he dealt only with the established, accepted questions of political science.

The second part contains an exposition of the rulings and teachings of Islam in the political sphere, accompanied by the opinions of the jurists (fuqahāʾ) and theologians (mutakallimūn) and their differing points of view. In it he made a point of citing the texts of the Qur'an and Sunna and the other sources on which he relied in setting out the positions of the jurists and theologians and their proofs.

Hence the book contains between its covers an introduction to the various political theories and prevailing systems of government on the one hand, and the premises and principles of Islam in the political sphere on the other — which will help, God willing, to clarify the basic features of Islamic government that are consistent with the demands of the present age and in harmony with its circumstances and needs.

Finally, I ask God the Exalted to accept this effort and to make it beneficial to the Umma, so that it may have the good fortune of contributing to guiding and correcting political efforts and to attaining the goal of establishing Islamic government in the full sense of the word. Amen.

**Kalimullah ibn Dāwūd Khān**
(One of those whom God has blessed with being a student of His Eminence Shaykh ʿAllāma Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani, may God preserve and protect him)
Karachi – Pakistan
11 Muḥarram al-Ḥarām 1435 AH / 16 November 2013 CE

## Foreword by His Eminence Shaykh Mufti Muhammad Rafi Usmani, may God preserve and protect him

[p. 15] We praise Him and send blessings upon His noble Messenger. To proceed:

It pleases and delights us — we, the teachers of Jāmiʿa Dār al-ʿUlūm Karachi and those affiliated with it — and it fills us with hope and expectation, that it has begun to take steps in the study of the burning issues that pose astonishing new challenges in most areas and fields of life for Muslims in general, and for the scholars of the Sharia among them in particular.

It is well known that with the passing of the fifteenth century CE, the Muslims were deprived of the land of al-Andalus — the lost paradise of Islam — which they had ruled for nearly a thousand years with all the splendour and power; and not only that, but they had attended to the sciences there with such care that it made them the leaders and guides of humanity in them. One never ceases to marvel that the West did not feel the stirrings of progress until the day the political star of the Muslims began to set. For the Muslims slid towards backwardness throughout the world as a result of the catastrophe of the fall of al-Andalus, while it gave Europe the opportunity to advance in the field of progress and development after it had been floundering in the darkness of ignorance and the black night of backwardness.

About a century after the catastrophe of the fall of al-Andalus, the countries of the West achieved great material development: on the one hand they managed to discover America, and on the other Vasco da Gama found a sea route to India. Although these were two simple events at first sight, they left a marked effect on changing the course of history and geography, and played a tangible role in bringing about a revolution in human society.

Thus began Europe's journey towards industrial development, and it made rapid strides in the field of the natural sciences and modern technology, drawing a great deal in this on the efforts of the scholars of al-Andalus, gleaning from the tables of their learning and harvesting the fruits of their labours. Through the products of their thought and the fabric of their efforts it attained what we Muslims did not. It seized [p. 16] the opportunity and devoted all its powers to exploiting it, which resulted in revolutions and clear, evident changes in thought, economics and politics at a pace that exceeded every measure. Among these were the countless modern issues that the Industrial Revolution in England gave birth to, in addition to the far-reaching changes produced by the French Revolution. These two revolutions changed the course of human life throughout the world.

It is our duty as Muslims — and especially as people devoted to the Sharia sciences — in the age in which we live, to offer solutions to the problems of the age and to its demands that arose from these two revolutions, and to confront the blatant challenges that face us in the light of current developments; all of this in the light of the lofty Islamic values and ideals, alongside the pure creeds of Islam and its sublime jurisprudence.

In short, many fields have opened up which look to the scholars of the Sharia to devote their efforts, exhaust their energies and apply their qualifications to them. The tragedy is that they have opened at a time when the Muslims are passing through the worst and lowest state in their history — enough to make the weeping weep — and they are, God forbid, on the verge of collapse and at the brink of ruin. Before now, Islam never faced a challenge without having among its sons those capable of resisting it. The philosophy of the Greeks did not penetrate the lands of the Muslims except when there were among them people able to resist it with argument and proof, such as the Greatest Imam Abū Ḥanīfa (may God have mercy on him) and other mujtahid imams. No sooner did the domain of the Islamic governments expand, their conquests widen and their demands and requirements consequently increase, than Islam carried within it those who undertook to resolve them and meet their needs — such as al-Māturīdī, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī and al-Ghazālī (may God have mercy on them) and other Islamic thinkers who bore the marks of asceticism and the adornment of piety.

Today, however, the revolution is more widespread and more comprehensive, its issues more complex and its problems harder to solve; and the pressure has grown greater since the scholars among the Muslims now lack, first, the necessary scholarly qualifications and physical energies, and second, sincerity and piety. [p. 17] All of this has in turn contributed to making their situation more aggravated and their position more dangerous. Yet since God has guaranteed this religion permanence and survival until the Day of Resurrection, in every age He raises up from among the Muslims those who undertake to provide Islamic solutions to every question and problem that humanity encounters or suffers.

We can limit the most important issues facing Islam, on which it requires its sons to concentrate, to five fields:

**(1) Thought and philosophy.** Just as, when Greek philosophy was translated into Arabic and reached the lands of the Muslims with its predominant materialist tendencies and heretical spirit, the Muslim scholars confronted it with all competence and merit — first by learning it and excelling in its method of argument, and second by using that very method to refute its proofs and expose the falsity of its arguments — which may be regarded as an immortal achievement and a bright blaze on the brow of Islamic history;

so today, Western materialist philosophy and the intellectual misguidance that follows it pose a blatant challenge and an overwhelming assault on the beliefs and principles of the Islamic Umma. It has thus become the responsibility of its scholars to resist it by answering the objections it has raised and the doubts it has stirred up, following the same method their predecessors followed: first by mastering that philosophy, and second by using its own style as a weapon to refute and silence it.

**(2) Economics.** The English revolution brought about astonishing development in it, and it came to be dominated by two schools of thought and the two economic systems born of them, which the world has known and experienced under the names capitalism and its opposite, socialism. What concerns us Muslims in this respect is that we explain to the world the economic system of Islam, so that it may arbitrate between those two prevailing systems — especially since the issue of economics has taken first place among the burning, important issues of the world, and has raised countless questions from the point of view of the Sharia, and specifically from the perspective of Islamic jurisprudence. Praise be to God, who has enabled some Muslims to begin to fill [p. 18] the tangible gap in these two fields; work has also begun in other fields, but the need for still more attention remains pressing and urgent.

**(3) Politics.** The world has witnessed a number of modern political theories and the various kinds of systems and methods of government that followed from them. It thus falls on the shoulders of the scholars of Islam to study them and then to clarify the clear, decisive position of Islam on them: distinguishing what is in harmony with the principles of Islam — which may properly be called Islamic politics — from what contradicts and clashes with them, which we consider heretical and irreligious; in addition to revealing the merits of Islamic politics and the dividing line that separates it from what prevails in the world today under the name of the democratic system, monarchy, and others.

**(4) Medicine.** Modern inventions have brought about tremendous development in it, which makes it all the more necessary for Muslim scholars to attend to studying the mass of jurisprudential questions accumulating in it, so that they can respond to the challenge this progress and development poses to them, and to what is actually put before them from the east and west of the earth in the way of questions about the permissibility or prohibition of things, and about the position of the Sharia on methods of treatment and the ways of medication current in the world. As we mentioned earlier, many scholars and muftis around the world have turned their attention to this field as well, but it still needs more effort and care from them to meet its needs and fulfil its requirements.

**(5) Law and legislation.** This is a field in which the Muslims were the foremost champions and the winners of the race, for the world had never known a codified, systematic law like that which distinguished Islamic jurisprudence and which enjoyed codification, foundation-laying and derivation of a kind the world had not witnessed before. But in later ages Europe gave it attention and so advanced in it over the rest of humanity, Muslims included, becoming its pioneers and leaders. Although a group of scholars has had the good fortune to serve it, it, like the preceding fields, still needs those who will delve into it with distinction and skill.

At any rate, these are five areas and fields that face the Muslims. In each [p. 19] of them there are thorny issues and complex questions. Their due can be fulfilled, and they can then be harnessed and the rulings of Islam applied in them, only if a sufficient number and a great many of the sons of the Umma devote their lives to specializing and becoming expert in them — some in one, others in another, and so on.

In keeping with the principle "What cannot be attained in full should not be abandoned in full", the Dār al-ʿUlūm Karachi family held a training course on economics and its issues from an Islamic perspective. A considerable number of scholars took part in it with eagerness and energy, and showed how greatly they valued its importance and great usefulness. It produced the book *Business and the Contemporary Economy in the Light of Islam*, which can be taken as a basis for large-scale projects in this field. Dār al-ʿUlūm Karachi has had the good fortune of playing its full part and prominent role in performing the communal obligation (wājib kifāʾī) that weighed on the shoulders of the Muslims of Pakistan, represented by its Vice-President, His Eminence Shaykh Mufti Muhammad Taqi Usmani — may God grant him well-being and bless his life — who has delved deeply into the study of economic questions and become thoroughly versed in them, and then compared them with the rulings and teachings of Islam, after simplifying them and casting them in a modern form so that they have become easily accessible to all who seek them among ordinary people.

Today we are embarking on a course on politics, hoping, by God's grace and generosity, that He will grant it acceptance and make it beneficial to the Muslims, as with its sister course held on economics, and that He will raise up from among the participating scholars someone who will devote his life to one of those five areas, giving it his attention and dedicating his care to it.

You have come here from the remotest and nearest parts of the country, covering long and arduous journeys, and I therefore pray for you from the depths of my heart, as I find a hadith ringing in my ears and running through my mind again and again — the saying of the Prophet ﷺ: "Whoever follows a path seeking knowledge in it, God makes easy for him a path to Paradise."¹

## Introducing the Subject

[p. 20] Praise be to God, Lord of the worlds, and blessings and peace upon our master and patron Muhammad, the trustworthy Prophet, and upon all his family and companions, and upon all who follow them in excellence until the Day of Judgement. To proceed:

Honoured scholars of the Sharia: as His Eminence the President of the University, may God preserve him, has explained to you, the modern age has directed at the Muslims diverse challenges in many fields and from various directions. He, may God preserve him, excelled in analysing them and limiting them to five fields, which call for exceptional attention from Muslim scholars and require mighty efforts from them. I personally believe that the fields of economics and politics are the most important and most dangerous of them, for they have left lasting effects on human life in this age of ours. In addition, they have very often given rise to questions and stirred up doubts among people: if we accept that the prevailing systems are impermissible according to the Sharia, because their foundations and principles clash with the foundations and principles of Islam, then what way is there to replace those foundations with these principles, and to run the helm of these rulings in accordance with them?

How often we are asked, and others are asked: if we purified our economic system of usury (ribā) on the grounds that God has forbidden it, and the scholars also consider bank interest to be part of forbidden usury, is there a Sharia alternative to it that could be adopted to run the economic system in this age? If we dissociated ourselves from modern political theories and abandoned the systems of government generated by them, on the grounds that they oppose Islam and clash with the Sharia, how could the helm of government be run according to the rulings of Islam in the present age and the demands of its political circles?! For various religious circles never cease to claim that Islam is free of monarchy and does not permit individual governments or dictatorships either, and that democracy too does not fully accord with its rulings — or so, at least, a serious and circumspect section of them maintains. So the question that arises in the mind of the child of the twentieth century is: what is the Islamic system of government? How can it be applied and implemented in the modern age while taking account of its demands, needs, circumstances and requirements?!

[p. 21] Since the fields of economics and politics are alike in facing this challenge and confronting this question in the first place, and because of their eminent position among those fields in the second place, we have devoted this course to the study of political issues, after the first one was devoted to economics and modern business. In it we aim, first, to review the various political theories and to pause over them; and second, to explain a plan and a picture of the actual application and real implementation of the rulings and principles of Islam. Although the period allotted to this course is ten days only — which is very short relative to the vastness and ramifications of the subject of politics, to the extent that we can say that political science has today assumed the character of an independent science that enjoys a great many works — we will nonetheless try, with God's help, to present to you an introduction to its important topics and a concise, summarized study of it, God willing. God, glorified be He, is the one whose help is sought.

### Politics and the First Works Written on It

Political science is not a modern science invented by Western men. Rather, it is an ancient science the world has known since the dawn of philosophy, since it is one of its branches and divisions. For the philosophers first divided wisdom — or say philosophy — into two basic parts: theoretical wisdom and practical wisdom. Then each of them was divided into three parts: the theoretical into the divine, the mathematical and the natural; and the practical into the refinement of character (ethics), household management (economics), and civil politics.

From this it is clear that politics is one branch of philosophy, which its practitioners have continued to treat in research and reflection alongside its other branches.

As for the first book on politics, I believe it is *The Republic* of the famous Greek philosopher Plato. He arranged it in the light of what he had learned from his teacher Socrates during his apprenticeship to him, merging his own personal opinions into it. This book of his has been translated into English under the title "Republic". It is most likely the first work on politics in the world, since the era of Plato goes back to around 400 years before Christ.

[p. 22] The book is worth reading, since it gives us a clear picture of the kind of writing and style of thinking that prevailed in that age. Although it is a large book, benefiting from it is somewhat difficult, especially in comparison with the style of writing prevalent in our age. For it is not arranged technically, and the topics are not set down in sequence and succession; rather he followed the method of dialogue in it — that is, conversation between two or more persons — and so presented all the topics in the form of a dialogue in which such-and-such a person met so-and-so, asked him about such-and-such, and he answered him thus. Although it can be regarded as the first brick and signpost in the field of politics, many of its discussions are shrouded in obscurity, or the allegorical style used in it makes them ambiguous. The researcher therefore faces a great problem and encounters extreme difficulty in extracting a conclusion from it or in identifying a position on something. At any rate, it takes pride in being the first book in the field of politics, and its author was the first to use the word "republic".

His most prominent student, Aristotle — who perhaps enjoyed more fame than his teacher in philosophical circles — then followed in his footsteps. He recorded the various portions of Plato's philosophy and presented them in the form of a book. To him goes the credit for founding all the fields of the natural sciences: you will hardly find a branch of them without finding him to be its first founder. Take, for example, medicine, zoology, geology, astronomy, optics and so on.

Among the works with which he enriched the human library is a book on politics entitled *Politics* itself, and this book of his is in truth an elevated and developed form of Plato's *Republic*, mentioned above. However, his book *Politics* surpasses Plato's *Republic* in the merits of its arrangement and composition, alongside its simplification of topics and clarification of issues, all of which the former lacks, as mentioned a little earlier.

His book has been printed in translation under the title "Politics", and fate has allowed me to read a large part of it. In the course of reading it, the reader soon gains a keen sense that philosophy, its methods and its ways of expression have undergone great changes and many upheavals, [p. 23] and that the philosophy studied by students in the scholarly circles of the Indian subcontinent, represented by the books written on it such as *al-Maybudhī*, *al-Hidāya al-Saʿīdiyya*, *al-Ṣadrā* and *al-Shams al-Bāzigha*, is not the philosophy of Aristotle. Rather, it is a branch of philosophy that the world knew centuries after Aristotle and is called Neoplatonic philosophy. Among the things that distinguish the two is that Aristotle's philosophy is marked by a focus on practical aspects more than theory, while the Neoplatonic philosophy is entirely its opposite, being dominated in its discussions by the theoretical side more than the practical.

At any rate, his book deserves to be read by people of learning so that they may come to know Aristotle's method and his arrival at forms of knowledge and philosophical theories that the world today calls "the most modern theories", wilfully blind to the clear, established facts, since Aristotle preceded them by thousands of years. What further increases the standing of his book and makes it a source of pride is that the scholars of politics have agreed that it is the main source and original reference for the theories that followed it throughout the history of humanity and over the course of human life.

In sum: politics is one of the ancient sciences that has enjoyed people's attention and scholarly service for a long time, beginning with Socrates and Plato, passing through Aristotle, and those political thinkers who followed in his footsteps. However, the basis of all of them and the centre of their discussions was nothing but pure theories that the toil of their minds and the labour of their thought produced alone. They therefore produced nothing but illusions built on mere speculation stripped of the light of revelation. But after the Muslims plunged into its depths, bearing a flame of faith and holding in their hands a torch of the light of revelation, they transformed its course and made of it an independent science which they called "Sharia governance" (*al-siyāsa al-sharʿiyya*). They devoted their care to it through research, writing and reflection until they reached its summit and attained its peak, so that it became capable of guaranteeing the soundness of the order of humanity and its safety if humanity took it into account and put it into practice.

However, in our course we shall refrain from exhausting its discussions and covering its subject matter, confining ourselves to its most important angles and practical dimensions which, in general, the scholars of the Sharia urgently need to know. Bear in mind that I am not one of the men of politics, nor one of the specialists [p. 24] and experts in its sciences; it is merely the extent of my knowledge from my study and reading of the books of these people. In the light of my survey and analysis of works on politics, I have chosen several headings to treat in this course of ours, devoting myself to explaining them and reaching sound conclusions about them with all my effort, God willing. These headings are as follows:

**(1)** How did the state (*State*) arise in this world? That is: how did it occur to human beings to form a state that divides society into a ruler who commands and a ruled who obeys? Since systems of government and practical political methods are closely connected with people's views on the beginning and rise of the state, it is fitting for people of Sharia knowledge to become acquainted with those theories, so as to be able to know these systems with insight and mastery.

**(2)** The second heading on which I would like to shed light is: an introduction to the various political systems and their philosophies and the ideas at work behind their rise and distinctiveness, such as monarchy, aristocracy, fascism, and democracy with its various branches and divisions.

**(3)** An introduction to systems of government and the kinds of powers. Under each political system the helm of government can be steered in many ways. Which method is preferable for forming the government? How many ministries and departments should it have, and how are powers distributed among them? We would like to present an introduction to the systems of government prevailing in the world today (as well as what prevailed in the past), such as the presidential system and the parliamentary system, for example, with their merits and the differences between them.

**(3)** [sic] In the course of introducing the systems of government, we shall move on to explaining the original sources of legislation and the basic sources of law-making. We shall likewise pass through the history of these systems from their origin to their actual implementation in the external world, and we shall cast a passing glance at the history of the two revolutions — the French and the Industrial in England — owing to their prominent, tangible role in the upheavals of the political world and in changing the course of history.

**(4)** Finally, we shall pause over the teachings of Islam and its rulings in the field of politics, and examine possible formulas and forms that could be adopted to apply and implement those rulings in [p. 25] this age of ours, while taking account of its needs and demands.

This, then, is a quick survey of the topics we shall treat through research and study in this course of ours, God willing.
